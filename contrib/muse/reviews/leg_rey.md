# Blind leg, Rey (outside eye). Issue #486, cinematic audit.

Written from the look bench captures alone (docs/shots/look_bench/: m1, m2, m3, m4, m5, f1, and deaths/capital_death_beats.jpg and deaths/cruiser_death_beats.jpg), before reading the playbooks, the audio files, the code or any other leg. I cannot hear the game and I have not seen it move; everything below is what a viewer sees in stills, plus what the stills imply about motion. Ranked by how much each one makes a battle feel staged rather than violent.

## B1. Every weapon hits the hull in exactly the same way

In m1, ten different weapons land on the same hull, and nine of them produce the same two-part stamp: one small round white glow at the impact point and one soft beige smoke puff beside it. At the battle camera (170 column) a macro cannon, a las, an autocannon, a scatter battery and a plasma hit are interchangeable. Only the lance (a beam) and the torpedo (a bigger puff) read differently. A film separates its weapons by what the hit DOES: a kinetic round sparks and skips, an energy hit splashes and runs, a torpedo bloom is dirty and directional. Here the hit is a pictogram, identical every time, always at the same spot on the hull, always the same size. The viewer learns within one battle that impacts are decorative.

## B2. The smoke is a cotton ball, and it is the only material in the game

Every explosion, hit and death in these captures is made of the same soft, round, beige-grey puff, scaled up or down. The torpedo's puff is the las puff enlarged. Nothing is ever thrown: no fragments, no sparks with trajectories, no venting jet, no debris that keeps moving, nothing left behind after the puff fades. Real destruction has at least two materials (a bright fast one and a slow dirty one) moving at different speeds in different directions. One soft ball, however big, reads as a stage effect, a puff of talc.

## B3. The shield is a perfect honeycomb egg, and hits do not touch it

In m2 and m3 the shield is a flawless ellipsoid wrapped in a regular honeycomb grid, identical in every row, every faction, every state. A hit does not dent it, ripple it or light the cells around the impact: the same beige puff floats at the bubble's edge, visually disconnected from it. Worse, a shield at one fifth strength is pixel-identical to a full shield; the bench caption itself admits the look does not exist yet. And the bubble bleaches the ship inside it white, so a shielded ship reads as a ghost of itself. In film (the Star Trek and Stargate shield hits, the Sins of a Solar Empire 2 local flares) the shield answers AT the point of impact and its condition is written on it. A perfect untouchable egg is the single most "game" object in these pictures.

## B4. A shield breaking does not read as breaking

The m3 collapse row shows a bright flash at one edge and a loose ring, with the ellipsoid still drawn around the ship. The biggest state change a shield can make arrives as a flash and a halo, not as a surface failing, peeling or going dark in patches. Compare B3: the shield never shows damage, so its death has nothing to build on.

## B5. One scale for everything: the frigate and the tomb-ark take the same hit

m5 is the audit in one picture. The same battleship macro battery hits an Eclipse frigate, destroyer, cruiser and tomb-ark, and the impact puff and glow are the same size on all four, while the incoming tracer is a glowing comet larger than the frigate itself. Nothing about the hit scales with the shooter or the target. Film grammar is built on scale contrast: a capital's main battery hit should dwarf a fighter's cannon hit, and a hit on a capital should look absorbed by its mass, not identical to a hit that would gut a frigate.

## B6. The chain blasts are identical, evenly spaced, and the core flash is a cauliflower

In the death beat strips, the capital's blast 4 (1.58 s) and blast 5 (1.93 s) are the same white sparkle band laid across the hull at the same density; the cruiser's blasts 1 to 3 are the same small fire at three evenly marched positions. Then the core flash (cruiser 1.67 s, capital 2.30 s) is a perfectly round orange ball with a scalloped edge, centred on the ship, owing nothing to the ship's shape or to where it was hit. A real sequence of internal explosions staggers: uneven gaps, different sizes, some deep and muffled, one bigger than the pattern. Even spacing and equal size is the signature of a schedule, not of a ship coming apart. (The wreck pieces themselves are issue #484's ground and are not listed here.)

## B7. Small deaths are a puff and a ring; one faction's death is nearly invisible

In m4, the frigate and destroyer die as the standard puff plus a translucent lavender ring, a perfect circle expanding on cue, with three small orange flame ticks on top. The Sunward cruiser's death puff is barely larger than the frigate's. The Eclipse burn-out row is the extreme case: three frames of a dark hulk, almost no visible event at all, a capital-scale death a viewer could miss while looking at it. Deaths are the moments a battle is remembered by; here the smallest and the largest are separated by puff diameter alone.

## B8. Beams are ruler-straight, uniform and hard-edged; one shot is a hairline across the screen

The lance and ion beam in m1 and m2 are perfect white bars: constant width, constant brightness, sharp edges, no taper, no flicker, no brighter head or fading tail. The gauss hit in m3 draws a thin line across the ENTIRE frame, edge to edge, like a scratch on the picture rather than something in the world. A filmed beam has a source that strains, a path that heats the space it crosses and an end that splashes. A bar of white is a diagram of a beam.

## B9. Nothing lights its neighbours, and space is always clean afterwards

Across every capture, no explosion throws light on a nearby hull, no fire reflects off armour, no scorch or residue persists, no debris field hangs where a ship died. Each event happens in its own clean bubble of space and leaves the scene exactly as it was. That tidiness, the world resetting after every act of violence, is a large part of why the battle feels staged.

## B10. The scene dim does not read

The capital beats strip prints the death twice, scene dim OFF and ON, and the two versions are hard to tell apart even side by side in a bench whose purpose is to show the difference. If a whole lighting treatment is invisible in its own proof sheet, it is not contributing drama in a real battle either.

## B11. At the battle camera the fleet sits still, centred and silent-looking

f1 shows every hull at true battle size: most are small dark silhouettes with one identical white engine smudge at the stern, the small craft are two to four pixel specks, and nothing in any capture suggests drift, recoil, roll, exhaust variation or a ship under way. I have only stills, so this is a prediction for the capture leg to test: if ships translate without any secondary motion (no settle, no thruster correction, no exhaust change between moving and stopped), the movement will read as sliding counters, the board-game tell. The R188 movement section of my chaired report takes this up from references.

## What I could not judge blind

Sound (nothing to hear; the chaired report audits it from the playbook and cue files). Timing and rhythm inside a turn (stills only). Whether salvos fire on one tick (the capture leg measures this). Where any of these looks are mandated (the playbook read comes next, in the chaired report).
