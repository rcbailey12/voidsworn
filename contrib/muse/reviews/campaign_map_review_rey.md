# Campaign screen information audit, Rey leg, 2026-10-26

Chair header: Rey's own audit for the campaign screen information review (the information half of 26c Job 4), written blind alongside the DeepSeek, Qwen and Argon legs against the same evidence pack. Filed as drafted; no chair corrections were needed. The synthesis is contrib/muse/reviews/campaign_map_review.md.

Rey (chair), Oct 26, 2026. Written blind: this audit was produced from the evidence pack (evidence_pack_campaign.md) and the fetched code beside it, plus public sources on the comparison games. The other legs for this job were not consulted; they run separately. Visual beauty, layout feel and clutter as seen are out of scope throughout: no screenshots exist, and every judgement below is about what the screens say, where, and when.

Pack claims were spot-checked against the code before writing: the Nemesis panel (campaign_ui.gd lines 516-540), the danger function and route scoring (map_intel.gd lines 11, 29-58, 95-134), the hover card's danger and route blocks (map3d.gd lines 570-606), the Nemesis node marking (map3d.gd lines 492-499), and a grep across all seven fetched files for any reader of his behaviour text, persona, traits, fed or riches (none exists). Where the audit adds something the pack did not say, it is marked OBSERVED (read in the code during the check) or INFERRED (judgement).

## 1. Route planning

**Verdict: PARTIAL.** The 3D map carries a real route planner, stronger than what at least one comparison game offers before commitment. The flat map carries none of it, and the planner answers only the two questions its fixed scoring chooses to ask.

What is there, and it is a lot (pack section 1): every node's type, encounter name, board, modifier, reward and danger band are visible before the jump, and the jump is the commitment (one jump per choice, no undo in any fetched file). Hovering any node lights the safest and richest routes through it to the flagship, colours the lanes, puts running-total chips (fights so far, repair stops so far) on each node of both routes, and prints both routes' full totals in the hover card: fights with elite count, repair stops, shipyards, boons, refits, relics, and the worst fight's band in its band colour. The reachable fights are additionally previewed in deduplicated panels under the map. OBSERVED in the card code (map3d.gd, route block): when the fleet counts as hurt, a route with no repair stop before the flagship gets an explicit warning line in the card, which is exactly the kind of conditional, decision-shaped information a planner should produce.

Three limits:

1. **The planner's two lenses are fixed and their weights are invisible.** OBSERVED (map_intel.gd lines 95-127): "richest" is a hard-coded value score (boons x3, relics x4, refits x2, credits x1, shipyards x2, events x1), and "safest" minimises the worst band, then a danger sum discounted by repair stops. The card prints the totals but never the score or the weights, so a player whose priority is neither (avoid elites, maximise credits, rush the boss) has no third lens and cannot tell why one route outranks another when the totals look close. INFERRED: the totals mostly let a player re-derive the ranking by eye, which softens this, but the ranking rule itself is a black box of exactly the kind section 3 warns about.
2. **The boss is excluded from every route total** (map_intel.gd line 100 and the card code): correct in that it ends every route equally, but a route whose earlier fights leave the fleet weaker arrives at a harder boss in practice, and no number anywhere prices that. The hurt-fleet warning partially covers it.
3. **All of it is 3D only.** The flat map (map_view.gd) has no bands, no routes, no card (pack section 1). A player who turns the 3D map off plans with encounter names and descriptions alone.

Against the comparison games: Rogue Trader's chart shows every discovered route with a four-step colour danger rating (green safe, yellow unsafe, orange dangerous, red deadly) and lets the player spend Navigator's Insight to lower a route's rating permanently, so route risk there is both displayed and negotiable (https://www.gamerguides.com/warhammer-40k-rogue-trader/guide/koronus-expanse-exploration/systems). Voidsworn's per-node bands plus per-route worst-fight is richer than a per-route colour, but its routes are take-it-or-leave-it: there is no equivalent of buying a dangerous leg down. BattleTech is the comparison Voidsworn beats: in BattleTech the contract board belongs to the system you are in, contracts are generated per system, and players complain they cannot know what work waits elsewhere before paying to travel (from memory of the game's contract flow; the per-system generation complaint is documented at https://steamcommunity.com/app/637090/discussions/0/571552638979547484). Voidsworn shows the whole sector's work, pay and difficulty up front, which is the stronger planning surface. Sins 2 plans across time instead: its Planner tool lets the player scrub up to an hour into the future to see where planets and phase lanes will be, because its map moves (https://store.steampowered.com/news/app/1575940/view/4209253428808433640?l=hungarian). Voidsworn's sector is fixed once generated, so it does not need a time tool; the honest gap is not foresight, it is that its foresight only comes in two flavours.

## 2. Information at a glance

**Verdict: PARTIAL, leaning PASS on the 3D map page.** The side panel is a genuine build summary, not a token HUD. The misses are a small set of specific facts: sector progress as a number, the sector condition's effect as text, and the run's Heat rules.

What earned always-on space (pack section 2) is the right core set: credits and command points as large readouts; admiral and doctrine; the full fleet list per ship with hull as numbers and a segmented bar, readiness words with plain thresholds (Ready at 70% or better, Damaged at 40%, Crippled below), captain rank, refit slots and promotion flags; a fleet-condition warning that does not just say the fleet is hurt but answers the planning question (average hull, and either "plan a repair stop" with the fact that one still exists on some route, or that none is left); the faction verb with its cost or its used state; the flagship aspect with its text; every boon and relic with full text; refits in the hold; patrons. The intel strip adds the sector's cast (hostile presence, rival raiders, sector condition, patrons) and the Nemesis box. Sins 2's equivalent strength is the Fleet Supply window, which puts the status and upgrades of every capital ship, titan and starbase in the empire in one place for exactly this reason (https://store.steampowered.com/news/app/1575940/view/4209253428808433640?l=hungarian); Voidsworn's side panel matches that philosophy and adds the build (boons, relics, aspect) on top, which Sins keeps in separate windows.

The gaps, in order of cost:

1. **No numeric sector progress anywhere on the map page** (OBSERVED absence, pack section 2). Progress is spatial only: darkened nodes, gilt lanes, the token. A player deciding whether they can afford a detour has to count nodes by eye. Every comparison layer gives progress a number somewhere (contracts done and days elapsed in BattleTech, from memory; colonies and route coverage in Rogue Trader). This is a cheap fact the game already knows.
2. **The sector condition's effect is tooltip-only.** The strip box prints the condition's name; its description, which is the part that changes decisions, appears only in the box's tooltip (campaign_ui.gd lines 424-425, per pack section 5). The same pattern holds for the faction blurb, the rival-raiders explanation and the patrons sigil rule. Tooltip-only rules text is the kind of thing players reliably never read once, let alone re-check mid-sector.
3. **Heat rules are not repeated on the map page.** They print on the aspect screen (campaign_ui.gd lines 354-358); no fetched map-page code repeats them, and Heat flags feed the danger estimate (map_intel.gd reads h_hunted, h_elite), so a rule that raises every band can be active with no on-page reminder of why. UNSURE whether another surface repeats them mid-run.
4. The strip's rival-raiders box compresses a real mechanic ("roughly one ordinary fight in five is a rival's") into a tooltip; the on-map signal (faction-colour ring, patrols) is good, but the rate is the decision-relevant part and it is hover-only.

None of these is a layering philosophy problem. The panel exists, is always on the page, and already carries denser material than these four facts. They are omissions from a strong surface, not a weak surface.

## 3. Danger readability

**Verdict: PARTIAL.** The estimate's construction is the most honest in the comparison set, and its grain is coarser. The flat map has no estimate at all.

The scheme (pack section 3; verified in map_intel.gd lines 5-11, 29-58): threat is computed from the same budget numbers the game builds fleets with (column budget, ramp growth, elite and boss factors, node modifiers, Heat flags) divided by the player's current fleet power, which itself prices hull damage, Marks, refits, captain rank, hangars, boons and relics. Four bands: Easy under 0.8, Even under 1.05, Hard under 1.4, Deadly above. The file header states plainly that the fleet is rolled at the fight, so the band is expected strength, not the exact one. The band shows as a coloured chip in capitals under every unpassed fight node, and the hover card adds the ratio in words ("their strength about N% of your fleet's"). The estimate also follows the player's AI difficulty setting (Standard budget factor), so the number describes the game the player is actually playing.

Why the percentage matters: BattleTech is the cautionary tale. Its contract skulls are widely read by players as something they are not; the documented community position is that skulls track the objective group's drop tonnage, that reinforcements are not counted into them, and that experienced players judge difficulty by the payout and the text intel instead (https://steamcommunity.com/app/637090/discussions/0/1696045708662787083/), with a long-running complaint that the ratings are a black box with no way to check the employer's honesty (https://steamcommunity.com/app/637090/discussions/0/1745605598715516841/?l=brazilian&ctp=2). Voidsworn avoids the worst of that in two ways: its estimate is derived from the actual build numbers rather than assigned, and the card states the ratio in words, which gives the player a checkable claim ("about 130% of my fleet") instead of an opaque icon. Rogue Trader sits at the other end: its four route colours rate the road, not the fight, and a red route means likely warp encounters rather than a stronger enemy (https://www.gamerguides.com/warhammer-40k-rogue-trader/guide/koronus-expanse-exploration/systems). Four bands as a grain is defensible (it matches Rogue Trader's four), and against BattleTech's ten skull steps the finer grain demonstrably did not buy trust.

Remaining problems:

1. **Thresholds are untested as far as the pack shows** (UNSURE whether tuned against play). The band edges (0.8, 1.05, 1.4) are asymmetric around Even, which is presumably deliberate, but nothing in the pack shows calibration evidence. A band system lives on calibration: if "Even" fights are routinely lost, the chips become decoration, the BattleTech failure mode by another road.
2. **The chips move as your fleet changes,** because fleet power is current. INFERRED: this is correct behaviour (danger is relative), but a node that flips from Hard to Even after a repair stop, with no visible cause on the node itself, can read as the map changing its mind. The card's percentage is the antidote and it is hover-only.
3. **Special-fight marking is strong; credit where due.** Elites get a brighter red diamond plus a gold crown ring plus animated hazard chevrons on the lanes in; the boss is the enemy flagship's own hull at scale inside a crown ring with the label "Sector flagship"; rival fights get a faction-colour ring, a card flag, and patrol ships drifting around the node (pack section 3). A player can tell at a glance which fights are special, and the "why" is carried by the card for elites (elite flag in previews and counts) and by shape for the boss. This is the area closest to PASS.
4. **The flat map shows no danger information at all** (OBSERVED, pack sections 1 and 3). Whatever the reason a player ends up on the flat map, the game's core difficulty signal simply is not there. map_intel computes bands independently of which map draws them, so this is a drawing omission, not a data gap.

## 4. The Nemesis as information

**Verdict: PARTIAL.** This section settles Job 2's open question 7 ("What does the Godot Nemesis card actually show, and when?"), and the answer is better than Job 2 feared and narrower than the system needs. Identity, schedule and schedule pressure: PASS. Behaviour, growth counters and the content of his counters: NOT PRESENT.

What is surfaced (pack section 4, verified in campaign_ui.gd lines 516-540 and map3d.gd lines 492-499 and 570-576): full name with title and epithet, faction, a framed portrait with one scar overlay per scar, scar count as text, the clock as dots in two places (side card and intel strip), a strike warning in words ("His fleet is coming: your next fight is his."), the standing clock explanation as standing text on the card, his converted node marked before committing (NEMESIS chip plus portrait on the 3D map, the word "Nemesis" in red on the flat map), weaknesses listed by name when revealed and as "unknown" when not, adaptation names when he has any, a danger band for his fight that prices his scars (10% each) and adaptation count (6% each), and a clean dead state ("<nemFull> is dead.", strip box gone).

The single surfaced item that does the most work is the combination of the node marking and the strike tail. Job 2's central complaint was that the clock was invisible state; on these screens it is not. The player can see the clock filling, sees the exact sentence when it fills, and sees which node becomes his before clicking it. That converts the Nemesis from an ambush timer into a planning input, which is the difference between a rival and a random event.

What is missing, ranked by what it costs the player:

1. **His faction behaviour text. This is the costliest absence in the whole audit.** No fetched campaign file reads his behaviour block (grep-verified). The player is never told on the campaign screens what his strike does: that a Corsair fills his clock and takes 25% of credits or cuts a crippled ship, that a Deathless returns once after death, that a Chorus grows from the player's losses anywhere, that a Hegemony fight brings round 3 reinforcements, or what the Voidsworn challenge and Ithar withdrawal mean at the fight. The clock's urgency is therefore flat across factions: the same dots mean "lose credits or a ship" against one rival and "a duel bonus" against another, and the screens give the player no way to price the difference. Job 2 found all six verbs are real mechanics; the campaign layer keeps every one of them secret. The behaviour strings exist as data (Job 2 read them in the data block); nothing in this layer prints them. UNSURE whether a battle or pre-fight screen states them at the fight itself; even if one does, the planning layer is where the clock is watched, and it is silent there.
2. **fed and riches, the two growth counters.** Neither has a reader in any fetched file (pack section 4, grep-verified). Chorus fed makes his fleet stronger from losses anywhere in the run; the danger function does not even take fed as an input (map_intel.gd line 54 prices scars and adaptations only; verified), so a fed Chorus's band rises only if the feeding entered through fleet building elsewhere (UNSURE). Riches at least becomes visible as extra ships at the fight. Fed is invisible at every stage: no counter, no line, and possibly no band movement the player can attribute.
3. **Adaptation effects and reasons on the card.** The card prints generic adaptation names only: no effect text, no "why" string, and not the faction-flavoured names. The one full explanation ("Intelligence: <nemFull> has adapted to you: <name> (<why>). <effect>") lives in the sector-change modal, shown once, covering only the adaptation recorded for that sector change (campaign_ui.gd lines 897-899, verified). Job 2 noted Chorus adapts after every meeting; whether any announcement exists for that is UNSURE from this pack, and no fetched file provides a second home for the modal's sentence. A player who clicks through the modal, or meets a mid-sector adaptation, is left with a red name on a card and no statement of what it does.
4. **Weakness effect text.** Revealed weaknesses print as names only; the description field is never printed on the card (pack section 4). The comparison is unflattering: in Shadow of War, revealing a captain through a Worm shows "their name, level, strengths, and weaknesses", with the weaknesses stated as exploitable effects, immunities to headshots, finishing strikes, fire and so on (https://www.thegamer.com/middle-earth-shadow-of-war-orc-nemesis-system-complete-guide/). A name like the data's weakness names tells a returning player plenty and a first-run player almost nothing; the effect text is the part that changes a loadout. Also OBSERVED: nothing in the fetched files announces a reveal when it happens, and how a weakness becomes revealed at all is decided in campaign.gd, outside this fetch (UNSURE). Mordor also ties intel to play (you hunt a Worm to earn it); how Voidsworn earns reveals cannot be judged from this pack.
5. **Persona and traits.** Never surfaced on the campaign screens. Persona drives his targeting (Job 2), so a player cannot anticipate whether he hunts crippled ships or the flagship; traits are smaller, but both are identity the generator spent effort creating and the card declines to show. The card has room: it already carries a portrait, a clock and two lists.

Net judgement for the area: the card is a good schedule board and a thin dossier. For a system whose design brief (R42) is a rival who feels different every run and behaves differently by faction, the campaign screens show the "different every run" half (name, face, scars, weaknesses, adaptations) and hide the "behaves differently by faction" half entirely.

## 5. Information layering and duplication risks at code level

**Verdict: PARTIAL.** The layering has a sound skeleton (always-on state, hover depth, modal announcements) and three structural leaks: rules text that exists only in tooltips, an announcement that exists only in a modal, and a second map that drops the top layer entirely.

The always-on set (pack section 5): header, an intel strip of up to five boxes, the chart, a node legend, preview panels for every distinct reachable fight, a hint line, and the side panel. As a set this is defensible and arguably generous; the side panel's internal order puts money, admiral, the Nemesis card and the faction verb above the fleet list and the build, which matches decision frequency. The panel scrolls, so boons, relics and the aspect sit below the fold in practice; that is acceptable for reference material, less so for the flagship aspect, which shapes every fight and sits near the bottom (campaign_ui.gd _side order, lines 462-514).

Hover is carrying the right things (deep per-node detail, the route comparison, the condition and blurb explanations) with one imbalance: several facts exist ONLY in tooltips (pack section 5: faction blurb, rival explanation, sector condition description, patrons sigil rule, captain perks on the fleet screen). Tooltips are the weakest channel a fact can live in: undiscoverable, unsearchable, and gone the moment the pointer moves. When a tooltip carries a rule (the condition's effect, the sigil rule that explains why boon nodes wear discs), the design is betting the player hovers the right small box at the right time. Section 2's gaps are all instances of this one pattern.

Modals are the second leak. One-time announcements (sector changes, the adaptation intelligence line, events, rewards) have mostly no home screen afterwards. The adaptation line is the documented case: one showing, then the information content (the why, the effect) exists nowhere, while its name persists on the card as a reminder of a sentence the player can no longer read. Any fact important enough to interrupt the player in a modal is important enough to keep a copy somewhere quiet.

Duplication, judged as design rather than looks: the Nemesis clock appears three times at once (strip dots, card dots, strike tail). That duplication helps, because the three copies sit at three attention levels and cannot contradict (all read the same RUN.nem state). Reachable fights are described twice, on-node and in the preview panels; the panels earn their place by deduplicating identical encounters and carrying the full descriptions the nodes abbreviate. Danger appears as chips, card text and route worst-fight text, all computed from the same map_intel.gd functions (single source, verified), so contradiction risk is low. The one duplication that does risk contradiction is subtler: the richest route's ranking uses weights the player cannot see (section 1), so the map can visibly prefer a route whose printed totals look worse than another's, and the player has no way to reconcile the two channels. That is a contradiction between the ranking channel and the totals channel, produced by hiding the scoring rule.

The structural note that frames the rest (pack section 5): the campaign UI renders exactly what the campaign's page data offers, so each absence in this audit is either the UI not drawing held state or the campaign not offering it; the behaviour/persona/traits/fed absences are grep-confirmed at the UI level, and which side of that line each falls on is UNSURE where the pack has not said. The fallback UI is a build-safety stand-in, not a player surface, and is excluded from these judgements, correctly per the pack.

What must wait for screenshots: whether the per-node label stack (name, NEMESIS chip, band chip, shape/modifier sub-line, reward, board name, route chips) stays legible in a dense late sector, whether the hover card occludes the nodes it describes, and whether the side panel's density reads as richness or noise. The information inventory says the material is there and layered sanely; it cannot say the page holds together when all of it fires at once.

## Ranked fixes and improvements

Ordered by decision value per unit of build effort. All data named below already exists in the run state or the data tables per the pack and Job 2; none of these needs new mechanics.

1. Print his behaviour line on the Nemesis card. One label under the faction line, reading the faction behaviour text the data block already carries (Job 2 records the strings). Smallest build in this list, closes the audit's largest gap, and makes the clock's urgency faction-shaped for the first time.
2. Give the adaptation intelligence line a permanent home: add the effect text and the why string to the card's Adapted line (or a one-line-per-adaptation list), keeping the modal as the announcement. The strings exist; the modal already formats them.
3. Print revealed weaknesses with their effect text, not names alone. The description field exists in the same table the card already reads for names.
4. Add fed and riches lines to the card for the factions that have them ("Fed on your losses: Nx" / plunder count), and check whether fed reaches the danger estimate; if it does not, the band is understating the one rival it most needs to price.
5. Announce non-sector-change adaptations and weakness reveals with a toast, reusing the modal's sentence. Depends on what campaign.gd raises (UNSURE), but the toast mechanism exists (campaign_ui.gd lines 239-244).
6. Draw danger bands on the flat map. map_intel.gd already computes them map-independently; map_view.gd just never asks.
7. Add a numeric sector progress readout to the header (nodes or fights cleared of total, from map data already drawn spatially).
8. Promote the sector condition's description from tooltip to a line of text under its strip box, and do the same audit pass over the other tooltip-only rules (rival rate, sigil rule): if a tooltip states a rule, the rule gets a text home.
9. Show the route scoring rule: one line in the hover card naming what Safest and Richest optimise (worst fight and danger sum; the value weights in words). Removes the one live contradiction between channels.
10. Repeat the active Heat rules in the intel strip or the side panel while they are in force, since they already move the danger bands the player is reading.

## UNSURE list

1. Whether his behaviour text, persona or traits appear on any battle, pre-fight or event screen (outside this fetch). Fix 1 stands for the campaign layer regardless.
2. How weaknesses become revealed, and whether any reveal is announced at the moment it happens (campaign.gd decides).
3. Whether adaptations gained outside a sector change (Chorus after every meeting, per Job 2) are ever announced anywhere.
4. What toasts actually fire during a run (messages are raised in campaign.gd); whether the strike landing itself (a raid taking credits or a ship, a tithe draining hull) is announced when it happens.
5. Whether fed enters the displayed danger for his fight through fleet building, since the danger function itself does not read it.
6. Whether the richest route's weights are explained on any screen.
7. Whether the 3D hover card and route planner are fully reachable and visibly open on keyboard focus alone (mechanism exists in code, map3d.gd lines 618-627).
8. Whether danger is shown at the moment of the fight itself, and whether the band thresholds (0.8, 1.05, 1.4) were tuned against play.
9. Whether any surface other than the aspect screen repeats the run's Heat rules during a sector.
10. Judgements that must wait for screenshots: per-node label stack legibility in dense sectors, hover card occlusion, side panel density as experienced, preview panel row crowding, and clutter as seen generally.

## Per-area verdicts

1. Route planning: PARTIAL. A real two-lens planner with running totals and full route accounts, ahead of BattleTech on pre-commitment transparency, but 3D only, boss-exclusive, and scored by rules the player cannot see.
2. Information at a glance: PARTIAL, leaning PASS. The side panel is a complete build and fleet summary; the misses are sector progress as a number, the condition's effect as text, and the Heat rules.
3. Danger readability: PARTIAL. An honest, self-derived estimate with the ratio stated in words, the strongest construction in the comparison set, at a coarser grain, with unproven calibration and a flat map that shows nothing.
4. The Nemesis as information: PARTIAL. Identity, portrait, scars, clock and node marking are all surfaced, settling Job 2's question; his behaviour, his growth counters and the content behind his weakness and adaptation names are not surfaced at all.
5. Layering and duplication: PARTIAL. Sound skeleton, single-source duplication that mostly reinforces; tooltip-only rules and modal-only announcements are the structural leaks.

**Overall verdict: PARTIAL.** The campaign screens already do the hard, unglamorous work (a real route planner, an honest danger estimate, a complete build panel) at a level that stands up against the comparison games; what keeps the layer from the R36 and R38 bar is that the game's signature system, the Nemesis, is the least fully explained thing on his own home screen, and that a handful of decision-shaped facts live only in tooltips, one-time modals, or the 3D map alone.
