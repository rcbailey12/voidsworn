# Story review: Voidsworn faction arcs (outlines draft)

Reviewer: Rey (chair review)
Date: Oct 26, 2026
Status: Written blind. The writer did not read or consult any other review of these outlines, and the draft was completed before the chair screened the legs that returned during writing.

## Verdict

GO WITH CONDITIONS. The writing itself is strong: five of the six factions have a voice you can recognise in two lines, and Hegemony Beat 2, "Acceptable Limits", is a real dilemma of the kind most games only pretend to offer. The weak layer is not the prose, it is the choice architecture underneath it. The same three problems repeat in arc after arc: half of the Beat 1 flags are never used again, the Beat 2 blue option stacks both normal rewards and removes the costs, and every Beat 3 choice is a pre-fight buff instead of a story decision. Fix those three patterns and the set is in good shape. There is also one naming fix that should happen before anything ships: the sample rival name Setekh in Hegemony Beat 1 is a near namesake of a named Warhammer 40k Necron dynasty (see section 3e).

## 1. What resonates in comparable games, and what transfers

### FTL: short text events, sharp risk, and blue options that feel earned

FTL events are short text encounters whose choices can go badly wrong: community guides catalogue events that can kill crew or damage the hull, so players learn to read a choice for risk before they take it (https://mikehopley.github.io/ftl-event-safety/). Its blue options are special choices opened by what your ship currently has, for example a crew type or a piece of equipment (https://ftl.fandom.com/wiki/Guides_and_Tips). Two lessons transfer directly. First, a 100 to 150 word card is plenty when the choice has a stated risk and a stated cost, which the Voidsworn outlines already do well. Second, a blue option feels earned when it reflects something the player built or chose earlier, and Voidsworn's flag-gated blues follow that same proven pattern. What does not transfer is FTL's gamble. The outlines state fixed results for each choice and never mention chance, so the reading tension here comes from trade-offs, not from hidden dice. That is fine, but it means a choice that looks balanced on paper will be solved by players once they know both results, where an FTL event stays tense because the outcome is uncertain. UNSURE: whether the built game adds any randomness to these choices. The outlines do not say so, and this review judges the text as written.

### Into the Breach: lean text, voice through small reactions, one thread carried forward

Into the Breach takes a deliberately light-handed approach to narrative: island leaders give brief context, and pilots stand out through short unique reactions to being damaged, killing enemies, or protecting civilians, rather than through cutscenes (https://www.gamedeveloper.com/design/let-s-compare-and-contrast-the-narrative-design-of-tactical-breach-wizards-and-into-the-breach). Its one strong memory device is that a pilot can be sent into the next timeline carrying their experience, so a single thread of continuity survives each reset run (https://www.engadget.com/2018-02-27-into-the-breach-subset-games-ftl-sequel-gameplay-video.html). What transfers: voice can be carried by reaction lines and word choice alone, which matters for a text-only game with no portraits, and the outlines already lean on this. What also transfers is the carried thread: Voidsworn's Chronicle on the Career screen could play the same role as the carried pilot, if it records what this run's commander actually did. What does not transfer: Into the Breach never builds to a grand authored finale, and its design accepts that. Voidsworn promises a finale at the Hollow Sun in every arc, so it has to deliver more payoff per beat than Into the Breach needs to.

### Hades: memory across runs is the story engine

In Hades, dialogue reacts to prior events and current state: characters comment on how you died, what weapon you carried, and what happened earlier in the run, and new pieces of story keep surfacing across many attempts (https://www.davideaversa.it/blog/hades-case-study-storytelling-roguelike-games/). New information arrives in small portions rather than lore dumps, and the game stays responsive to the player's earlier actions even deep into repeated runs (https://www.avclub.com/hades-and-failure). What transfers is the principle, and it is the most important one in this section: repetition stops feeling like repetition when the text notices what you did last time. Voidsworn has the machinery for a cheap version of this, the flag conditionals already written into its Beat 2 bodies. What does not transfer is the volume. Hades supports its memory with voice acting and a very large script; UNSURE, I did not verify an exact line count in this pass and do not cite one. Voidsworn has three beats per arc, so each conditional sentence has to do the work that Hades spreads across dozens of exchanges. That is an argument for spending those few conditional sentences on the rival (see section 3c), not only on your own fleet.

### Darkest Dungeon: a narrator voice, and damage that stays on the character

Darkest Dungeon's own feature list leads with its Affliction System, where stress produces gameplay-meaningful quirks such as paranoia and fear, a narration system that marks your successes and failures, and hero permadeath (http://store.steampowered.com/app/262060/Darkest_Dungeon/?curator_clanid=28455643&snr=1_1056_4_1056_1057). What transfers: first, a single consistent voice per arc is worth more than plot complexity, and the outlines already commit to one register per faction. Second, memory works best when it is visible on the character or record, the way a quirk is, rather than stored invisibly. Voidsworn's flags are currently invisible memory. The blue options and the Chronicle are the two places that memory becomes visible, which is why sections 3a and 3d push on both. What does not transfer: Darkest Dungeon works with a disposable roster in a single long campaign, so its memory can attach to individual heroes. Voidsworn arcs must attach memory to one run's commander and rival instead.

### BATTLETECH: travel events keyed to who is in the room

In BATTLETECH (Harebrained Schemes), the player runs a mercenary company, and travel brings random events such as disagreements between pilots or mishaps, with multiple choice outcomes, many of which are unlocked or modified by the traits of the characters involved or by the player's reputation with a faction in the event (https://www.sarna.net/wiki/index.php?title=BattleTech_(Video_Game)&diff=next&oldid=961627). What transfers: gating an event option on something specific the player has, a trait there, a flag here, is proven design in exactly this genre, a tactics game with a campaign layer. The difference worth copying is variety of keys. A BATTLETECH event keys off a roster that changes from campaign to campaign, so the same event plays differently for different players. A Voidsworn blue keys off one binary choice made earlier in the same run, which always has the same right answer once known (see section 3a). What does not transfer: event volume. A long mercenary campaign can carry many small throwaway events; a three-beat arc cannot afford a throwaway beat, and none of these eighteen beats should be one.

### Rogue Trader: fateful decisions at full CRPG scale, and a vocabulary warning

Owlcat's Rogue Trader was announced on the strength of fateful decisions, non-linear stories, and a strong and diverse companion cast, with choices that affect the lives of entire worlds (https://www.gematsu.com/2022/06/owlcat-games-announces-crpg-warhammer-40000-rogue-trader-for-consoles-pc). What transfers is the stance: choices should be framed as decisions a commander owns, with consequences the text names out loud. Hegemony's "Acceptable Limits" (Beat 2) already writes in that stance. What does not transfer is scale: companions with personal journeys over a full-length RPG cannot be compressed into 150 words, and trying would produce name-checks instead of story. Rogue Trader also matters to this review for a second reason. It is a licensed Warhammer 40k game, so its God-Emperor and heresy vocabulary is at home there. The same register in an unlicensed game reads as borrowing, which is the subject of section 3e.

### Shadow of Mordor: the rival who remembers is the whole point

The Nemesis System in Shadow of Mordor tracks ranked enemies who remember their previous encounters with the player and respond accordingly. An enemy who defeats you rises through the ranks, gains a name and title, and as ranks rise, enemies gain strengths and lose weaknesses; survivors remember specific encounters, such as being thrown into a fire, and want revenge for them, and players can interrogate lesser enemies to learn a leader's strengths and weaknesses (https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Middle-earth:_Shadow_of_Mordor). Two lessons transfer. First, the memory that players treasure is specific and spoken back to them: the rival names the thing that happened. Second, learning a rival's weakness feels good when it is framed as intel you worked for, which Voidsworn's weakness reveals can borrow directly. What does not transfer is the system itself: Mordor's rivals are procedurally promoted inside a hierarchy, while Voidsworn's rival is one scripted commander met on a fixed three-beat track. That is a legitimate smaller design, but it means the word Nemesis sets an expectation of personal memory that the current text only half meets (section 3c). One more caution: the Mordor system was patented by Warner Bros., covering methods for managing non-player characters based on hierarchies and individualised correspondences (https://www.gamesradar.com/middle-earth-shadow-of-mordors-nemesis-system-was-patented-by-warner-bros/). A scripted single rival is a long way from that system, and this note is an observation, not legal advice.

### Stellaris: small texts that accumulate into one destination

In Stellaris, precursor stories are explored through anomalies that spawn special projects, each granting a small reward and a precursor artifact, with six artifacts revealing the location of the precursor home system (https://stellaris.paradoxwikis.com/Discovery). What transfers: a chain of small, individually modest texts can add up to one anticipated destination, and the Hollow Sun is built to be exactly that, one place all six arcs travel toward. The three-beat version of the trick is foreshadowing discipline: each beat should add one new fact or image about the Hollow Sun rather than restating that it is a dead star, and the arcs mostly do this (fleet anchorage survey, treasure fleet in its glow, archive in its core). What does not transfer: Stellaris spreads its chains across dozens of optional finds in a long campaign, so any single text can be light. Voidsworn has exactly three mandatory beats per arc, so none of them can coast.

### Section 1 summary

Three lessons matter most for these outlines. A blue option should feel earned, not stacked (FTL, BATTLETECH). Memory should be spoken back by the rival, specifically (Shadow of Mordor, Hades). And a three-beat arc has no spare capacity, so every conditional sentence and every choice slot has to earn its place (Into the Breach, Stellaris).

## 2. Per faction

### Voidsworn Crusade, "The Oath at the Hollow Sun"

Strongest beat: Beat 2, "The Burning Choir". It is the arc's best piece of dilemma writing. Both courses cost something the prose makes you feel first: recovering the verse spends time while the rival prepares, striking at once leaves the choir singing to no one. The gutted hymn-ship with its looped recording is the strongest single image in this arc, and the conditional sentence for hand carriers, the saint's hand growing warm, is the flag memory done right: one sentence, only for the players who chose it.

Weakest beat: Beat 3, "Relight the Hollow Sun". The win line is excellent, the beacon catching on the third attempt is the best closing image in the whole set, but the beat's choice is the arc's emptiest. Sing the Rite or answer with the litany is a repair or experience pick dressed in ceremony. Nothing in the choice touches the arc's own objects: the hand, the oath, the keepers. The rival's one templated line is his only presence, so the climax of a crusade arc turns on fleet state, not on anything the commander swore or sold.

Biggest fix: make Beat 3 answer Beat 1. The hand should be spent, raised, or returned at the beacon, using only the verbs the campaign already has (relic, repairs, experience, credits are all already in this arc). A player who sold the hand should get a visibly different finale from a player who carried it, even if the difference is one conditional sentence in the body and one changed word in the win line. Right now the flag vesper_sold is set in Beat 1 and never mentioned again anywhere in the outlines, so the seller's arc and the carrier's arc converge completely by sector III.

### Hegemony Navy, "Pacification Order 41"

Strongest beat: Beat 2, "Acceptable Limits". This is the best beat in all six arcs. The dilemma is real because both options are defensible inside the faction's own values: the Admiralty's order is lawful and arguably correct at fleet scale, and the escort commander's message makes the cost of obeying a specific nineteen year old and eleven specific crews. The black box callback sentence shows the flag working as memory, and the title phrase coming back as the Admiralty's own words is the most controlled piece of writing in the set.

Weakest beat: Beat 1, "The Listening Post". The choice is tilted by its own framing. Recovering the black box is presented as the interesting, human, story carrying act, done quietly by an officer who clearly expects you to want it. Following orders is presented as declining the story, and its reward, a commendation and 50 credits, reads like a receipt. The line that Intelligence has a file on the rival and nothing in it helps also undercuts the payoff of the intel path one beat before that path pays out a revealed weakness. The beat works, but it works as a tutorial for the flags, not as a dilemma in its own right.

Biggest fix: give the orders flag a life. The flag hg_orders is set in Beat 1 and never used again, while hg_offbook gets a callback sentence and the blue option in Beat 2. Obeying should open something of its own later: an Admiralty favour, an authorisation that arrives early, a commendation that matters in Beat 3 where authorisation currently arrives eleven days late for everyone. Until both Beat 1 answers have a Beat 2 or Beat 3 echo, experienced players will treat off the record as the correct answer and the orders option as a trap for new players.

### Ashfall Corsairs, "Saints' Gold"

Strongest beat: Beat 2, "The Rattlehold Auction". It is the best staged scene in the set. The rival is physically present, front row, smiling, having bought the room in advance, which gives him a personality no other arc's rival gets before Beat 3. The three ways through, steal it and burn the docks, outbid him at cost, or remind him about the last box if you rigged it, are genuinely different in flavour, not just in price. The humour lands because it is doing character work: the bosun uneasy at being called honest tells you more about this faction than any lore sentence in the arc.

Weakest beat: Beat 3, "Saints' Gold". The arrival at the treasure fleet is good, and Old Mott weeping is good, but the choice is the weakest in the set. Promising double shares or promising nothing is a question about a split that only happens after the fight, and neither option states any consequence that the coda then honours. The win line describes the split taking three days and two fistfights, identical for the captain who promised double and the captain who promised nothing. The nothing option's stated benefit, credits held back for repairs, is bookkeeping where the arc's voice promised gallows humour.

Biggest fix: make the Beat 3 promise bind. The arc already states its own rule in the body: the captain who wins sets the split. So let the promise be the thing the win line remembers: double shares promised should cost something out of the haul or be celebrated by name, and promising nothing should be remembered as meanness the crews noticed. One conditional sentence in the win line, keyed to the Beat 3 choice itself, fixes this beat and gives the Chronicle something worth recording for Corsair runs.

### Chorus, "Eat the Sun"

Strongest beat: Beat 1, "The Silent Node". The cleanest statement of any faction in the set. Eat the dead and grow, or carry the silence so the hunger remembers whom to hate: appetite against memory, in the faction's own grammar, with a desecrated brood-node as the image. It also quietly sets up the arc's best idea, that this swarm can choose to remember a specific enemy, which is the most personal version of the Nemesis idea any of the six arcs proposes.

Weakest beat: Beat 2, "The Drowned Song". It nearly repeats Beat 1's shape: feed and heal while the rival benefits, or take the slower, stranger path for a longer gain. Beat 1 offered heal 30 percent against a revealed weakness; Beat 2 offers heal 30 percent against a new ship, with the same weakness returning in the blue. The mined shallows and the rival waiting in orbit are told rather than staged, and the spawn, the beat's one new element, get no line of their own. A player who understood Beat 1 has nothing new to weigh here.

Biggest fix: separate Beat 2 from Beat 1 in kind, not in numbers. Make the cost of feeding concrete in the prose, spawn lost to the mines, a throat gone quiet, so the heal is visibly bought with the thing the arc is about. Then let the teaching path pay off in the finale text: if the spawn were taught the song, Beat 3, "Eat the Sun", should sing with their voices added, the way the win line already sings with one more voice. One specification gap matters here: the outlines say every choice writes a flag, but the draft names flags only for Beat 1 choices, not for any Beat 2 choice in any arc. Until the Beat 2 flags are named on paper, a Beat 3 callback like this one cannot be checked against the draft, and the teaching choice is the callback this arc most needs.

### Ithar, "The Grave of Houses"

Strongest beat: Beat 2, "The Glass Choir". Cruelty you can hear. The rival breaking wrecks one by one so the screams blind your sensors is active, present-tense villainy, the strongest rival presence in any Beat 2, and the seraph who sang in this choir makes the field personal to a named member of your fleet. The blue option, singing the buoy's song so the wrecks fall silent, is the best earned blue in the set because Beat 1 told you exactly what taking that song cost.

Weakest beat: Beat 1, "The Lamp-Buoy". The dilemma is real, taking the song ends the buoy, but the mercy side is blurred. The buoy woke and sang a warning, it offers its song, and the option labelled letting it sleep pays out because your captains carry its warning instead, which is a third thing, neither taking the song nor refusing contact. A reader has to stop and work out what physically happened. This is also the arc where the rival is most distant in Beat 1: he exists only as a report of feeding on buoys' memories, secondhand twice over.

Biggest fix: clarify the buoy bargain, then pay mercy off. One clause stating that the song can only be taken by ending the buoy, and that letting it sleep means it keeps singing its warning to whoever passes next, would clean up the choice. Then give the mercy flag it_mercy what the song flag it_song already has: a later echo. The outlines never use it_mercy again. A surviving buoy that appears once more, at the edge of Beat 3 or in the win line's voyage home, would make restraint feel remembered rather than merely unrewarded.

### Deathless Dynasty, "The Court Restored"

Strongest beat: Beat 2, "A Crown on the Wrong Head". A values dilemma with a perfect image: the stolen crown worn openly in a broadcast, an insult calculated to eleven decimal places, and below you a sleeping reserve the old law forbids you to wake except in defence of the court. Waking it is power bought by breaking your own civilisation's oldest rule; keeping the law is pride and restraint. The blue option for patient players, triggering the flaw the archivists found in the crown, is the best argument in the whole set for Beat 1 restraint paying off in Beat 2.

Weakest beat: Beat 3, "The Court Restored". After two beats about law, patience, and counting, the finale choice is recite the census for experience or wake the honour guard for repairs, the generic finale shape again. Worse, the honour guard option raises a question the text does not answer: waking sleepers was the forbidden act in Beat 2, and Beat 3 offers waking more sleepers with no comment on whether the court's own guard is lawful to wake. It probably is, defence of the court is the stated exception, but the arc that taught the player to care about the law should say so in a clause, or make this the second, deliberate law test. As written, careful readers will hesitate for the wrong reason and uncareful readers will miss that a rule existed.

Biggest fix: let the census recital actually count this run. Beat 3's own body says the census engines finish their count of every warden, crown, and insult. So the Recite the census option should recite the arc's facts, law kept or law broken, patience or vengeance, templated from the flags the player set, instead of paying out flat experience in silence. That turns the finale choice into the Dynasty reading your run back to you, which is what this faction's voice has been promising since Beat 1.

## 3. Cross-cutting issues

### 3a. Dilemmas: real in Beat 2, keyed in Beat 1, absent in Beat 3

The eighteen beats sort into three tiers.

Real dilemmas. Hegemony Beat 2, "Acceptable Limits" (orders against eleven crews). Deathless Beat 2, "A Crown on the Wrong Head" (law against insult). Voidsworn Beat 2, "The Burning Choir" (time against reverence). Ithar Beat 2, "The Glass Choir" (the lament costs time and visibility, going dark costs hull). In each, the prose argues both sides before the options appear, and there is no answer a returning player can call simply correct.

Key selections dressed as dilemmas. Every Beat 1 works this way once you have played the arc once. Each Beat 1 flag that unlocks the Beat 2 blue option also tends to carry a respectable immediate reward: carrying the hand gains a relic, recovering the black box reveals a weakness, rigging the box gains credits, carrying the silence reveals a weakness, taking the buoy's song reveals a weakness, patience gains credits and the crown study. The alternative flags are not bad, but they lead nowhere (see 3a, dead flags, below). FTL survives this problem because its blue options key off a build assembled over hours and its normal options carry uncertain outcomes (sources in section 1). Here the key is a single choice made one sector earlier, with fixed stated results, so the dilemma reliably collapses on the second run of a faction.

Not dilemmas at all. All six Beat 3 choices. Three of the six are the identical pair, repair 20 percent against captain experience 20 (Voidsworn, Hegemony, Deathless, with Hegemony reversing the order). Chorus Beat 3 offers regrowth against a weakness reveal, Ithar offers experience against a weakness reveal, and Corsairs Beat 3 offers experience against credits. Choosing between hull and experience before a known final fight is a fleet state question, not a story question: there is a right answer and it is printed on your ship list. The most important fight in the run deserves the most argued choice in the arc, and currently gets the least argued one.

The blue stacking problem. In all six arcs, the Beat 2 blue option combines the rewards of both standard options while removing or reversing their costs. Voidsworn: weakness revealed plus 15 experience, where the standard options give one or the other and the intel option also advances the clock. Hegemony: the column lives, a weakness revealed, and 15 percent repairs. Corsairs: the box for 30 credits instead of 80, against a theft that starts your next fight ambushed. Chorus: weakness revealed and the clock goes back. Ithar: weakness revealed and no damage, replacing an option that pays 5 percent hull for the same reveal. Deathless: weakness revealed and the clock goes back. This is worth naming as a story problem, not a balance problem: a choice where one answer is written as strictly best teaches the player to stop reading. The prose in these beats is good enough that players should keep reading.

Dead flags. Half the Beat 1 choices write a flag that the outlines never use again: vesper_sold (Voidsworn), hg_orders (Hegemony), cs_party (Corsairs), ch_ate (Chorus), it_mercy (Ithar), dd_vengeance (Deathless). In every arc, only one of the two flags gets a conditional sentence in Beat 2 and the blue option. The corsair party flag is the saddest of the six: a crew that swore to the last scrap at a party should visibly fight like it, or visibly resent the captain who then promised them nothing at the treasure fleet. As drafted, choosing the non-blue path is choosing the thinner version of the same arc.

### 3b. Faction voice: distinct, with two collisions to police

The voices hold. You can tell these apart blind: Chorus speaks in hunger and short lines, Deathless in census and law, Corsairs in shares and debts, Hegemony in orders and appendices, Ithar in grief and long time. The system-spoken choice labels, percentages and gains, are identical across factions, which is normal for a game interface and not counted against the writing here.

First collision: Voidsworn against Hegemony. Both are disciplined human fleets under command. What keeps them apart is direction of address: Voidsworn speaks upward, to saints, oaths, and the dead; Hegemony speaks sideways, to the Admiralty, files, and reports. The outlines mostly police this well (Voidsworn writes names in chalk, Hegemony files a one-line report), but any future editing should treat a faith word in a Hegemony beat, or a paperwork word in a Voidsworn beat, as a small leak.

Second collision: song and choir everywhere. The Chorus's signature is song, yet a choir appears in three arcs: the Voidsworn hymn-ship and its choir in "The Burning Choir", the Ithar location in "The Glass Choir", and the Chorus's own song language throughout "Eat the Sun". Each use is individually fine, and the Ithar one is doing real work, but together they dilute the one word the Chorus most needs to own. Recommendation, not a hard finding: reserve choir, unqualified, for the Chorus, and rename around it elsewhere.

A note in the Chorus's favour: it is the only arc whose rival problem is structural rather than vocal. The Chorus voice stays in character even in its choice labels' subjects, eat, carry, teach, sing, while other arcs' bodies occasionally slip into neutral summary. The Chorus never sounds neutral. That is the standard the others should be held to in Beat 3, where several arcs go quiet at exactly the wrong moment.

### 3c. The rival: he acts, you react, he never answers

The rival does not remember you. He performs at you.

Evidence from the text. In every arc, the conditional sentences in Beat 2 are keyed to the player's flag: the hand grows warm, the black box gave you his patrol pattern, the silence has a shape, the buoy's song rises in your throat, the archivists finished studying the crown. Every one of them describes your fleet's state. None of them shows the rival speaking or acting differently because of what you did in Beat 1. His Beat 2 deeds are fixed: he hangs the hymn-ship, holds the exit, sits in the auction's front row, seeds the shallows, breaks the wrecks, wears the crown, identically, for every player, whatever they chose. The single exception proves the rule: the corsair blue option says he flinches when reminded about the last box, and that one word, flinches, is the only moment in eighteen beats where the rival visibly reacts to your history. It is in a choice label, not in a beat body.

Beat 3 then gives him one hail, one set of last words, and one gloat, selected only by his own faction from the rival voice table. Nothing in those lines can reference the hand, the black box, the rigged box, the silence, the song, or the crown, because the table has no slots for them. Shadow of Mordor's lesson (section 1) is that players treasure the rival naming the specific thing that happened. Hades makes the same point at larger scale: the text notices what you did. Here the noticing all runs one way.

There is a second, quieter problem. On a repeat run of the same player faction, the rival's name and faction change, but his deeds, words, and beats are pasted onto the new name unchanged. A Warlord Grim Rattigan and whoever replaces him next run strip the same reliquary and leave the same message cut in the same stone. Changing only the name risks making the template more visible, not less: the player learns that the rival is a slot, at the exact moment the game most wants him to feel like a person.

His scripted deeds also ignore his own faction. Some fit anyone: seeding mines, holding an exit. Two fit badly for at least one possible rival. In Corsairs Beat 2, he has bought the auctioneer, the guards, and the docking clamps, which is natural for a Hegemony officer or a rival corsair and strange for a Chorus swarm. In Deathless Beat 2, he wears a crown in a broadcast and offers to return it if the Dynasty kneels, which is natural for most rivals and strange for a Chorus hunger that, in its own arc, does not bargain in words at all. UNSURE how much this matters in play: the outlines do not state how strongly a rival's faction identity is presented before Beat 3, and if the player mostly meets a name and a fleet, the friction may be minor. The chair read is that the two cases above are worth a fallback sentence each, not a rewrite.

Cheap fix, using machinery the outlines already prove exists: one conditional sentence in Beat 3, keyed to the player's Beat 1 flag, spoken by or about the rival, mirroring the Beat 2 conditional sentences that already exist. He names the hand. He names the black box recovery. He wears the crown's flaw. That single sentence per arc would do more for the rival than any new hail line.

### 3d. Replay: the template will show, and the Chronicle is the defence

Assume a player who runs the same faction several times, which is the normal way to play a roguelite. What varies between those runs, per the outlines: the rival's name and faction, his three Beat 3 lines drawn from a table of six sets, which Beat 1 flag path you took, and the win or loss ending. What does not vary: all eighteen beat bodies for that faction, every rival deed, every dilemma, every coda.

Expect staleness in a predictable order. Beat 3 goes first: its choice is a solved buff pick, so on run two it is a button, not a scene. Beat 1 goes second: once the blue key is known, the choice is a build decision. Beat 2 goes last, because its prose dilemmas, Hegemony's above all, stay live even when you know the outcomes: knowing what leaving the column costs does not make leaving it feel free. This ordering is actually good news. It says the writing in the middle beats is carrying replay nearly alone, and the fix list concentrates on the first and last beats for that reason.

The Chronicle is the set's best anti-staleness asset and its least specified one. The outlines say the arc closes with a win or loss line and lands in a Chronicle on the Career screen. They do not say what a Chronicle entry contains. If an entry records only that the arc was completed, it is a stamp collection. If it records the flags, hand carried or sold, law kept or broken, shares doubled or withheld, then each run files a different short record, and the Hades lesson applies: the game remembering you, in writing, between runs, is what makes the next run feel like a continuation. UNSURE: this may already be the build intent. The outlines do not say, and the fix is written as a specification request, not a claim about the code.

One more replay point, in the set's favour: because the rival can come from five factions, a player who plays all six factions meets the shared Hollow Sun finale six times in six registers, and that cross-faction variety is real. The risk is concentrated in same-faction repeats, and the fixes above are aimed there.

### 3e. Warhammer 40k proximity: the roster maps, so the words have to work harder

No single word in these outlines belongs to Games Workshop. Crusades, saints, dynasties, tombs, and dying elder races are all older than Warhammer 40k. The proximity risk is at roster level: four of the six factions map closely onto a 40k faction, and a fifth doubles one of those mappings. A reader who knows 40k will make the mapping in seconds, so the defence has to be the factions' own obsessions, which are genuinely different, showing earlier and louder than the shared furniture.

Deathless Dynasty and the Necrons: highest risk. In 40k, Necrons are organised into dynasties ruled by Phaerons, served by royal courts, with Overlords ruling clusters of Tomb Worlds, and the race waking from the Great Sleep, a long hibernation, to reclaim its old empire (https://wh40k.lexicanum.com/mediawiki/index.php?title=Necron&oldid=575478). The Deathless arc uses dynasty, tomb after tomb waking, a restored court, old crownworlds held by squatters who must be corrected, and a ten thousand year wait, all in three beats. Its saving difference is real: census, law, and accountancy as a personality is not the Necron register, and the stolen crown with a maker's flaw is an original plot. Fixes: lean the opening of Beat 1 on the census report before the tomb imagery arrives, and change the sample rival name in Hegemony Beat 1. Dynast Setekh the Patient is a near namesake of Sautekh, listed as a major Necron dynasty (https://wh40k.lexicanum.com/mediawiki/index.php?title=Oruscar_Dynasty&mobileaction=toggle_view_mobile). Whether or not that echo was intended, it is the single most citable proximity item in the document and it costs one word to remove.

Voidsworn Crusade and the Imperium's faith: high risk, and mostly a word density problem. In 40k, saints are proclaimed by the Ecclesiarchy, living saints lead Wars of Faith and Crusades, and heretics are the enemy within and without (https://wh40k.lexicanum.com/mediawiki/index.php?title=Living_Saint&oldid=612716). The Voidsworn arc stacks crusade, heresy and heretic, saint, reliquary and relic, litanists, confessors, and a pyre in the rival gloat table, into one faction. Its own centre is different and better: an oath, a dead beacon to relight, names written in chalk by people who expect to burn. Fixes: cut the heresy vocabulary roughly in half and let the faction's own word, oath-breaker, do that work; consider renaming confessors to a title built from oaths; keep saint if the faction keeps only one borrowed register word at full strength.

Chorus and the Tyranids: medium risk. 40k's Tyranids are hive fleets under a single hive mind, driven by unending hunger, consuming biomass to grow (https://www.wargamer.com/warhammer-40k/factions). The Chorus shares swarm, hunger, eating, and broods. It differs where it matters: it sings, it can choose, and its defining act in Beat 1 is carrying a silence so the hunger remembers a specific enemy, which is grief and grudge, and a hive mind that eats does not hold grudges against individuals. Fixes: keep the personal memory motif in the front of every Chorus text, and it already is in Beat 1; watch the choice labels, where eat and regrow percentages make the Chorus sound most like a generic devourer and least like itself.

Ithar and the Aeldari: medium-low risk. The Aeldari are an ancient race whose galaxy spanning empire collapsed, who are now a dying race with low fertility (https://wh40k.lexicanum.com/mediawiki/index.php?title=Aeldari&oldid=619846). The Ithar share the fallen empire, the long lives, and the fact that when they die nothing is born to replace them, which is the most Aeldari-shaped sentence in the document. They differ in furniture: song, archives, lamp-buoys, and sails, with no psychic powers, no craftworlds, and no farseers anywhere in the outlines, and that restraint should be protected in future writing. Fix: none needed now beyond guarding that restraint; if any later draft adds prophecy or seers to the Ithar, the mapping closes and this rating rises.

Hegemony and Corsairs: low risk. Admiralty, orders, and acceptable limits are the language of real navies and colonial offices, not of one game, and the Hegemony's nearest 40k cousin, the Imperial Navy, shares only that generic furniture. The Corsairs are pirates organised around shares and debts, a universal type, and their skull with a gear jaw is the one crest a chair should glance at twice, UNSURE whether it echoes any specific 40k ork or pirate mark, I did not verify a specific source for that resemblance and do not assert one. Both arcs are safe enough that they can lend distinctiveness to the set as a whole: the more the set reads as six kinds of people, not five kinds of 40k army and one pirate, the weaker the roster mapping becomes.

## 4. Ranked top 10 fixes

1. Give every non-blue Beat 1 flag a later payoff (all six arcs; flags vesper_sold, hg_orders, cs_party, ch_ate, it_mercy, dd_vengeance). Half the opening choices currently dead-end after Beat 1. One conditional sentence or one echo option per flag, in Beat 2 or Beat 3, doubles the number of real paths through each arc at the lowest writing cost of any fix on this list.
2. De-stack the Beat 2 blue options (all six arcs, Beat 2). The blue should be a different kind of good, not both standard rewards with the costs removed. Give each blue a trade the player can argue about, so having the flag opens a choice instead of ending one.
3. Give the rival one history-keyed line per arc (all six arcs, Beat 3, using the rival voice slot). One conditional sentence where he names what you did in Beat 1, built on the same flag conditional pattern the Beat 2 bodies already use. This is the cheapest large gain available: it is the difference between a Nemesis and a name slot.
4. Make Beat 3 a story choice, not only a buff pick (all six arcs, Beat 3). Tie at least one option per finale to the arc's own object, the hand, the names, the split, the taught spawn, the archive, the law, and let the win or loss line show which you chose.
5. Rename the sample rival Setekh and trim tomb and sleep density at the top of Deathless Beat 1 (Hegemony Beat 1 sample name; Deathless Dynasty Beat 1, "The Opened Tomb"). Setekh is a near namesake of the Necron dynasty Sautekh, and Beat 1 front-loads the exact Necron furniture, dynasty, tombs waking, court, before the arc's own census voice establishes itself.
6. Cut heresy vocabulary by about half in the Voidsworn arc and replace it with oath language (Voidsworn Crusade, Beats 1 to 3 and the Voidsworn row of the rival voice table). The oath, the beacon, and the chalk names are this faction's own; heretic, confessor, and pyre are borrowed register, and the borrowing is what a 40k reader will quote.
7. Specify what a Chronicle entry records, and make it record the flags (all six arcs, arc close). A Chronicle that files hand carried or sold, law kept or broken, column saved or left, turns cross-run memory from a hope into a feature, and it is the set's main defence against same-faction staleness.
8. Separate Chorus Beat 2 from Beat 1 in kind (Chorus Beat 2, "The Drowned Song"). Make feeding cost something visible in the prose, name the Beat 2 flags in the draft (none are named in any arc, including teach the spawn) so later beats can key off them, and let taught spawn appear in the Beat 3 song.
9. Clarify the law around waking sleepers in Deathless Beat 3 (Deathless Dynasty Beat 3, "The Court Restored"). One clause stating the court's honour guard is lawful to wake in the court's defence, or a deliberate second law test. The arc taught the player a rule; the finale must visibly respect it.
10. Reserve the word choir for the Chorus and rename around it elsewhere (Voidsworn Beat 2, "The Burning Choir"; Ithar Beat 2, "The Glass Choir"). Song is the Chorus's signature, and two other factions using its word in beat titles spends distinctiveness the set worked hard to build.
