RANKED FINDINGS (twenty, seven-part form, biggest cinematic gain first)
Finding 1 - The shield is a perfect honeycomb egg that nothing touches
What the player sees now. Every ship's shield is a flawless ellipsoid wrapped in a regular honeycomb grid, identical in every row, every faction, every state. A weapon hit produces the same beige puff at the bubble's edge, visually disconnected from the surface. A shield at one-fifth strength is pixel-identical to a full shield. The bubble bleaches the ship inside it white, so a shielded vessel reads as a ghost of itself.
What a film would do instead. In Star Trek: The Next Generation and the 2009 Star Trek film, a shield hit flares locally at the point of impact - a bright ripple that spreads two or three panels outward and fades, while the rest of the bubble stays dim. In Stargate SG-1, repeated hits on the same quadrant visibly weaken that section. In Sins of a Solar Empire II, shield impacts produce directional local flares and the shield's overall opacity drops with remaining strength. The shield answers the hit; it does not ignore it.
Where the limit lives. docs/playbooks/effects.md, section 1: "Shields: invisible at rest, a faint edge glow on zoom, hover or a recent hit, a downed side reads as a dark gap, and they keep faction colours (R163; Oct 24)." No rule exists for a localised impact response, for damage state, or for the honeycomb density varying. The bench caption for m3 admits the damage-state look "does not exist yet."
Why it is there. A single static mesh is trivially checkable and costs one draw call. A per-hit ripple or per-state opacity would need a shader parameter fed by game state, which was not specced. Nothing recorded as a deliberate aesthetic choice.
The loosening you propose. (a) Replace the uniform honeycomb with a Voronoi-cell pattern whose cell density and edge brightness are driven by a single shield_hp_ratio uniform; at 100 % the cells are tight and dim, at 25 % they are sparse, flickering and dark at the edges. (b) On each hit, spawn a local flare: a 0.35 s radial ripple centred on the impact hex-face, lighting 3-5 neighbouring cells, using the existing torpedo-on-shield row (110 px flash, 156 px ripple body from deaths_and_hits.md) as the size anchor. (c) Remove the white bleach; instead tint the interior by faction colour at 12-18 % opacity so the ship remains readable. (d) Shield break: the cells crack outward from the last hit point, going dark in a 0.4 s wave, not a single flash-and-ring.
Size of the job. Medium (two to three agent runs: one shader pass, one game-state hook, one bench strip). Frame-time cost: one extra uniform update per shielded ship per frame, negligible; the ripple is a single quad decal, well within budget.
Picture to point at. docs/shots/look_bench/m2 (shield hits) and m3 (shield states).
Finding 2 - Every weapon hits the hull the same way; one scale for everything
What the player sees now. In m1, ten different weapons land on the same hull and nine of them produce the same two-part stamp: one small round white glow and one soft beige puff. A macro cannon, a las, an autocannon, a scatter battery and a plasma hit are interchangeable at the battle camera. In m5, the same battleship macro battery hits an Eclipse frigate, a destroyer, a cruiser and a tomb-ark, and the impact puff and glow are the same size on all four, while the incoming tracer is a glowing comet larger than the frigate itself.
What a film would do instead. In Battlestar Galactica (2004), a kinetic round sparks and ricochets off angled armour in a directional cone; a missile bloom is dirty, asymmetric and throws debris across the hull. In The Expanse, railgun impacts punch clean holes with a fast metallic spray; torpedo hits are slower, fatter, and leave a burning wound. Scale is everything: a PDC burst on a destroyer is a pinprick; a nuke on a capital is a sun. In Sins of a Solar Empire II, weapon classes have distinct impact shaders and the impact size scales with both weapon class and target class.
Where the limit lives. docs/playbooks/deaths_and_hits.md, "The hits, upgraded" table: Las 40 px, Macro 56 px, Lance 78 px per pulse, Torpedo 154 px. The 1.4× step is stated in effects.md section 1: "Each step up the ladder is about 1.4 times the one below." But the table has no column for target size, no column for angle of incidence, and no entry for a "glancing" or "absorbed" variant. The bench m5 confirms the same puff at every scale.
Why it is there. One impact effect per weapon type is easy to bench at camera 170 and easy to tick off the staging table. Scaling by target size would multiply the number of baked assets. Nothing recorded as a deliberate "small is fine" choice; it is a convenience that survived because the check is "is it at the table's size within 15 percent?"
The loosening you propose. (a) Add a target_scale multiplier: frigate hits render at 0.6×, destroyer 0.8×, cruiser 1.0×, capital 1.4×, tomb-ark/barg 1.8× the table's px value. The 15 % bench tolerance absorbs this. (b) Split each weapon's impact into a directional component (sparks, molten spray along the surface normal, 3-5 particles) and a radial component (the existing glow/puff). The directional part sells the hit as physical. (c) Give the las a tiny scorch (already in the table: "a small scorch that glows for about a second and a half") but make it actually render at camera 170 - at 40 px flash the scorch is sub-pixel today. Bump the persistent mark to 18 px minimum. (d) A capital absorbing a macro hit should show the puff flattening against the hull (squashed ellipse, 0.6× height) rather than blooming outward like a frigate hit.
Size of the job. Large (more than three runs: one per weapon-class rescale, one for the directional particle pass, one bench sweep). Frame-time: 3-5 extra particles per hit is trivial; the persistent scorch is one decal quad per hit, capped at 12 per ship.
Picture to point at. docs/shots/look_bench/m1 (all weapons on hull), m5 (one weapon at every weight).
Finding 3 - The only material in the game is a soft beige cotton ball
What the player sees now. Every explosion, hit and death in the look bench is made of the same soft, round, beige-grey puff, scaled up or down. The torpedo's puff is the las puff enlarged. Nothing is ever thrown: no fragments with trajectories, no venting jets, no debris that keeps moving, nothing left behind after the puff fades. One material, one speed, one direction (outward, symmetric).
What a film would do instead. Real destruction has at least two materials moving at different speeds: a bright fast component (sparks, molten metal, plasma) and a slow dirty component (smoke, gas, dust). In The Expanse, a torpedo hit throws a cone of white-hot fragments ahead of a billowing grey cloud. In Battlestar Galactica, hull breaches vent atmosphere as a focused white jet that persists for seconds. In Alien (1979), the Nostromo's damage throws sparks, smoke and coolant in different directions at different speeds. In Homeworld, ship deaths trail a long ribbon of debris that keeps drifting.
Where the limit lives. docs/playbooks/effects.md, section 5, Traps: "Pale blobs. Before route B, a torpedo hit was a large cream cloud with no fire in it." Route B added a hot core, but the body is still one gas puff. deaths_and_hits.md rule 2: "Flash and snuff out. No flame lives longer than 0.70 s; no hot core holds full heat longer than 0.25 s (owner ruling)." This caps the slow dirty component before it can exist. The flame cap kills lingering smoke.
Why it is there. The flame cap is an owner ruling (Ryan, via the staging table) to prevent "rolling fire" and keep deaths snappy. The single-material puff is a baking convenience: one particle texture, one shader, one size curve.
The loosening you propose. (a) Keep the 0.70 s flame cap for the hot component. Add a second, cold component: a dark grey-brown gas that persists 2.5-4 s, drifts with a slight directional bias (away from the hit normal), and thins rather than pops. This obeys "flash and snuff" for fire while giving the eye a lingering material. (b) Add 4-8 small bright fragments (2-4 px at camera 170) that travel outward in a cone at 3-5× the gas speed and fade over 0.5 s. These are the "bright fast" material. (c) For deaths only, add one or two "venting jets": narrow white streaks from breach anchors, lasting 1.2 s, aimed along the hull surface normal. The jet reads as atmosphere escaping.
Size of the job. Medium (two runs: one for the cold-gas layer and fragments on hits, one for the vent jets on deaths). Memory: two additional small textures (64 × 64 gas, 16 × 16 spark); negligible. Frame-time: 4-8 extra particles per event, well within budget.
Picture to point at. docs/shots/look_bench/m1, m4, deaths/cruiser_death_beats.jpg.
Finding 4 - Beams are ruler-straight, uniform, hard-edged bars; one shot crosses the entire frame
What the player sees now. The lance and ion beam in m1 and m2 are perfect white bars: constant width, constant brightness, sharp edges, no taper, no flicker, no brighter head or fading tail. The gauss hit in m3 draws a thin line across the ENTIRE frame, edge to edge, like a scratch on the photograph rather than something in the world.
What a film would do instead. In Star Wars, a turbolaser bolt has a bright head, a dimmer trailing body, and a slight glow halo. In Star Trek, a phaser beam wobbles slightly, has a visible source flare at the emitter, and a splash of sparks at the target. In The Expanse, a railgun round is invisible but its muzzle flash and the target's impact are the two visible events; the "beam" is implied. In Sins of a Solar Empire II, beam weapons have a hot core, a softer outer glow, a slight sine-wave wobble and a bright impact flare. None is a constant-width bar.
Where the limit lives. docs/playbooks/deaths_and_hits.md, hit table: "Lance: 78 px each pulse. A contact burn that stays lit and crawls along the hull while the beam dwells, spraying molten metal; a third of a second of tail when it lifts." The design calls for a crawl and a tail, but the bench stills show a static bar. effects.md section 3: "the flash reaches its size inside two frames." Two frames means the beam appears at full width instantly: no charge-up, no thinning at the tail.
Why it is there. A constant-width quad is the simplest render primitive. The "two frames to full size" rule was written for flash effects and applied to beams by analogy. No wobble or taper was specced because the staging table measures width in pixels and a wobble would fail the "within 15 percent" check.
The loosening you propose. (a) Give the beam a three-part profile: a bright emitter flare (1.5× beam width, 0.1 s), a mid-section with ±8 % width oscillation at 6 Hz, and a target splash (the existing 78 px contact burn). (b) Add a 0.15 s ramp-in (thin to full) and a 0.33 s tail (the design already says "a third of a second of tail when it lifts" - enforce it). (c) For the gauss: do NOT draw a line across the frame. Draw a 3-4 px bright tracer that travels from muzzle to target in 0.08 s, with a small muzzle flash and a target spark. The line-across-frame is a rendering artefact of drawing the full path at once. (d) The ion beam gets a faint blue-white halo (2 px) and visible arcing at the contact point (already in cues.json: "arc" layer).
Size of the job. Medium (two runs: one for beam profile and timing, one to replace the gauss full-frame line with a travelling tracer). Frame-time: negligible; the beam is one quad with a shader. The wobble is a vertex offset.
Picture to point at. docs/shots/look_bench/m1 (lance, ion beam), m3 (gauss line across frame).
Finding 5 - Chain blasts are identical, evenly spaced, and the core flash is a centred cauliflower
What the player sees now. In the death beat strips, the capital's blast 4 (1.58 s) and blast 5 (1.93 s) are the same white sparkle band at the same density. The cruiser's blasts 1 to 3 are the same small fire at three evenly marched positions (0.5, 0.8, 1.1 s per the playbook). The core flash is a perfectly round orange ball with a scalloped edge, centred on the ship, owing nothing to the ship's shape or where it was hit.
What a film would do instead. In Star Wars: The Force Awakens, the destruction of the Hosnian system shows explosions of wildly different sizes with irregular timing. In Battlestar Galactica, a dying ship has small pops, then a big one, then silence, then one more - the rhythm is uneven and surprising. In The Expanse, the Donnager's death has internal explosions that stagger in size and position, some deep and muffled, one dramatically bigger. The key device: one blast breaks the pattern.
Where the limit lives. docs/playbooks/deaths_and_hits.md, cruiser death table: "Three chain blasts walk along it at about 0.5, 0.8 and 1.1 s, each at least 75 px, each a flash with a hot core that is gone in a quarter second." The word "each" and the equal 0.3 s spacing enforce sameness. Capital: "Five chain blasts walk the break line." Same "each" logic. The core flash: "302 px for 0.20 s, at the lip of the torn face" - but the bench shows it centred on the ship, not at the lip.
Why it is there. Equal spacing is checkable: you can count three blasts and verify the timing. "At least 75 px" sets a floor but no ceiling variation, so all three land at 75 px. The centred core flash is easier to attach than one offset to the tear's lip.
The loosening you propose. (a) Stagger the chain blasts: cruiser at 0.42, 0.74, 1.15 s (gaps of 0.32, 0.41); sizes 68, 82, 74 px. Capital: five blasts at 0.5, 0.85, 1.1, 1.55, 1.9 s, sizes 72, 95, 65, 88, 78 px. One blast per death is 1.3× the others - the "one bigger than the pattern" rule. (b) Offset each blast ±12 px from the break line so they do not march in a row. (c) The core flash: place it at the tear's widest point, not the ship's centre. Make it elliptical (1.3× along the tear axis, 0.8× across), not a round ball. (d) Add one deep blast: a muffled glow inside the hull (no external flash, just the hull plates lighting from within for 0.3 s) between two external blasts. This is free drama inside the FLASH_CAP of 3.
Size of the job. Small (one run: retime and resize the existing chain blast effect, offset positions, reshape the core flash). No new assets.
Picture to point at. docs/shots/look_bench/deaths/cruiser_death_beats.jpg, deaths/capital_death_beats.jpg.
Finding 6 - Nothing lights its neighbours; space resets after every act of violence
What the player sees now. Across every capture, no explosion throws light on a nearby hull, no fire reflects off armour, no scorch or residue persists after the effect fades, no debris field hangs where a ship died. Each event happens in its own clean bubble of space and leaves the scene exactly as it was before.
What a film would do instead. In The Expanse, a torpedo detonation floods the surrounding ships with harsh white light for the duration of the flash; nearby hulls show reflected fire. In Battlestar Galactica, a Viper explosion illuminates the hull of the Galactica. In Star Wars, the Death Star's explosion lights every ship in the frame. In Sins of a Solar Empire II, capital-ship deaths cast a visible light radius. Persistent wreckage fields mark where ships died for the rest of the battle.
Where the limit lives. docs/playbooks/deaths_and_hits.md: "Light on neighbours. Torpedo hits and every death light the hulls around them for the length of the flash." The rule exists but the bench shows no evidence of it: m4, m5, f1-f3 show no secondary illumination. effects.md section 4 checks: "The bench strip at camera 170 shows the effect at the table's size within 15 percent." The check measures the effect itself, not whether it lights anything else. The design is written; the implementation or the bench does not yet prove it.
Why it is there. Per-frame dynamic lighting on neighbouring ships is more expensive than a self-contained particle effect. The bench measures the effect in isolation on a single ship, so the light-on-neighbours pass has no check to satisfy.
The loosening you propose. (a) Implement the already-written rule: a point light at the flash position, radius 2 hexes (torpedo) or 3 hexes (capital death, per the playbook: "it lights every hull within about two hexes" / "lights hulls within about three hexes"), intensity following the flash curve, colour matching the flash (white-hot → orange). This is one extra light uniform per active flash. (b) Persistent scorch decals: the deaths_and_hits.md table already specifies them ("A wound that glows and cools over about four seconds, then a dark crater that stays"; "A dent that glows and cools over about two and a half seconds"). Enforce the stays part. Cap at 8 decals per ship to bound memory. (c) Small-craft deaths and frigate deaths leave a 2-3 px debris speck that drifts for 6-8 s. Twenty specks in a fleet battle make the space feel used.
Size of the job. Medium (two to three runs: one for the point-light pass, one for persistent decals, one bench sweep). Frame-time: one point light per active flash (max 2-3 simultaneous given FLASH_CAP) is cheap. Decals: 8 quads per ship is negligible.
Picture to point at. docs/shots/look_bench/m4, f1 (fleet under fire - no cross-lighting visible).
Finding 7 - Small deaths are a puff and a ring; the Eclipse burn-out is nearly invisible
What the player sees now. In m4, the frigate and destroyer die as the standard puff plus a translucent lavender ring, a perfect circle expanding on cue, with three small orange flame ticks on top. The Sunward cruiser's death puff is barely larger than the frigate's. The Eclipse burn-out row is the extreme case: three frames of a dark hulk, almost no visible event at all, a capital-scale death a viewer could miss while looking at it.
What a film would do instead. In Star Wars, a TIE fighter death is a quick, bright, directional pop with a small debris scatter - tiny but visible. A Star Destroyer death in Return of the Jedi is a long, multi-stage event with internal flashes, hull buckling and a final bloom. The contrast between the two is the drama. In The Expanse, a small ship dying is a brief but sharp flash and a tumbling wreck; a capital is a prolonged catastrophe. The Eclipse "lights go out, nothing happens" is cinematically valid if it is followed by a visible cold drift and a faint gas vent - the absence of fire is the faction's voice, but the absence of anything is a gap.
Where the limit lives. docs/playbooks/deaths_and_hits.md, frigate/escort/destroyer section: "0.72: the pop. Frigate: 216 px flash for 0.18 s, 180 px body for about a second." The ring is not in the playbook; it appears to be a default particle. Eclipse death: "Lights and engines die; one vent of gas. No break: a whole cold hulk drifts. No fire at all." The playbook says "one vent of gas" but the bench shows nothing visible. Small craft: "A single-frame spark and a puff, under 40 px, no wreck. Twenty of them dying must never hide a cruiser."
Why it is there. The 40 px cap on small craft is a readability rule (twenty dying must not clutter). The frigate's 216 px is on the ladder. The Eclipse "no fire at all" is a faction identity rule and is good design, but the vent that should sell it is apparently too small or absent at camera 170.
The loosening you propose. (a) Replace the expanding lavender ring with a directional debris scatter: 3-5 small bright fragments in a cone away from the hit, plus one short gas jet. The ring reads as a UI element, not physics. (b) Eclipse burn-out: make the single gas vent visible - a 30 px narrow white jet from one engine, lasting 0.8 s, plus the running lights dying bow-to-stern over 0.5 s (already in the cruiser tell; apply it here). The hulk then drifts with a very faint rotation. The absence of fire is the point, but the vent and the lights-dying are the visible events. (c) Small craft: keep the 40 px cap but replace the "single-frame spark" with a 3-frame event: bright spark, one small fragment, gone. Three frames reads as a pop; one frame reads as a glitch.
Size of the job. Small (one run for the frigate/destroyer ring replacement, one for the Eclipse vent visibility, one for the small-craft 3-frame pop). No new textures.

Finding 8 - FLASH_CAP = 3 flattens the biggest moments; drama must be found inside it
What the player sees now. godot/fx/fx.gd FLASH_CAP := 3: at most three large bright flashes in any second, applied to everyone regardless of the reduce-flashing comfort option. In a fleet battle where a capital dies (core flash + 5 chain blasts + 10 secondaries), most of the death beats must queue or drop their flashes. The result: a capital death, the loudest visual event in the game, looks like a cruiser death with extra steps because the big flashes are rate-limited.
What a film would do instead. A film has no flash cap; it uses the full dynamic range of the image. But a film also does not need to protect a player's photosensitivity. The device a film uses instead of more flashes is bigger, slower, more colourful light: a long ember glow, a colour shift from white to orange to deep red, a slow bloom that fills the frame over a second. The drama is in the sustain, not the strobe.
Where the limit lives. godot/fx/fx.gd, FLASH_CAP := 3, cited in FINDINGS R187: "the Board's one caution: FLASH_CAP (three large flashes a second, godot/fx/fx.gd) is the WCAG 2.3.1 photosensitivity threshold and applies to everyone." The pack instruction: "Do not propose simply removing it. Do report where it visibly flattens a fight, and propose ways to get the drama inside it."
Why it is there. WCAG 2.3.1 photosensitivity safety. Real and non-negotiable as a floor.
The loosening you propose. (a) Redefine "large bright flash" for the cap: only count flashes above 120 px and above 70 % luminance. The 75 px chain blasts and the 40 px las hits fall below the threshold and no longer consume cap budget. This immediately frees the cap for the events that matter (torpedo hits, core flashes, shield breaks). (b) Replace queued flashes with lingering light: a 1.5 s warm glow (orange → deep red) at the blast site that does not count as a flash because it ramps slowly (rise > 0.25 s). (c) Use colour and area instead of brightness: a deep-red bloom at 200 px reads as "big" without triggering the cap, because the cap measures bright flashes. (d) Sound carries the hits the cap blocks: pair each suppressed flash with a low-frequency thump (see Finding 12). (e) For the reduce-flashing option, the existing softer rise/fall per the staging table remains. Whether to relax the cap for players who have not opted into reduce-flashing is Ryan's call; the facts above are for him.
Size of the job. Medium (two runs: one to reclassify the flash threshold, one to add the lingering-glow alternative). No memory cost. Frame-time: one additional glow quad per suppressed flash.
Picture to point at. docs/shots/look_bench/deaths/capital_death_beats.jpg (the strip where scene dim ON and OFF are "hard to tell apart" - evidence of the cap flattening).
Finding 9 - The flame cap (0.70 s life, 0.25 s full heat) kills every lingering fire
What the player sees now. No fire in the game rolls, lingers, or cools visibly. Every flame and hot core appears, holds for a quarter second, and vanishes inside 0.7 s. A torpedo hit's fire is gone before the gas puff has fully expanded. A death's secondary explosions are individual pops with no thermal continuity between them. The wreck does not glow and cool in a visible arc.
What a film would do instead. In Alien, the Nostromo's fires burn and flicker for minutes. In The Expanse, a breached hull vent burns for the duration of the scene. In Battlestar Galactica, a damaged ship trails fire and smoke for the rest of the episode. Even in the compressed grammar of a game, Sins of a Solar Empire II shows wreck fires that dim over 5-10 seconds. The key device: fire that cools through a colour arc (white → yellow → orange → red → dark) over 2-4 seconds sells heat and mass.
Where the limit lives. docs/playbooks/effects.md, section 1: "Flame cap: no flame or hot core lives longer than 0.70 s, and none stays at full heat longer than 0.25 s (owner ruling). Flash and snuff out, never rolling fire." deaths_and_hits.md, rule 2: same text. The wreck section says "The torn faces show their decks, glowing white, then orange, then dull red, then dark" over "to about 10" seconds - but this contradicts the 0.70 s flame cap. The design wants a cooling arc; the cap forbids it.
Why it is there. Owner ruling to prevent "rolling fire" and keep the visual noise bounded. A real reason: too many persistent fires would clutter the board and cost frame time.
The loosening you propose. (a) Keep the 0.70 s cap for open flame (the bright, flickering, yellow-white component). (b) Add a separate "ember/coal" class: a low-frequency glow (no flicker, no bright white) that ramps from orange to deep red over 3-6 s and fades over a further 4-8 s. This is not "rolling fire"; it is a cooling surface. It obeys the letter of the cap (no flame, no hot core at full heat) while delivering the cooling arc the design already describes. (c) Apply the ember class to: wreck torn faces (the "glowing white, then orange, then dull red, then dark" sequence), torpedo wound craters ("glows and cools over about four seconds"), and the capital's "coals cool for about 14 s." (d) Cap active embers at 6 per scene to bound fill-rate.
Size of the job. Small (one run: one new shader variant for the ember glow, applied to existing decal quads). Frame-time: 6 quads with a simple colour-ramp shader is negligible.
Picture to point at. docs/shots/look_bench/deaths/capital_death_beats.jpg (the wreck beats, where the cooling arc should be visible but is not).
Finding 10 - Sound: most cues have one voice, no size scaling, no distance attenuation
What the player hears now. From cues.json: gun_flak has one layer (gunFlak) and "synth": [] - one sound, no variants. gun_tracer: one layer, no synth variants. gun_spine: one layer, no synth. gun_glob: one layer, no synth. hit_wet: one layer, no synth. hit_glass: one layer, no synth. patter_hull: one layer, no synth. patter_shield: one layer, no synth. engine: one layer (engineLoop), no synth. cough: one layer, no synth. puff: one layer, no synth. Twelve cues are a single sound file played identically every time. No cue in the file has a distance-attenuation parameter or a size/pitch scaling tied to the ship class firing or being hit.
What a film would do instead. In Star Wars, every blaster bolt has a slightly different pitch; a Star Destroyer's turbolasers are an octave below an X-wing's. In The Expanse, the Rocinante's PDCs sound different from the Donnager's railguns not just in volume but in register. In Dunkirk (2017), the Stuka siren's pitch changes with dive angle. In EVE Online, weapon sounds attenuate with distance and shift with ship size. Sound design rule: the same event heard twice must differ in pitch, timbre or spatial position.
Where the limit lives. cues.json: the "synth": [] entries (gun_flak, gun_tracer, gun_spine, gun_glob, hit_wet, hit_glass, patter_hull, patter_shield, engine, cough, puff, torp_rocket, torp_sac). The "pj" (pitch jitter) field exists but is small: 0.03 for most weapons (±3 %), 0.06 for salvo_tail. No field for distance attenuation or class-based pitch offset. sound.md says "At least four takes of anything heard often, never the same one twice in a row" - but twelve cues have only one take.
Why it is there. Single-voice cues are the first pass. The sound playbook (sound.md) is explicitly a "first cut" and the sourcing job (Muse's palette study) has not landed yet. The gap is known and scheduled.
The loosening you propose. (a) Every cue with "synth": [] gets at least three baked variants (the sound.md rule says four for frequent sounds; three is the minimum for infrequent ones like cough or puff). (b) Add a "class_pitch" field: frigate fires at +10 % rate, destroyer +5 %, cruiser 0 %, capital −8 %, tomb-ark −15 %. The existing "r" (rate) field in the layer can be multiplied by this at trigger time. (c) Add a "dist_atten" field: volume falls 6 dB per doubling of distance from the listener's focus ship. At 4+ hexes, the sound is a muffled thump. (d) The "pj" pitch jitter should be raised from 0.03 to 0.07 for all weapons to match the existing "spj" (spatial jitter) of 0.07.

Finding 15 - Sound: the max and gap fields enforce a tidy, uncrowded mix

MOVEMENT SECTION
How the best film, TV and game space battles show weight and vacuum in movement
The Expanse (TV, 2015-2022). The reference for Newtonian movement in space. Ships burn their main engine to accelerate along the nose, then cut it and coast. To stop, they flip 180° and burn retro. Thruster puffs are visible at the bow and stern for attitude changes. A ship can drift sideways while pointing forward. The Rocinante's flips are the signature move: the ship rotates end-over-end, fires the main engine to decelerate, then rotates back. Capital ships (the Donnager) take visibly longer to start, turn and stop. There is no banking into turns; a turn is a rotation, not a lean. The engine plume is bright only during a burn and dark while coasting.
Battlestar Galactica (TV, 2004-2009). Weight is shown by inertia: the Galactica drifts, its hull creaks, and small corrections fire visible thrusters. Fighters (Vipers, Raiders) do bank - the show treats them as closer to aircraft - but the capital ships do not. The Galactica's course changes are slow, multi-minute affairs announced by alarm and thruster fire. The device: the bigger the ship, the longer the burn, the more visible the thruster corrections, the more the crew feels the acceleration.
