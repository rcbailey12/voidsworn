# Making space feel 3D, without giving up turn-based hexes

Reviewer: Muse. Basis commit: 99345bc (2026-10-22). Written at the owner's request: find ways for the game to feel truly three-dimensional and space-like, while keeping the turn-based approach.

Method: this is a design proposal, not a bug review. Claims about the current code are marked OBSERVED and were checked for this file in battle.js (movement and gravity), core.js (arcs and shield facings) and scene3d.js (the rendered board). Design claims are marked INFERRED or stated as proposals. Quotes are exact and under 20 words.

Correction, Oct 23 (owner): the first version of this file gave Chorus hulls a sinking rule ("a swarm that must keep swimming") and described Hegemony dives as falling. Both were naval thinking in a game that is explicitly space, not sea; the owner's standing rule on this predates the file. The Chorus and Hegemony bends are rewritten below in delta-v terms, a framing section ("Bands are delta-v" and "How the 2D hex plane survives") is added, and sinking now exists only inside real gravity wells, where it belongs.

## Part 1: where the game is flat today (OBSERVED)

- **Position is two numbers.** A unit lives at (q, r) on an axial hex grid with a facing in the plane. There is no altitude field on a unit anywhere in the movement, firing or AI code read for the earlier reviews.
- **Shields are a compass, not a sphere.** core.js resolves every hit into one of four facings: `const QUAD_NAME = ['prow','starboard','rear','port'];`. Nothing can hit a ship from above or below, because above and below do not exist.
- **Weapon arcs are flat too.** inArc in core.js tests a single relative angle in the plane (prow 120 degrees, broadsides to the sides, rear). A lance cannot plunge, a broadside cannot fire down.
- **Verticality exists only as a comment on a movement discount.** The thruster hop lets a ship pass an occupied or blocked hex at +1 movement, and the code describes it in 3D words it does not model: battle.js:388, "and can climb over or dive under a rock, wreck or enemy ship it cannot stop on", and battle.js:412, `// climb over terrain, dive under ships`. The ship ends the hop exactly as flat as it started. The fiction is already written; the state is not.
- **Gravity pulls sideways.** The dead star's gravity well (battle.js:270) gives a slingshot bonus, drags ships toward itself in the plane at turn end and deals tidal damage. It is the most space-like system in the game and it operates entirely in 2D.
- **The render agrees with the rules.** The board is discs on one plane (scene3d.js:265) and the camera's anchor math zeroes the vertical axis (`d.y=0`, scene3d.js:371). The ships are 3D models flying over a 2D board, which is why battles read as tabletop: handsome, but tabletop.

None of this is a bug. Flat is the genre default: Battlefleet Gothic, Star Wars Armada and Battlestar Galactica Deadlock all fight on a plane. But the owner is right that it leaves the setting's best toy unplayed with. Space is the one wargame theatre where "up" is real, and this game already has a 3D engine, a free camera and ships modelled on every side.

## Part 2: the core proposal: three altitude bands

One new piece of state per ship, `u.band`, in {low, mid, high}. Everything else is rules hung off that one field. Three bands, not continuous height and not five: three is the most a player can hold in their head on a phone screen, it matches the game's existing taste for small numbers (three sectors, three reward steps, Commands at power 1/2/3), and it is how the one successful turn-based space game with real height did it (Star Hammer's altitude levels). The cautionary counterexample is the Saganami Island Tactical Simulator, the Honor Harrington board game, which does true 3D hexes honestly and is famously a spreadsheet with dice. Bands keep the soul of that idea at a tenth of the bookkeeping.

Two framing rules keep this honest space physics instead of sea physics with the serial numbers filed off (owner correction, Oct 23: space is not naval, and nothing in this proposal may rely on water logic):

- **Bands are delta-v, not altitude in a gravity field.** Low, mid and high are offsets from the battle's reference plane, nothing more. In open space nothing sinks, drifts down, floats or falls: changing band is a vector change, it costs movement because delta-v is the real currency of spaceflight, and a ship that stops paying stays exactly where it is, forever. The only places anything falls are inside actual gravity: the dead star's well and a planet's floor (Part 2, gravity), which is what makes those maps special.
- **Facing, band and roll are the whole attitude.** A Star Trek ship angles: it yaws, pitches and rolls continuously, and a player's instinct is to want that. Quantized, it is already three-quarters in this game: yaw is the existing 6-step facing, pitch becomes the 3-step band (which offset plane you fight from, and whether your guns bear down or up), and roll is the Roll maneuver below. Six yaws times three bands times two roll states is a rich attitude space a phone player can still read at a glance. Continuous angles would break the hex math, the AI's pathfinding and the sims for a marginal gain in feel; the quantized version captures the decisions that matter (dive on the dorsal, climb to shoot over a screen, roll to present a fresh shield) at a fraction of the cost.

### How the 2D hex plane survives

It survives intact, as the chart. The hex grid is the reference plane of the battle (call it the ecliptic): every existing system that keys off (q, r) keeps doing so, unchanged. Range is still hex distance. Arcs are still tested in the plane. What the band adds is a second, tiny coordinate read after the hex: which of the three offset planes parallel to the chart the ship currently occupies. Visually the fleet floats on three glass shelves above one map, each ship tied to its hex by its drop line (Part 2, presentation); the hex is the address, the band is the shelf. Cross-band interaction is deliberately coarse so no new range table appears: weapons fire across one band step with the plunging/falloff modifiers below; two steps of separation (low to high) is out of arc for everything except lances and torpedoes, which is what gives altitude its tactical meaning (a carrier parked high is genuinely hard to reach, and genuinely blind to the low fight). Nothing stacks, nothing hides, and a player who ignores bands entirely is playing the current game with slightly worse firing solutions, which is the correct failure mode for an added dimension.

### Movement: climb and dive are the new turns

- Changing band costs 1 movement per band, paid from the same pool as hexes and 60-degree turns, and does not change facing. A fast frigate can dive two bands and still move; a battleship with speed 2 must choose between climbing and fighting, which is correct: mass should feel heavy in three dimensions too.
- The existing thruster hop becomes literal: passing over a ship or rock now means ending the move in a different band than you crossed it in, or paying the old +1 to pass through its band. The help text stops being a lie.
- Faction bends, one line each in MOVE_RULES beside the existing six, each expressed as delta-v economics, never buoyancy: Ithar fan sails change band free once per round (sails re-vector without spending reaction mass; offset planes are their home). Chorus hulls thrust from a single metabolic drive organ, so re-vectoring is clumsy: a band change costs +1, but the lunge carries them, and a Chorus ship that changes band also gains +1 hex of movement along its facing. Deathless phase drive ignores band for movement cost entirely. Hegemony vector thrusters tie into their existing retro bend: a Hegemony ship that backs off may change band as part of the same burn, free (their thruster layout is built for reversing and re-vectoring, not for falling; there is no falling). Corsair bolted burners are oversized and under-governed: changing band is free, but a ship that changed band this activation fires at -10 accuracy, because an ungoverned burn shakes the firing solution loose. Voidsworn broadside doctrine gains a rider: raking fire delivered from the band above counts as a rake.

### Shields and arcs: the sphere arrives

- Two new shield facings, dorsal and ventral, resolved exactly like the existing four: an attack from a higher band hits dorsal, from a lower band hits ventral, same band resolves in the plane as today. Shield strength splits six ways instead of four on paper, so per-facing values drop; big ships get a deliberately thin dorsal (their guns and sensors crowd the top) and a thick ventral plate, frigates stay near-even. Suddenly the correct way to kill a battleship includes getting above it, which no flat game can say.
- Weapon arcs stay in-plane, with one modifier: a weapon firing down a band gets +1 effective range band-step (plunging fire reaches), firing up loses accuracy past optimal range (the existing falloff stat is the natural hook). Torpedoes dive: a torpedo fired from above arrives on the dorsal facing and ignores part of dorsal armour, which finally gives the torpedo's evMult stat a spatial meaning.
- **The Roll.** Once per activation, a ship may spend a turn allowance to roll 180 degrees: port and starboard facings swap, dorsal and ventral facings swap, prow and rear unchanged. Rolling brings a fresh shield face to the enemy and turns your guns' arcs with you (port broadside now points where starboard did, which the arc system already knows how to test). Battlestar Galactica's most famous maneuver is rolling the ship to spread damage across armour; The Expanse's flip-and-burn is the same instinct. No flat tactics game offers it because flat games have nothing to swap. Voidsworn would, and it costs the player a real resource (the turn they needed to reposition), so it is a decision, not a button.

### Blocking, terrain and gravity

- Ships block line of sight and fire only within their own band. A screen of frigates at mid cannot body-block for a carrier hiding high; a rock occupies the bands its height says (the asteroid models already vary in height; give each rock hex a height of 1 or 2 bands as terrain data). Nebula and storm hexes become columns (all bands) or layers (one band) per map, which makes the existing terrain kinds feel different from each other for the first time: a storm you can fly under is a different problem from a storm you cannot.
- The gravity well goes vertical, and it is the only place in the game where anything sinks, because it is the only place with something to sink toward: within its rim, at round end a ship drops one band as well as being dragged in the plane, and a ship dragged into the low band inside the rim takes the tidal damage that exists today. Slingshot becomes literal: crossing the well from high to low grants the +1 movement it already grants, now with a direction a player can picture.
- Planet and station maps gain a floor, for the same reason: the low band beside a planet is inside its gravity well, so leaving it costs an extra movement of delta-v (climbing out of a well is real work; entering costs nothing, wells are easy to fall into and expensive to leave, which is orbital mechanics in one rule). Holding station there is free for the objective ship, and in Hold the Station the station can only be occupied or attacked from low and mid. Breakthrough exits can be taken from any band, but the exit hexes at high are fewer (the polar door is narrower).

### Squadrons: the sphere's natives

Wings are the units that should love 3D most. A wing lives in its target's band when striking and its carrier's band when guarding; fighters may change band freely (they are small, it is the entire point of them), bombers may not (one band change per activation, like ships). Point defence becomes a sphere with a seam: flak fires within its band at full effect and one band off at half (the vsSq stat splits cleanly), so a bomber diving from high through a fighter screen at mid onto a dorsal facing is a three-layer problem the defender can actually answer at each layer. This also deepens the carrier game the enemy-AI review worried about, without adding a single new unit type.

### Presentation: where 3D is won or lost

Rules alone will not sell it; readability will. Four presentation rules, all buildable in the current scene:

1. **Drop lines.** Every ship gets a faint vertical line down to the mid plane and a small hex shadow-disc in its own band's colour on the plane below (Homeworld's sensor manager solved exactly this problem this way). At a glance, the board reads as three stacked boards.
2. **Band chevrons on the nameplate.** The nameplate system already rebuilds per ship; add one to three chevrons (low/mid/high) next to the hull pips. On a 412 px phone this is the difference between a feature and a rumor.
3. **Tilt on transition.** A ship that climbs pitches its prow up for the move, dives prow-down, and a rolling ship visibly rolls (the ship roots already rotate for facing; band and roll are two more rotations on the same node). Movement should look like flying, not sliding.
4. **A "look from the band" camera key.** One tap frames the camera level with the selected ship's band; the existing orbit camera already allows the angle, it just never has a reason to go there.

## Part 3: phasing (INFERRED effort)

- **Phase 1: state and sight.** Add u.band, climb/dive movement, band display (drop lines, chevrons, tilt), same-band-only blocking, and the AI's simple band policy (match the target's band when striking; carriers sit high; brawlers sit mid). No shield or arc changes: attacks across bands resolve in-plane as today, with only the torpedo dive and plunging range modifier as a taste. This phase alone changes how battles look and move. Effort: the largest single chunk is the AI's pathfinding, because pathField's BFS gains a band axis; keeping band changes out of the BFS (plan in-plane, then spend leftover movement on band changes by heuristic) bounds it.
- **Phase 2: the sphere.** Dorsal and ventral facings, the Roll, band-aware arcs and flak seams, rock heights. This is the phase that makes veterans relearn the game, in the good way. Balance pass required on shield splits and BOSS_SCALE, since bosses gain two facings to defend.
- **Phase 3: the theatre.** Vertical gravity, planet floors, band-gated objectives, layered terrain. Content work on maps and encounters rather than engine work; each encounter type gets one band twist, not a rewrite.

Keep a Flat toggle in SETUP beside the existing turn-order toggle, defaulting to bands on. The game already ships a classic/alternating rules switch, so a spatial rules switch has precedent, and it protects every existing sim, balance table and player habit while the new geometry beds in.

## Part 4: visibility, clarity, usability: the confusion audit

The owner asked the right hostile question (Oct 23): yes, it is just three planes, high, mid and low, played over one hex map. So where exactly does that confuse a player, and what design rule kills each confusion before it ships? The framing sentence the tutorial should teach is one line long: "You still play on the hex map. Each ship also stands on one of three floors. Changing floors costs movement." Everything below defends that sentence. Two pieces of good news from the code first (OBSERVED):

- **The renderer already takes a height.** scene3d.js:7: `function hexWorld(q,r,y=0){ return new V3(HW*(q+r/2)+G.ox, y, -1.5*HS*r+G.oz); }`. Every board disc and ship position in the game is placed through this function with y left at 0. Bands are, mechanically, the first callers that pass a real y: three shelf heights into a parameter that already exists.
- **Picking is already two-step, and already fights this fight.** onHover in ui3d.js first raycasts the actual 3D hulls (scene.pick against meshes carrying unit metadata, ui3d.js:363), then falls back to a ground ray intersected with a plane (`k=-ray.origin.y/ray.direction.y`). It even carries a disambiguation rule whose comment reads "a reachable hex beside the selected ship beats the ship's own tall hull". Band picking is the same code with the plane's height swapped for the active band's height; hull picking does not change at all, because a ray hits a 3D hull wherever it floats.

Now the failure modes, in the order a player meets them:

**1. "Which ship did I just tap?"** Two ships share a hex column on different floors. Rule: taps hit hulls, not columns (true today), and where a ray passes two hulls, the ship on the player's active floor wins; tapping the same column again cycles down through its ships, highest first. The selected ship's floor becomes the active floor, so selection and viewpoint never disagree.

**2. "Where can I move?" painted on three floors at once.** This is the big one, and the answer is architectural: never show three floors of movement at once. The reachable-hex overlay paints only the selected ship's current floor, exactly as today; the other floors show nothing but the faint column dots under ships. Changing floor is not a destination in the path preview at all. It is an order, two buttons (Climb, Dive) in the orders row beside Brace and the ability button, costing movement from the ship's remaining pool. You move on a plane, then step floors with a button, then the preview repaints on the new floor. One floor at a time means the screen is never more complex than the current game's, and it has a second benefit: the reach system stays keyed by hex as it is today (bestFacingReach stores one best state per hex key, ui3d.js), instead of tripling its state space. Pathfinding never leaves the plane; altitude is a decision, not a route.

**3. "Which floor is that ship on?" from a bad camera angle.** In a near-top-down view three floors project onto one and the dimension vanishes. Four redundant channels, any two of which survive any angle: the ship's actual height, its drop line to the chart, its shadow disc drawn on the chart under its hex, and band chevrons (one, two or three) on its plate. Plus a camera rule: in battle the orbit clamps short of fully top-down (roughly 25 degrees off vertical at most), so height always buys screen separation; the showcase and attract cameras stay free. Band separation in world units should be about a hex width, far enough that silhouettes never merge at the clamp angle on a 412 px screen.

**4. "What hit my dorsal?"** Six shield facings is two too many to track in the ring, and the ring cannot show them anyway: shields today are four arcs merged into the ring around the hull (fx3d.js:13-14, makeShieldRing). Rule: the four in-plane arcs stay on the ring; dorsal and ventral become two diamond pips inside the ring, top and bottom, coloured by strength like the arcs. In the ship panel, one octahedron glyph carries the whole sphere: a diamond with six labelled faces (dorsal on top, ventral underneath, prow, port, starboard, rear around) is the one icon that maps 1:1 onto the model with no legend to memorize. And every band-relevant event speaks: damage floaters and log lines name the facing and the geometry ("dorsal hit, plunging fire"), extending the facing names the game already prints, never colour alone.

**5. Cross-band geometry the player would have to compute.** "Can that cruiser two floors up hit me?" must never be a mental calculation. Rule: selecting your ship rings every enemy that can fire on it, as enemies in range are already ringed today, with the ring style distinguishing level fire from plunging or climbing fire. The geometry engine answers; the player reads. The same applies in reverse when targeting: a target two floors away simply shows out of arc for weapons that cannot reach it, in the existing red-ring language.

**6. The enemy turn becomes unreadable.** Eight enemy ships changing floors while the camera follows is noise. Mitigations: changing floor costs delta-v, so it is naturally rare (most activations change floor zero or one times); the AI in phase 1 changes floor at most once per activation; and a floor change is staged as an event, with the follow camera taking the beat, a "climbing" or "diving" floater over the ship, and a pitch in its engine note if sound allows. Rare, announced, and visible is the opposite of confusing.

**7. Orders row crowding on a phone.** Climb and Dive are two more buttons in a panel that already carries move, fire, ability, brace, ram, board and Command. Rules: they live as one stacked pair of small chevron buttons at the edge of the orders grid (44 px targets, per the UI review's touch findings), they grey out with their cost when unaffordable like every other order, and on desktop W and S mirror Q and E's turn keys. No new panel, no mode switch, no long-press.

**8. Deployment and setup.** Skip it in phase 1: every ship enters at mid, and floor is purely a mid-battle decision. One less screen to teach, one less pre-battle optimization for new players to drown in. If veterans later want deployment floors, it slots into the existing deployment step without touching anything above.

Residual risks, honestly stated (INFERRED): the follow camera's framing (ctl.establish targets y=0 today) must learn the fleet's floor spread or high ships will drift out of frame at battle start; the threat map and intent displays gain a band dimension and need the same one-floor-at-a-time discipline or they will contradict rule 2; and the teaching load is real, three tutorial tips in the first banded battle (floors exist, changing floors costs movement, get above a battleship) is the minimum, using the tip machinery the game already has. None of these are reasons not to build it; all of them are reasons the phasing in Part 3 puts presentation (Phase 1) before geometry (Phase 2).

## What not to do

- **Not true continuous 3D.** No free Y coordinate, no cube-corner hexes, no 26-neighbour grids. Every game that tried honest 3D at tactics scale became a specialist instrument (Attack Vector: Tactical is a masterpiece almost nobody can play). Bands are the 80/20.
- **Not more than three bands.** A fourth band doubles the "where is everything" problem for near-zero decisions; the interesting choices are above / level / below, which is three by definition.
- **Not vertical stacking of ships in one hex across bands as a hiding place.** Ships in the same hex in different bands must still see and shoot each other (it is the same column of space); if different-band hexmates cannot interact, the optimal play becomes camping invisibly, and the game dies of politeness.
- **Not as a mid-run patch.** This changes pathfinding, LOS, shields and the sim harness at once. It is a headline feature for a major version, with the sims (the ALT drivers in sim3d.js) extended to band-aware policies before any balance claim is made about it.

## The pick

Ship the package of **bands + dorsal/ventral facings + the Roll**, phased as above. Of everything in this file, the Roll is the signature: it is the one maneuver that is impossible in a flat game, trivially explainable in one sentence ("roll the ship to face a fresh shield side"), visible on screen, and steeped in the space-opera sources this game's factions are drawn from. If only one piece survives contact with the schedule, keep three bands for movement and blocking (Phase 1) plus the Roll, and let the full shield sphere wait: even that much turns Voidsworn from a space game on a board into a game that is about space.
