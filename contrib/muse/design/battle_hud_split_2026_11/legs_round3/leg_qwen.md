1. Silhouette Construction
    
    All five silhouettes share a common grammar: side profile facing right, drawn as a single closed fill path, no interior line work at 16 px, one or two interior cut lines permitted at 40 px. The fill is the ink colour. At 40 px the silhouette sits in the integrity unit as the anchor glyph; at 16 px it lives in the fleet strip. Both readings come from the same vector path, just rasterised at different scales, so the builder authors one shape and exports two sizes.
    
    Frigate. Overall proportion: 5 units long by 1 unit tall. Bow is a sharp wedge, roughly the front fifth of the length, tapering to a point. The hull line runs flat and low. Stern is a clean vertical drop into a small single engine nozzle, drawn as a shallow notch cut into the rear. Spine: none raised; the deck line is one unbroken gentle curve from bow tip to stern. Signature feature: a single forward-swept sensor fin on the dorsal side, about one-fifth back from the bow, rising to maybe a third of the hull height. At 16 px the fin merges into the top contour and the shape reads as a slim dart. At 40 px the fin is distinct and the engine notch is visible.
    
    Destroyer. Proportion: 5.5 units long by 1.3 units tall. Bow is angular but blunter than the frigate, a faceted chisel rather than a needle. The hull has a visible step: the forward third sits slightly lower than the aft two-thirds, giving a stepped-deck profile. Stern carries a twin engine block, two square nozzles side by side, which at 40 px read as two small rectangles protruding aft. Spine: a short raised section amidships, about one unit long, suggesting a weapons housing. Signature feature: a forward gun mount bump on the stepped deck, a small trapezoid that breaks the top line. At 16 px the step and the twin engines give it a chunkier, more squared-off read than the frigate. The gun bump disappears.
    
    Cruiser (Vigil class, the reference ship). Proportion: 7 units long by 1.6 units tall. This is the long hull Ryan named. Bow tapers over the front quarter into a clean raked stem, not as sharp as the frigate but more drawn out. The hull runs long and low through the middle third. Stern is broad and flat, dominated by the engine block: three nozzles in a row, the centre one slightly larger, housed in a squared-off transom. Spine: a raised dorsal ridge running from roughly the one-third mark to the two-thirds mark, about half a unit tall, giving the ship a distinct humpbacked profile. Signature feature: a command tower at the forward end of the spine, a small vertical rectangle with a slight forward lean, breaking the spine line. At 16 px the long low hull, the spine hump, and the broad stern are the three reads. The tower and individual nozzles vanish. At 40 px all of it is legible.
    
    Carrier. Proportion: 6.5 units long by 2.2 units tall, the tallest class. Bow is bluff and slightly rounded, almost vertical for the first tenth of the length. The defining feature is the flight deck: the top line is flat and continuous for the middle three-fifths of the ship, sitting well above the waterline equivalent, supported by visible hull sides below. Stern is squared with a single large engine block. Spine: absent in the traditional sense; instead the flat deck is the dominant horizontal. Signature feature: a small island superstructure, a narrow vertical block sitting on the deck about two-thirds aft, rising another half unit above the deck line. At 16 px the carrier reads as a tall flat-topped slab with a tiny bump aft. That slab shape is unmistakable against the other four. At 40 px the island, the deck overhang, and the engine block are all clear.
    
    Capital hull. Proportion: 8 units long by 2.5 units tall, the largest. Bow is a layered prow: two or three stacked plates stepping forward, giving a terraced front. The hull is deep and carries a visible belt line (a horizontal groove) at 40 px only. Stern is massive, four engine nozzles in a broad transom. Spine: a long raised superstructure running more than half the ship's length, with two distinct tiers, the lower tier wider than the upper. Signature feature: a forward command spire at the bow end of the superstructure, a thin vertical element rising above everything else, roughly one unit tall. At 16 px the capital reads as the biggest, tallest blob with a flat layered front and a long top ridge. The spire, the belt line, and individual engines drop out. The sheer mass and the terraced bow distinguish it from the carrier.
    
    State handling for silhouettes. The silhouette is a filled shape, so state maps to fill treatment. Full readiness: solid off-white ink fill. Damage: the fill remains off-white but a muted damage colour (a desaturated rust, already in the palette) creeps in from the stern forward as a second path layered under the main fill, clipped to the hull outline. The damage path grows with hull loss. This keeps the colour discipline because the damage colour is already sanctioned and the silhouette shape does not change. The acting ship: the off-white fill stays, and a 2 px ember orange underline appears beneath the silhouette, matching the ember underline grammar from round 2. The silhouette itself never turns ember. Unavailable or destroyed: the fill drops to roughly one-third opacity, same shape, no additional marks. At 16 px in the fleet strip, the damage creep simplifies to a two-tone split (off-white forward, damage colour aft) because fine gradation would mush. At 40 px in the integrity unit, the creep can be a smoother boundary.
    
    2. Upgraded Glyph Constructions
    
    The upgrade principle: each glyph keeps its round 2 base shape as the skeleton, then gains one signature detail that ties it to a specific physical object in the Voidsworn fiction, and a secondary interior detail that gives the eye something to grip at 32 px without cluttering the 20 px read. All line work is flat vector, single stroke weight for the primary contour (2 px at 32 px design size) with an optional secondary weight (1 px) for interior detail. Square line caps, square joins. No rounded terminals. No gradients. All glyphs sit on the dark veil with no background shape.
    
    Weapon Glyphs (20 px design size)
    
    Battery (kinetic broadside). Round 2 placeholder: three parallel lines. Upgrade: keep the three lines but angle them at roughly 15 degrees from horizontal, converging slightly toward the right, suggesting a firing solution. Each line gains a small perpendicular tick at the left end, like a breech block, so they read as barrels rather than abstract strokes. Signature detail: a short recoil gap. The middle line is set back by 2 px from the outer two, implying a firing sequence. Interior detail at 32 px: a faint (1 px) horizontal baseline connecting the three breech ticks, grounding them as a single weapon mount. At 20 px the baseline drops. The three angled lines with ticks are the glyph.
    
    Lance (beam weapon). Round 2 placeholder: a bare triangle. Upgrade: the triangle remains but becomes elongated and narrow, more like a blade profile than an equilateral shape. The left edge (the base) is a straight vertical line. The top and bottom edges sweep right to a point. Signature detail: a single interior line running from the base midpoint to the tip, splitting the triangle into two halves, suggesting a focusing channel. At 32 px, add a small circle (2 px diameter) at the base midpoint, the emitter lens. At 20 px the circle drops and the glyph is the split blade. Two-weight: the outer contour at 2 px, the interior split line at 1 px.
    
    Torpedo. Round 2 had no specific torpedo glyph described, but it is in the working set. Construction: a horizontal elongated hexagon (six-sided, but stretched so it reads as a cigar with a pointed nose and a flat tail). The nose points right. Signature detail: two small fins at the tail, drawn as short diagonal strokes angling up and down from the flat rear edge, giving a cruciform tail read. Interior detail at 32 px: a single 1 px band across the body about one-third from the nose, suggesting a warhead casing seam. At 20 px the band drops. The hexagonal cigar with tail fins is the glyph.
    
    Point Defence. Round 2 placeholder: not explicitly drawn but implied as a small gun. Construction: a short thick vertical stroke (the barrel) rising from a small square base (the mount). Off the tip of the barrel, three short lines radiate upward in a shallow fan, suggesting an interception burst. Signature detail: the base square has a small notch cut from its bottom edge, implying a pivot or hinge. At 32 px, add a tiny 1 px arc above the fan lines, suggesting a tracking radar sweep. At 20 px the arc drops. The vertical stroke, the base, and the three-line fan are the glyph.
    
    Integrity Glyphs (12 to 20 px design size)
    
    Hull (keel plate). Round 2: a keel plate shape. Upgrade: the base shape is a wide, low trapezoid, broader at the bottom than the top, with the top edge slightly convex (a single gentle curve, not an arc segment). This reads as a cross-section of a hull plate. Signature detail: a single rivet line. Three small dots (1.5 px each at 20 px, dropping at 12 px) run horizontally across the middle of the trapezoid. Interior detail at 20 px: a 1 px horizontal line near the bottom edge, suggesting a weld seam or structural rib. At 12 px the dots and seam both drop and the glyph is just the trapezoid with the convex top. The convex top is the signature; it keeps this from being a generic rectangle.
    
    Shield. Round 2: an empty hexagon. Upgrade: keep the hexagon but orient it flat-side-down (a wide hexagon, not a pointy-topped one). The contour is the full 2 px stroke. Signature detail: a smaller concentric hexagon inside, at 1 px stroke, inset by about 3 px at 20 px scale. This inner hex suggests a layered shield field. At 12 px the inner hex drops and the glyph is a single hexagon outline. The shield glyph is the only glyph that takes glacier blue as its stroke colour when representing the shield readout in the integrity unit. In all other contexts (order references, log) it stays off-white. Two-weight: outer hex at 2 px, inner hex at 1 px.
    
    Order Glyphs (32 px design size)
    
    Brace. Round 2: bare glyph plus label. Upgrade: a chevron pointing downward, thick and wide, like a brace or a shield wall seen from above. The chevron arms are straight, meeting at a blunt point (not sharp; the vertex is truncated by 2 px to give a flat tip). Signature detail: a short horizontal line sits just below the chevron's vertex, 4 px wide, suggesting the deck or the line being held. Interior detail: at 32 px, two small vertical ticks at the outer ends of the chevron arms, suggesting locked supports. At 20 px (if the order glyph ever scales down for a tooltip or log) the ticks drop. Two-weight: chevron at 2.5 px, the underline and ticks at 1.5 px.
    
    Focus Fire (order). Construction: three short lines converging from the left toward a single point on the right, like sight lines or vectors meeting on a target. The point is marked by a small filled square (3 px). Signature detail: the middle line is slightly longer than the top and bottom lines, and the three lines are not evenly spaced; they bunch slightly toward the centre, giving a sense of tightening. Interior detail: none needed; the convergence is the detail. At 20 px the filled square shrinks to 2 px but the convergence still reads. Single weight, 2 px.
    
    Turn. Construction: a curved arrow. The arc sweeps about 270 degrees, drawn as a single stroke, with the arrowhead at the terminal end. The arrowhead is a simple open chevron (two short strokes), not a filled triangle. Signature detail: at the centre of the arc's implied circle, a small dot (2 px), suggesting the pivot point of the turn. Interior detail: a second, fainter (1 px, 50 percent opacity of the ink) arc just inside the first, offset by 2 px, suggesting the wake of the turn. At 20 px the inner arc drops. The outer arc, arrowhead, and centre dot remain.
    
    Fire for Effect. Construction: a horizontal line (the firing line) with three short vertical strokes rising from it, spaced evenly, like a stylised volley. Above the three strokes, a single wider chevron pointing upward, suggesting the concentrated burst going out. Signature detail: the three vertical strokes are not identical heights. The centre one is tallest, the outer two shorter, giving a peaked volley shape. Interior detail: at 32 px, a small horizontal tick at the base of each vertical stroke where it meets the firing line, suggesting a muzzle. At 20 px the ticks drop. Two-weight: firing line and verticals at 2 px, the upper chevron at 1.5 px.
    
    End Ship. Construction: a vertical bar (the ship's station) with a horizontal line crossing it near the top, forming a T shape. Below the crossbar, a small downward-pointing chevron sits on the vertical bar, suggesting finality or closure. Signature detail: the left end of the crossbar terminates in a small square cap (3 px), while the right end is open, implying the ship is locked in but the order radiates outward. Interior detail: none. The T with the downward chevron and the capped end is specific enough. Single weight, 2.5 px for the T, 2 px for the chevron.
    
    Command (star). Round 2: a star. Upgrade: keep the star but make it a four-pointed star (a compass rose shape) rather than a five-pointed one, to avoid generic decoration. The four points are long and narrow, the cardinal points slightly longer than the diagonals if a second set is added, but the base is four points only. Signature detail: a small circle (3 px diameter) at the centre of the star, hollow, suggesting the commanding officer or the nexus. Interior detail: at 32 px, a 1 px crosshair through the centre circle, extending just beyond the circle's edge, reinforcing the command-and-control read. At 20 px the crosshair drops. The four-pointed star with the centre circle is the glyph. Two-weight: star points at 2 px, centre circle and crosshair at 1 px.
    
    3. The Detail Budget
    
    The rule a builder follows: every glyph is authored in three layers, and the layers are stripped in order as size falls.
    
    Layer 1, the skeleton. The base contour shape. The silhouette outline, the triangle, the hexagon, the chevron. This layer must carry the full meaning of the glyph by itself. If the skeleton does not read, the glyph fails. This layer survives all the way down to 12 px.
    
    Layer 2, the signature detail. The one element that makes this glyph specific rather than generic: the recoil gap in the battery, the split line in the lance, the tail fins on the torpedo, the rivet dots on the hull plate, the centre dot in the turn arc. This layer is present at 20 px and above. It drops at 16 px if the glyph appears at that size (fleet strip context) and is absent at 12 px.
    
    Layer 3, the interior texture. Weld seams, tracking arcs, crosshairs, muzzle ticks, secondary inner contours. This layer exists only at 32 px. It drops at 20 px. It is never drawn at 16 px or 12 px.
    
    The builder's test: render the glyph at 12 px on a dark background. If you cannot name the glyph in under two seconds, the skeleton is wrong and no amount of layer 2 or 3 detail will save it. Redesign the skeleton. Then render at 20 px. The signature detail should be visible but not crowded. Then render at 32 px. The interior texture should add richness without competing with the signature.
    
    A secondary rule: no detail element may be smaller than 2 px at the glyph's design size. If a detail would be a 1 px mark at 20 px, it belongs in layer 3 and only appears at 32 px. This prevents sub-pixel mush.
    
    For silhouettes specifically: at 16 px, only the outer contour matters. The builder should check the fleet strip by rendering all five silhouettes at 16 px in a row. Each must be identifiable by its top-line profile and overall mass distribution alone. If two silhouettes look alike at 16 px, one of them needs a proportion change, not a detail addition.
    
    4. What I Would Refuse
    
    Faction chrome. The reference games lean heavily on faction identity in their icon work. Voidsworn's discipline is a single unified ink language. I would not introduce faction-specific border treatments, heraldic shapes, or ornamental scrollwork around glyphs. The glyphs serve the HUD, not a faction identity sheet.
    
    Skeuomorphic metal. No brushed-steel textures on the hull plate glyph. No glowing plasma cores inside the lance. No bolt heads or rivet shading on the battery. The line style is flat and graphic. Texture is the enemy of readability at 12 px and it breaks the veil discipline.
    
    Gradients inside glyphs. Already banned by the standing grammar, but worth restating because the reference games use them heavily. No radial glow on the shield hex. No linear fade on the lance blade. Flat ink or nothing.
    
    Tiny text inside glyphs. No letter abbreviations inside the command star. No numeric damage values baked into the hull plate. Text lives in the label beside the glyph, never inside it. At 12 px any interior text is a smear.
    
    Tile or background dependency. No glyph may require a coloured square, a rounded rectangle, or a border to be legible. If a glyph only reads because it sits on a lighter tile, the glyph is broken. Everything sits on the dark veil directly.
    
    Animated glyph states as part of the base art. The cooldown arc, the ember underline, the opacity dim: these are state treatments applied by the HUD code, not baked into the glyph artwork. The glyph file is one static shape per glyph. State is composited at runtime.
    
    Silhouette interior detail at 16 px. I would refuse any request to put deck lines, window dots, or weapon hardpoints inside the fleet strip silhouettes. At 16 px those become noise. The silhouette is a filled shape and nothing else at that size.
    
    5. Honest Costs
    
    Art pass in agent terms. The five silhouettes are the heavier lift because each needs a 40 px master with interior detail and a verified 16 px read. Budget roughly 45 minutes of agent work per silhouette for path construction, proportion tuning, and the two-size check. That is about 4 agent-hours for the set. The upgraded glyph set is 12 glyphs (battery, lance, torpedo, point defence, hull, shield, Brace, Focus Fire, Turn, Fire for Effect, End Ship, Command). Each glyph needs a 32 px master, a 20 px verification, and a 12 px skeleton check. Budget 20 to 30 minutes of agent work per glyph depending on complexity, so roughly 4 to 6 agent-hours for the full set. Total round 3 art pass: approximately 8 to 10 agent-hours, including two revision cycles for Ryan's review. This assumes the agent is working from these written specs and producing flat SVG or equivalent vector output.
    
    Risk that richer glyphs read worse at shelf sizes. This is the real risk and I will not wave it away. The round 2 placeholders were plain because plain reads. Adding the signature details and interior textures creates more visual mass in a 20 px or 12 px space. The danger is that at shelf size, where the player is scanning quickly during alternating activations, the upgraded glyphs blur into a uniform grey texture and the player can no longer snap-read "that is a lance, that is a battery." The upgrade must make the glyphs more specific, not more busy. If a signature detail does not improve recognition speed at 20 px, it should be cut regardless of how good it looks at 32 px.
    
    How to test before adoption. Three checks, run before the set is locked.
    
    First, the two-second naming test. Render every glyph at its shelf size (20 px for weapons, 32 px for orders, 12 px for integrity) on the dark veil. Show each one to a test viewer for two seconds, then hide it and ask them to name it. Target: 90 percent correct identification across the set. Any glyph below that threshold gets its skeleton reworked.
    
    Second, the row scan test. Place all weapon glyphs in a horizontal row at 20 px, spaced as they would appear in the HUD shelf. Place all order glyphs in a row at 32 px. Ask the viewer to find a specific glyph by name as fast as possible. Measure time to locate. If any glyph takes more than 1.5 seconds to find in a row of its peers, it is not distinct enough and its signature detail needs sharpening or its skeleton needs a proportion change.
    
    Third, the silhouette strip test. Render all five silhouettes at 16 px in a row, off-white on the dark veil. Ask the viewer to identify each class. Then render them at 16 px with one silhouette in ember underline state (the acting ship) and confirm the state mark does not obscure the class read. Then render one silhouette with the two-tone damage split and confirm it still reads as the right class.
    
    These three tests are cheap in agent terms (roughly 30 minutes to set up and run) and they catch the mush problem before the set is committed to the Godot project. If a glyph fails, the fix is almost always to simplify the skeleton or enlarge the signature detail, not to add more.
    
    One last note on cost. The silhouette set and the glyph set should be built in the same pass because they share line-weight conventions and the same ink colour. Splitting them across two agents or two sessions risks a drift in stroke weight or corner treatment. One agent, one pass, one style lock.
