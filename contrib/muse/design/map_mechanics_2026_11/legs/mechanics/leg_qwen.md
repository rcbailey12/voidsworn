Making Movement Matter in Voidsworn - Council Pack

Bucket A: Mechanics (what moving buys)

A1 - Evasion Through Movement
Bucket: Mechanics
Description. A ship that moves a minimum threshold (say 3 hexes at the 1.25 speed baseline) gains one evasion charge; moving 5+ gains two. Each charge imposes a flat penalty on incoming hit rolls (e.g. -1 per charge, capped at -2). Charges decay: a ship that moves fewer than 2 hexes on its next activation loses all charges. Charges are visible to both sides.
Causal path. Standing still is currently free. Under this rule, standing still is expensive: you shed your evasion and become easier to hit. After firing, the dominant play becomes "move 3+ hexes to keep my charges," which pulls ships apart, forces repositioning every activation, and makes speed a real stat because fast ships maintain evasion while still closing or crossing. The enemy, in turn, wants to close range (shorter shots are harder to evade, or a "sensor lock" ability strips charges) or concentrate fire before the target moves. The slugfest breaks because both sides are incentivised to keep sliding.
Smallest implementation sketch. One global rule, no new subsystem. Track an integer evasionCharges per ship. On activation: if hexes moved ≥ 3, set charges to 1; if ≥ 5, set to 2; if < 2, set to 0. In the hit-resolution step, subtract charges from the attacker's hit roll. In the bot's scoring, add a term: +k × evasionCharges_gained_if_I_move_here. Start with k = 0.15 of expected-incoming-damage so it's felt but doesn't override offense.
Simulator test. Run 200 mirror-fleet battles. Metrics that should move: distance travelled per move (up from 2.3-2.8 toward 3.5+), post-contact move rate (already >90 %, but the distance of those moves should jump), mean firing range (should rise slightly as ships kite), walking-only turn share (should stay low). Add a new metric: evasion-uptime, the fraction of activations where a ship holds ≥ 1 charge. If the mechanic works, evasion-uptime should be > 60 % for surviving ships.
Biggest risk. If the evasion penalty is too strong, battles become low-hit slogs where nobody can land shots and rounds stretch past 12. Mitigation: cap at 2 charges, make the penalty -1 not -2, and let short-range fire (range ≤ 2) ignore one charge. Also, if every ship simply moves 3 hexes in a circle to farm charges without changing tactical position, the movement is cosmetic. The bot scoring must tie evasion to directional movement (toward a flank, away from a threat cone) rather than any 3-hex shuffle.

A2 - Turn-Rate Limit (facing commitment)
Bucket: Mechanics
Description. A ship may rotate its facing by at most one hex-face (60°) per activation, whether or not it moves. Currently, if facing is freely settable at the end of a move, then shield routing is trivially optimised and position around the enemy doesn't matter. With a turn limit, a ship that wants to bring its nose to bear on a new threat, or that wants to present its strong shield facing to a different attacker, must spend activations turning. Moving to an enemy's rear arc is now a real tactical gain because they cannot snap to face you.
Causal path. If I can only turn 60° per activation, a fast ship that moves 4 hexes around my flank forces me to spend 2-3 activations turning before my weapons and shields are optimised against it. During that window I am vulnerable on the unshielded arc. This makes lateral and flanking movement valuable even at the same range, which is exactly the movement the current game lacks. It also makes speed meaningful: a faster ship completes the flanking arc sooner. The standing slugfest breaks because the optimal play is to keep angling, not to park.
Smallest implementation sketch. One rule change. On activation, record the ship's facing at the start. After movement, the new facing may differ by at most 1 hex-face. Shield-routing for the rest of the round (including enemy return fire) uses the new facing. No new subsystem. In the bot, the destination-state enumeration already includes facing; restrict the facing options in that enumeration to ±1 from current. Add a scoring term that values destinations where the enemy's current facing is away from the bot's ship (i.e. I am in their weak arc).
Simulator test. 200 battles. Metrics: distance travelled per move (should rise as ships sweep arcs), shot clustering (should fall, because ships spread around the enemy rather than parking in front), mean firing range (may stay similar but the angular distribution of shots around the target should widen - add a new metric: arc coverage, the standard deviation of firing bearings per target). If it works, ships stop lining up on one axis.
Biggest risk. A 60° limit may feel sluggish and frustrating for a human player, especially on a 30-hex board where reorienting takes several turns. It also interacts awkwardly with torpedoes: if I can't turn to face an incoming torpedo, that feels arbitrary rather than tactical. Mitigation: allow a "brace" action (no move, no fire) that permits a free 120° turn, giving a meaningful trade-off. Also playtest 90° (1.5 hex-faces) as a softer version.

A3 - Range-Band Damage Falloff
Bucket: Mechanics
Description. Weapons have an optimal band. Inside the inner half of their range they deal full damage; in the outer half, damage tapers linearly to 60 % at maximum range. (Example: a range-6 weapon does 100 % damage at range 1-3, then 93 %, 80 %, 60 % at ranges 4, 5, 6.) Torpedoes are unaffected.
Causal path. Right now, being at range 3 and being at range 6 are identical for the shooter, so there is no reason to close or to hold a specific distance. With falloff, a ship at range 3 hits ~40 % harder than one at range 6. The shooter wants to close; the target wants to open distance. Both want to move, in opposite directions, and the fight becomes a range-management dance rather than a static exchange. Fast ships can close and maintain the inner band; slow ships get stuck at the outer band and deal less damage, making speed matter. Different weapon loadouts create different preferred ranges, so a mixed fleet has ships that want to be at different distances, which naturally spreads the formation.
Smallest implementation sketch. One lookup table per weapon: a damage multiplier indexed by range. In the bot's offense-scoring, multiply expected damage by the falloff factor for each target. No new subsystem. The bot will automatically prefer closer hexes for the same target, and will start to differentiate between "move 2 hexes closer to shoot at 80 %" and "hold position and shoot at 60 %."
Simulator test. 200 battles. Metrics: mean firing range (should drop from the current ~3.1 toward ~2.4-2.8 as ships seek the inner band), distance travelled per move (should rise because ships are actively closing), walking-only turn share (should fall because standing at max range is now inefficient). Also check rounds to resolution - if battles get shorter because close-range damage is high, that's a signal to flatten the curve.
Biggest risk. If the falloff is too steep, every ship rushes to range 1 and the game becomes a knife-fight, losing the feel of capital-ship gunnery. If too shallow, nobody notices. The 60 %-at-max floor is a starting guess; it may need to be 70 %. Also, if all weapons have the same range, all ships converge on the same hex and the blob problem returns. This mechanic works best when the fleet has weapons of different ranges, so ships hold different bands. In mirror-fleet testing, the effect will be muted; test with asymmetric loadouts too.

Bucket B: AI Design (how the opponent should think)

B1 - Threat and Influence Fields
Bucket: AI Design
Description. Maintain two spatial grids over the board, updated once per round. The threat field marks, for each hex, the expected incoming damage from all enemy weapons given their current position, facing, and range, decayed with distance. The influence field marks friendly support: for each hex, how many allied weapons could bear on an enemy at that hex, again decayed. The bot scores destinations against both fields instead of computing per-weapon threat from scratch for each candidate hex.
Causal path. The current bot evaluates each destination independently against the enemy fleet. It has no sense of "this hex is in the overlapping fire arcs of three enemy ships" versus "this hex is only threatened by one." A threat field makes that overlap visible and cheap to query, so the bot will route ships around danger zones rather than through them. The influence field lets the bot see where its own fire is concentrated, so it can push ships toward the weak frontier of the enemy line or pull them back from an over-matched sector. This is the standard Tozour / Dave Mark influence-map approach. The key effect on movement: ships stop beelining into the blob because the field shows the blob is red. They curve around, spread, and attack from multiple directions.
Smallest implementation sketch. Two 30×22 (or 38×26) float arrays, recomputed at the start of each round (not per activation - that's fast enough). For each enemy ship, stamp its weapon arcs onto the threat field: for each hex in range, add expected damage × (1 / distance) × facing-arc multiplier. For the influence field, do the same for friendly ships. In the bot's destination scoring, replace the current per-enemy-weapon threat loop with a single lookup: threatField[hex]. Add +w × influenceField[hex] as a friendly-support term. This is a scoring change plus two arrays; no new subsystem beyond the arrays.
Simulator test. 200 battles. Metrics: board coverage (the 0.13 hex-visited fraction should rise toward 0.18-0.22), shot clustering (should fall because ships spread to avoid overlapping threat), distance travelled per move (should rise as ships take curved paths around threat zones). Add a metric: min-inter-ship distance (mean distance between allied ships at end of round). If the field works, this should increase - ships stop stacking.
Biggest risk. A threat field updated once per round goes stale mid-round as ships move and die. In an 8-ship battle, by activation 5 the field computed at round start may be wrong. Mitigation: recompute after each activation (it's just two array stamps, very cheap), or accept the staleness and note it as a known limitation for the first test. A second risk: if the threat field dominates scoring, the bot becomes purely evasive and never closes to fire. The response-curve fix in C1 addresses this.

B2 - Coordinated Flanking (role pairs)
Bucket: AI Design
Description. Before each round, the bot assigns pairs: one ship is the anvil (it approaches the enemy head-on, fixing their facing and attention), the other is the hammer (it takes a wide path to reach the enemy's flank or rear arc). The assignment is simple: for each enemy ship, pick the closest allied ship as anvil and the second-closest as hammer. The hammer's scoring gets a bonus for hexes in the enemy's side or rear arc; the anvil's scoring gets a bonus for hexes in the enemy's front arc.
Causal path. Without coordination, every bot ship independently picks the hex with the best offense-minus-threat, which tends to be the hex directly in front of the nearest enemy. All ships converge on the same axis. By splitting roles, one ship deliberately goes wide. The wide ship's movement is longer (it's taking a flanking path), the fleet spreads, and the enemy must split fire or turn to face two directions. This works especially well with A2 (turn-rate limit): the hammer arriving on the flank is a genuine threat because the enemy can't snap to face it.
Smallest implementation sketch. At the start of each round, for each enemy ship, sort allied ships by distance. Assign the nearest as anvil, the second-nearest as hammer. Store the assignment. In the destination scoring for a hammer, add +f × flankBonus where flankBonus is 1 if the candidate hex is in the target enemy's side/rear arc, 0 otherwise. For an anvil, add +f × frontBonus for hexes in the front arc. f is small (0.1 of expected damage). No new subsystem; it's a per-round assignment table and two conditional scoring terms.
Simulator test. 200 battles. Metrics: arc coverage (from A2 - the angular spread of firing bearings should widen), board coverage (should rise because the hammer takes a wide path), distance travelled per move (hammer ships should move 4+ hexes). Also check win split - if flanking is effective, the bot should win more often against a non-flanking bot, confirming the tactic has value.
Biggest risk. With only 6 ships per side, splitting into 3 pairs leaves no reserve and makes each pair vulnerable to focus fire. If the enemy bot focuses both ships of a pair, the pair dies before the flank arrives. Mitigation: only assign flanking pairs when the fleet has ≥ 4 surviving ships; otherwise fall back to the current behavior. Also, the assignment is re-rolled every round, so a dead hammer's partner gets reassigned. A subtler risk: the flanking path may take the hammer out of support range of the anvil, so the anvil gets destroyed before the hammer arrives. The influence field (B1) mitigates this by penalising hexes with zero friendly support.

B3 - One-Ply Anticipation
Bucket: AI Design
Description. When scoring a destination, the bot asks: "If I move here and fire, what will the enemy's best response be on their next activation?" It doesn't search the full tree - it just, for the top 3 candidate destinations, computes the enemy's best single-ship response (the enemy ship with the best offense against me from my new position) and subtracts the expected damage from that response. This is one-ply lookahead: I consider my move, then their best reply.
Causal path. The current bot is greedy: it picks the hex that maximises this activation's net utility. It doesn't notice that hex A, which looks great now, puts it in the overlapping arcs of two enemy ships that activate next. With one-ply anticipation, the bot sees that hex A will draw 40 damage next turn and hex B only 15, so it picks B even though B's immediate offense is slightly lower. This makes the bot's movement more deliberate and positional - it starts moving to deny the enemy good responses, which means more lateral and retreat moves, not just forward shuffles.
Smallest implementation sketch. In the destination-scoring loop, after computing the base utility for a candidate hex, identify the 2 enemy ships with the highest expected damage against that hex. Compute their best response (move to optimal range, fire). Subtract that expected damage from the candidate's utility. This is the existing threat-term logic but applied after the move, not at the current position, and it considers the enemy's movement, not just their current position. Cost: for each of ~20 candidate hexes, evaluate 2 enemy responses, each a simple range-and-damage check. No new subsystem.
Simulator test. 200 battles. Metrics: walking-only turn share (should fall because the bot sees that standing still invites a response), rounds to resolution (should stay stable or drop - the bot makes fewer suicidal moves), mean damage taken per round (should fall because the bot avoids bad positions). Add a metric: exposure rate, the fraction of activations where a ship ends its move in a hex threatened by 2+ enemy ships. This should drop.
Biggest risk. One-ply lookahead doubles the scoring cost. With 20 candidate hexes and 2 enemy responses each, that's 40 extra evaluations per activation. On a 38×26 board with 12 ships this is still trivial for a modern CPU, but it needs profiling. The bigger risk is that the anticipation model is wrong: the bot assumes the enemy will act optimally, but the enemy bot (or player) might not. If the anticipation is too pessimistic, the bot becomes timid and never closes. Mitigation: weight the anticipated damage by 0.7, not 1.0, to account for model error.

Bucket C: Bot Approach (implementation steps)

C1 - Multiplicative Scoring with Response Curves
Bucket: Bot Approach
Description. Replace the current additive utility sum (offense - threat - distance + objective) with a multiplicative structure where each consideration is normalised to [0, 1] via a response curve and the final score is their product (or weighted product). The considerations are: offense potential, survival (inverse threat), range quality, evasion gain, objective proximity. Each has a curve that maps raw value to [0, 1] with a shape (linear, exponential, step) chosen per consideration.
Causal path. The additive sum is the root cause of the bot's blindness. When offense is 40 and threat is 35, the net is 5; when offense is 10 and threat is 2, the net is 8. The bot picks the second hex even though the first hex is where the fight is. Worse, the objective term at +3 can't compete with offense at +40, so objectives are ignored (exactly what the 600-battle data showed). In a multiplicative model, a hex with zero offense scores zero regardless of safety - the bot won't park in a safe corner. A hex with zero threat (total safety) scores zero on survival if that's how the curve is set - the bot won't suicide. Each term gates the others rather than being drowned out. The distance-to-nearest-enemy term, which currently acts as a crude attractor, becomes a curve that peaks at optimal range and falls off on both sides, so the bot stops beelining and starts orbiting.
Smallest implementation sketch. This is a scoring-function rewrite, not a new subsystem. Define five floats per candidate hex, each mapped through a curve:
offense = expectedDamage / maxPossibleDamage → [0,1], linear.
survival = 1 - (expectedIncoming / shipHP) → [0,1], clamp.
rangeQuality = 1 at optimal range, falling to 0.4 at max range (ties into A3 if implemented, or approximated as 1 - range/maxRange).
evasionGain = 1 if moving ≥ 3 hexes, 0.5 if ≥ 1, 0 if 0 (ties into A1).
objectivePull = 1 / (1 + distToObjectve), logistic.
Score = offense^w1 × survival^w2 × rangeQuality^w3 × evasionGain^w4 × objectivePull^w5, with weights w1-w5 summing to 1 (start at 0.3, 0.25, 0.2, 0.15, 0.1). This replaces the existing sum. One function rewrite.
Simulator test. 200 battles, A/B against the current additive scorer. Metrics: board coverage (should rise), walking-only turn share (should fall), shot clustering (should fall), objective hold rate (should rise because the objective term is no longer drowned). Most importantly, win split against the old bot should be ≥ 55-45 in favour of the new scorer, confirming it's not just different but better.
Biggest risk. Multiplicative scoring is brittle when a term is near zero: a single zeroed consideration kills the hex. If the bot has no weapon in range, offense = 0, and every hex scores 0. Mitigation: use max(offense, 0.05) as a floor, or switch to a weighted geometric mean with additive fallback when the product is below a threshold. Tuning five response curves and five weights is also a lot of knobs; the risk of overfitting to mirror-fleet tests is real. Test against asymmetric fleets before declaring victory.

C2 - Staged Rollout Order
Bucket: Bot Approach
Description. This is not a new mechanic but the implementation sequence for testing the above ideas one at a time, so that each change is isolated and measurable. The order is chosen so each step builds on the previous one and each can be validated independently.
Step 1 - Scoring restructure (C1 only). Implement multiplicative scoring with response curves. No new game rules. Run 200 mirror battles. Confirm: coverage up, walking turns down, win split ≥ 52 % vs. old bot. If this fails, stop and debug the curves before adding mechanics.
Step 2 - Add evasion (A1). Add the evasion-charge rule. Add evasionGain to the bot's scoring (it's already a slot in C1). Run 200 battles. Confirm: distance-per-move up, evasion-uptime > 60 %, rounds stable. If battles go long, reduce the hit penalty from -1 to -0.5.
Step 3 - Add range falloff (A3). Add the damage-falloff table. Update the offense curve to use actual expected damage after falloff. Run 200 battles. Confirm: mean firing range drops by 0.3-0.5 hexes, distance-per-move rises further.
Step 4 - Add threat field (B1). Build the two arrays. Replace per-enemy threat computation with field lookup. Run 200 battles. Confirm: board coverage up, min-inter-ship distance up, shot clustering down.
Step 5 - Add turn-rate limit (A2). Restrict facing changes to 60° per activation. Update the bot's facing enumeration. Add flanking-arc bonus to scoring. Run 200 battles. Confirm: arc coverage up, distance-per-move up.
Step 6 - Add flanking pairs (B2). Add the per-round role assignment. Run 200 battles. Confirm: arc coverage up further, board coverage up, win split vs. Step-5 bot ≥ 54 %.
Step 7 - Add one-ply anticipation (B3). Add the enemy-response subtraction. Run 200 battles. Confirm: exposure rate down, damage taken per round down, rounds stable.
Each step is one PR, one test run, one go/no-go.
Causal path. The sequence matters because C1 fixes the bot's ability to see value before A1/A3 create new value to see. B1 gives spatial awareness before B2 asks the bot to coordinate spatially. A2's turn limit is more valuable after B1 (the bot can see threat arcs) and B2 (the bot can exploit them). B3 is last because it's the most expensive per-activation and benefits from all prior information being in place.
Simulator test. Each step has its own test as described above. The meta-metric is: cumulative coverage (board coverage should rise monotonically across steps, from 0.13 toward 0.20+). If a step causes coverage to drop, that step is reverted before proceeding.
Biggest risk. The sequence assumes each step's effect is independent. In practice, evasion + range falloff + turn-rate limit interact: a ship that moves 4 hexes to maintain evasion and close to optimal range and reach a flank arc is doing three things at once, and the combined effect may be stronger (or weirder) than the sum. The staged rollout catches this at each step, but the final configuration needs its own balance pass. Also, 7 steps × 200 battles = 1,400 battles minimum; budget the simulation time.

Top-3 Ranking
1. C1 - Multiplicative Scoring with Response Curves.
This is the highest-leverage change because it fixes the root cause identified in the 600-battle data: the additive scoring drowns the objective term, can't represent spatial nuance, and reduces every decision to "move toward the enemy and shoot." Every other idea in this document - evasion, range falloff, threat fields, flanking - is invisible to the bot unless the scoring can weigh multiple considerations without one dominating. It's also the cheapest to implement: one function rewrite, no new game rules, no new subsystems. If this doesn't move the needle, nothing else will save the bot.
2. A1 - Evasion Through Movement.
This is the single cleanest mechanical answer to "standing still is fine." It makes movement defensively valuable every activation, not just offensively. The causal path is short and direct: move or become easier to hit. It works for human players (the rule is visible and intuitive), it scales with the speed stat, and it interacts naturally with range falloff (kite at optimal range while maintaining evasion). It's also the idea most validated by a successful reference (BattleTech's evasion charges have carried that game's movement economy for years). The risk of low-hit slogs is real but tunable.
3. B1 - Threat and Influence Fields.
This is the highest-leverage AI-design change because it gives the bot spatial awareness it currently lacks entirely. Without a field, the bot evaluates each hex against the enemy fleet as a flat list of weapons; with a field, it sees overlapping arcs, safe corridors, and the shape of the battle. This is what makes ships curve around threats instead of beelining into the blob, and it's what makes flanking coordination (B2) possible later. The implementation is two float arrays and a lookup - well within the "scoring change" constraint. The reason it's third rather than first is that the field is only as good as the scoring that reads it, so C1 needs to be in place for the field's information to translate into better decisions.

The remaining ideas (A2 turn-rate limit, A3 range falloff, B2 flanking pairs, B3 one-ply anticipation) are all worthwhile, but they are amplifiers: they make the top three work better rather than fixing the core problem. Implement them in the staged order in C2 after the top three are validated.
