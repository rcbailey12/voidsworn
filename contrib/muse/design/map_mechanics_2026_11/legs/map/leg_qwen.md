# Leg: Qwen (Qwen3.8-Max, selector verified before, header verified after; no A/B choice)
# Whole-map design round, Nov 17, 2026. Answer reproduced verbatim as returned.

Design Leg: Making the Whole Map Matter
1. Diagnosis
The map doesn't matter because every encounter defines exactly one point of value, and both fleets are pulled to it by a single attractor. The objective function is a dot on the grid. Speed governs how fast you reach the dot; range governs how close you must get before you can hit things near the dot. But no ship ever has a reason to be anywhere except "at the dot" or "on the shortest path to the dot." Coverage on 38×26 sits at 0.118-0.135 not because ships are too slow to spread, but because there is no second place worth being. The probes confirm this directly: intercept's "spread" is one side's forced march along one lane (enemy coverage 0.100 vs player 0.063); break's fight never leaves the deployment edge; hold's relay sits where the knife-fight was already happening. Deployment distance is the only pacing lever because it is the only axis along which the single attractor can be displaced. Once you collapse the distance, the rest of the map is scenery.
The mechanism, in one sentence: a single-attractor objective function makes position interchangeable, and interchangeable position makes the board irrelevant.
I largely agree with the chair's five readings. I would refine point 3: asymmetric speed does express itself-it buys 7-8 wins in 12-but it expresses as a race advantage to the one point, not as a tactical choice. Speed without a second destination is just a smaller number on the approach timer. The fix is not to nerf speed's win-rate expression but to give it somewhere else to go.
2. Design Principles
Two attractors minimum. Every encounter must present at least two spatially separated things worth being at, so a fleet must split, choose, or sequence. A single objective point is banned as a shape.
Position must pay rent. A hex or zone should confer an ongoing, visible advantage (combat bonus, denial, scoring) for as long as a ship occupies or contests it. Passing through a hex should be worth less than holding it. This is what makes range feel like range (hold the edge of a zone and fire in) and speed feel like speed (reposition between zones before a slower opponent can follow).
The current bot must be able to see the value. Every mechanism must either register under the existing objective-term scoring or require exactly one stated additional bot term. If the bot can't perceive the advantage, the sim can't measure it, and the parity harness is blind.
Data-shaped before logic-shaped before new-subsystem-shaped. The browser/Godot parity cost scales steeply with subsystem count. Prefer changes that are new fields on existing structures, then small new functions in existing passes, then new passes. Rank honestly.
3. Proposals, Smallest First
3a. Multi-point objectives (data edit on existing encounter definitions)
What changes: Objective target definitions become lists of 2-3 spatially separated hex clusters instead of one. Hold/Relay gets 3 relays placed at roughly triangular positions (e.g., hex-distance ≥ 10 apart on 34×24). Defend gets 2 station nodes. Break gets exit hexes on two non-adjacent edges. Intercept keeps its crossing lane but adds a second, offset lane. The allowed-shape weighting per objective is re-tuned so that shapes with natural chokepoints between the points (hourglass, lanes, archipelago) are favoured over open.
Reuses: Objective hex lists (already data), bot objective-term scoring (already evaluates "is a ship at the objective"), arena-weighting table (already per-objective).
Expected probe movement: Coverage should rise above the 0.163 intercept high-water mark because the fleet must physically split to contest separated points. Move-only activations should drop below 15% on 34×24 because ships are always transiting between points rather than parking at one. First-contact activation stays similar (the fleets still approach each other), but the location of contact varies by which point each side commits to. Rounds should tick up slightly (10-13) because splitting delays concentration.
Feel: Speed lets a fast wing contest relay A, then reposition to relay B before a slow wing can follow. Range lets a ship sit between two relays and fire at enemies approaching either one, making a central position genuinely strong.
Sim status: Testable now. The bot already scores objective proximity. If it currently picks the single nearest objective hex, the only change is that the objective-hex list has three entries instead of one, and the bot picks the nearest of the three. No new scoring term needed.
Implementation: 1 pass. Objective definition data file (target hex lists), arena-weight table, possibly one line in bot objective scoring if it assumes a single target hex. ~2 files.
3b. Terrain combat bonuses (small data + combat-calc addition)
What changes: The existing ~33 terrain hexes get a bonus tag. Three kinds, kept simple: sensor ridge (+1 hex effective weapon range while occupying), shielded debris (+1 shield regen per round while occupying), firing lane (+1 to-hit for weapons fired from this hex). These are placed in data per board shape, biased toward positions between the multi-point objective sites from 3a so they sit on natural transit routes.
Reuses: Terrain hex definitions (already exist, ~33 hexes per board), combat resolution pass (already looks up attacker/defender hex for terrain modifiers, if any; add the lookup if absent).
Expected probe movement: Coverage rises modestly (ships detour 1-2 hexes to sit on a bonus hex en route to an objective, spreading the fleet slightly). Move-only activations drop because a ship that stops on a sensor ridge and fires at range 7 instead of moving to range 6 has converted a move-only turn into a firing turn. Mean firing range ticks up by ~0.5 hex on sensor ridges, making range felt as a positional choice rather than a flat stat.
Feel: A player with longer-range ships parks on sensor ridges and controls the lane; a short-range brawler must close through the debris (shielded) to get into knife-fight distance. Speed determines who gets to the good hexes first.
Sim status: Testable now. The bot already moves toward objectives; a 1-hex detour to a bonus hex that also advances toward the objective will show up in its existing pathing. If the bot strictly shortest-paths, one small term is needed: add the bonus value to the hex score in the movement evaluator. State it explicitly: bot term = terrain bonus value added to destination-hex score, weighted at roughly 0.3× the objective pull so it's a tiebreaker, not an override.
Implementation: 1-2 passes. Terrain data file (add bonus field), combat calc (add lookup, ~10 lines), bot movement scoring (add term, ~5 lines). ~3 files.
3c. Deployment as a structured choice (deployment-phase rework)
What changes: Each map shape defines 2-3 deployment zones (e.g., left-flank cluster, centre cluster, right-flank cluster, each a contiguous set of ~6-10 hexes on the appropriate edge). At battle start the player picks one zone for their line and optionally designates 1-2 ships as reserves (see 3d). The enemy's zone choice is hidden until the end of round 1 (their ships are placed but not revealed; the sim can simply enumerate all zone-pair combinations). Deployment distance between zones varies: same-flank zones start ~18 hexes apart on 30×22; opposite-flank zones start ~26 hexes apart and force a diagonal approach.
Reuses: Deployment code (already places ships in lines; change the anchor hexes), the existing FAC_MAP faction-to-shape preference (factions that prefer corridors get corridor-shaped deployment zones), reserves flag (already in code).
Expected probe movement: Coverage rises because different zone-pairings produce different approach vectors and contact points. A left-flank vs right-flank deployment on a 34×24 board forces a diagonal crossing that touches hexes the centre-vs-centre approach never does. Move-only activations stay similar (the approach is still movement-heavy), but the spatial distribution of those moves widens. First-contact activation varies by zone pairing (12-13 for opposite flanks, 8-10 for same-flank), giving deployment a tangible pacing effect beyond "one fixed distance."
Feel: Speed matters because a fast fleet deployed on the left flank can cross to contest a right-side objective before the enemy's centre deployment arrives. Range matters because a long-range fleet can deploy centre and fire at both flanks. The pre-battle choice is a real decision with visible consequences.
Sim status: Testable now for the sim (enumerate all zone pairings, run 12 seeds each, measure coverage spread across pairings). The hidden-reveal timing is a player-facing feature and doesn't affect the sim's measurement. No new bot term.
Implementation: 2 passes. Deployment data (zone definitions per shape, ~2-3 zones × 12 shapes), deployment placement logic (swap fixed line for zone anchor), reveal timing (1 flag, checked at round-end). ~4 files.
3d. Reserves as a timed commitment (small rule on existing flag)
What changes: The player may hold up to 2 ships as reserves at deployment (already flagged in code). At the start of any round from round 2 onward, the player may commit reserves to any board edge hex that is (a) in the deployment half of the map and (b) not within 3 hexes of an enemy ship. Committed reserves activate normally that round. The bot commits reserves when its objective-term score for the nearest contested point drops below a threshold (i.e., it's losing a point and needs reinforcements), targeting the edge closest to that point.
Reuses: Reserve flag (exists), alive-count exclusion (exists), edge-hex validation (trivial), bot objective scoring (exists; threshold check is ~5 lines).
Expected probe movement: Coverage rises in the mid-game (rounds 3-6) because reserves enter from a different edge than the main deployment, creating a second cluster of activity. Move-only activations for the reserve ships are high on their entry round but fall after. The main effect is temporal: the battle has a second wave that shifts the point of contact, preventing the single-park pattern.
Feel: Speed matters because a fast reserve can enter and reach the fight in 1-2 rounds; a slow reserve enters and is still 3 rounds from being relevant, so commitment timing is a real decision. Range matters because a long-range reserve can enter and immediately contribute fire from the edge; a short-range reserve must survive the approach.
Sim status: Testable with one stated bot term: commit-when-losing threshold on the objective score, plus nearest-valid-edge-hex selection. Without that term the bot never commits and the mechanism is invisible.
Implementation: 2 passes. Reserve commit trigger (round-start hook, ~15 lines), edge-hex validation (~10 lines), bot commit decision (~10 lines). ~3 files.
3e. Threat projection / area denial (new rule, medium)
What changes: Each ship projects a threat zone equal to its longest weapon range. An enemy ship that ends its movement inside a threat zone is marked "contested" and suffers -1 to its next attack roll (or the projecting ship gets a free 1-damage reaction shot; pick one, keep it simple). This makes holding a position deny an area, not just occupy a hex. Threat zones are visible in the UI.
Reuses: Weapon range stat (exists), movement-resolution pass (add contested check), combat calc (apply the -1 or reaction shot).
Expected probe movement: Coverage rises because ships must route around threat zones rather than beelining, spreading movement paths. Move-only activations may rise slightly (ships take longer routes), but effective coverage (hexes that matter tactically) rises because threat zones make empty hexes meaningful. Mean firing range rises because ships fire from the edge of threat zones rather than closing to point-blank.
Feel: Range is felt as area control: a ship with range 6 denies a 6-hex bubble. Speed is felt as the ability to slip through gaps between threat zones or to reposition before a zone locks down. A slow, long-range ship becomes a fortress; a fast, short-range ship becomes an infiltrator.
Sim status: Testable with one stated bot term: add a threat-zone penalty to hex scores in the movement evaluator, so the bot avoids ending movement in contested hexes unless the objective pull overrides. Without this, the bot ignores threat zones and the mechanism is invisible.
Implementation: 3 passes. Threat zone calculation (per-ship, per-round, ~20 lines), movement-resolution contested check (~15 lines), combat modifier (~5 lines), bot movement scoring penalty (~10 lines), UI display of zones. ~5 files. This is the first genuinely new rule in the list.
3f. Speed as activation currency (larger rule, listed for completeness)
I include this only to scope it and then set it aside. The idea: faster ships get 2 activations per round (move + act, or move + move), slower ships get 1. This makes speed a per-round tactical resource rather than a flat move stat. However, it touches the activation scheduler, the round loop, and every system that assumes one-activation-per-ship-per-round. It is a subsystem change, not a data or small-logic change, and the parity cost across browser and Godot is high. I would not build this before 3a-3e are measured. Listed here so the council knows it exists and where it sits in priority.
Sim status: Untestable until built; requires activation-scheduler rework.
4. The Deployment Question
Yes, deployment should become a decision. The form is 3c above: 2-3 zones per map shape, player picks one, enemy's pick is hidden until end of round 1, reserves are a sub-choice within deployment (3d).
What stops it from being a solved dominant choice: Three things, all already in the proposals.
Multi-point objectives (3a) mean no single zone is closest to all objectives. Left-flank deployment is best for the left relay but worst for the right one. The "correct" zone depends on which objective you prioritise, and that depends on what you think the enemy will do.
Hidden enemy choice means you cannot counter-deploy optimally. You must commit without full information. In the sim, this is tested by running all zone-pair combinations and checking that no single zone wins >60% across pairings. If one zone dominates, the objective placement or zone geometry needs adjustment.
Terrain bonuses (3b) mean the approach vector matters, not just the destination. A deployment that routes through sensor ridges is better for a range-heavy fleet; one that threads through debris is better for a brawler. Faction composition (already tied to FAC_MAP) interacts with zone choice.
The anti-dominance test is empirical: run the sim across all zone pairings, 12 seeds each, and check that win-rate spread across pairings is within ±15%. If one pairing is >65%, rebalance the zone geometry or objective placement.
5. The Hunt Problem
Hunt is degenerate because line deployment places the flagship at activation-0 reach, and it dies in 2-3 rounds (12/12 loss). The quarry is never out of reach, so there is no chase.
Smallest fix, in three data-level changes:
Deploy the flagship in the far quarter of the map (any hex beyond the 75% depth line from the hunter's deployment edge), with 1-2 escort ships in a screen 4-6 hexes ahead of it. The hunting fleet deploys at the near edge in its normal line. This creates 16-20 hexes of separation on a 30×22 board, forcing at least 4-5 rounds of pursuit before contact.
Give the flagship a 1-hex speed bonus (a "flight-speed" tag on the flagship unit, not a global speed buff). On a base-speed map this means the flagship moves 4 hexes per activation vs the hunters' 3, so the gap closes at 1 hex per round rather than 3. The chase lasts 8-12 rounds instead of 2-3.
The flagship activates first each round (it is the quarry; it runs). This is a one-line change to the activation order for hunt encounters. It prevents the hunters from collapsing the gap before the flagship can extend it.
Expected probe movement: Hunt coverage rises from 0.089/0.058 toward 0.14+ because the chase traverses the map. Rounds rise from 2-3 to 8-10. Move-only activations rise for the hunters (they are chasing) but the flagship's escorts create intermittent contact that breaks up the pure-move pattern. The 12/12 loss becomes a genuine contest.
Sim status: Testable now. Deployment position is data. Speed bonus is a unit stat. Activation order is one line. No new bot term; the bot already chases the objective (the flagship), and the flagship's bot flees toward the far edge (existing flee logic at 2/3 losses, or a simple "move away from nearest enemy" if flee doesn't trigger before death).
Implementation: 1 pass. Hunt deployment data (flagship position, escort positions), flagship unit speed tag, activation-order override for hunt. ~2 files.
6. Refusals
No bigger maps. The owner ruled it out, and the coverage data confirms it: going from 30×22 to 38×26 drops coverage from ~0.18 to ~0.12. More hexes around the same single attractor means more empty margin. The problem is the attractor, not the acreage.
No global speed or range multipliers as pacing fixes. The 2,160-battle sweep already showed these compress the approach timer without changing the shape of the fight. Speed ×2 on 38×26 still puts first shot at activation 12-13. Range ×1.5 adds plinking without adding decisions. The owner ruled these out and the data agrees.
No random events as structural pacing. The owner ruled it out. I add: random events would add variance to when things happen but not to where they matter, which is the actual problem.
No fog of war over the full map. It is expensive to implement in parity, it does not fix the single-attractor problem (hiding the one point of value doesn't create a second one), and the sim cannot meaningfully model imperfect information without a much more complex bot.
No new encounter types beyond the existing eight. The owner's constraint. The eight encounters are sufficient if their objective geometry is fixed.
No per-ship speed differentiation as the primary fix. It is a fine secondary dial (and the hunt flagship speed bonus above uses it surgically), but making speed heterogeneous across the fleet does not create a second place worth being. It changes who arrives first at the single attractor, not whether there are two attractors.
No activation-scheduler rework (proposal 3f) before the above are measured. It is the highest-cost item, touches the most systems, and its benefit is unclear until multi-point objectives and threat zones establish whether per-round activation asymmetry is even the right lever.
7. Single First Build
Build 3a: multi-point objectives, starting with Hold/Relay.
Change Hold from one relay hex to three relay hexes placed in a triangle with ≥10-hex mutual separation on 34×24 (≥12-hex on 38×26). Adjust the arena weighting to favour hourglass, lanes, and archipelago shapes (shapes that put natural terrain between the relays). Keep the win condition the same: control a relay (ship adjacent, no enemy adjacent) at round start, need 3 round-starts. Run 12 seeds on belt, station, and storm at 34×24 and 38×26. Compare to the existing hold baseline (coverage 0.128/0.116, rounds 9, move-only ~22%).
What tells you it worked:
Combined coverage rises above 0.18 on 34×24 (above the rout baseline on the smaller 30×22 map, which is the current high-water mark). This means the fleet is physically spreading to cover multiple points.
Player and enemy coverage converge toward each other (both sides above 0.09), rather than one side dominating coverage as in intercept. Both fleets are splitting.
Move-only activations fall below 16%, because ships are cycling between relays and firing at enemies contesting adjacent relays, rather than parking at one point.
The fight does not park. No single hex cluster accounts for >40% of all shots fired. If 60%+ of shots still happen at one relay, the triangle is too tight or the bot is over-weighting the nearest relay and the geometry needs widening.
Rounds stay in the 8-12 band. If rounds spike above 14, the relays are too far apart and the game is stalling; if they drop below 7, the relays are too close and the fleet can cover all three without splitting.
If those five numbers hit, the single-attractor diagnosis is confirmed as the binding constraint, and the council can proceed to 3b (terrain bonuses) and 3c (deployment zones) as the second and third builds, each measured against the multi-point baseline.
