# Blind leg, Rey, for issue #515 (concepts round 8)

Written before looking at any of this round's reference pictures, per the issue block. The question set for this leg: what makes a ship concept picture useful to the person building the 3D model from it, and what makes a concept read as a cartoon or a toy. Everything below is from the craft of concept art and kit-bash modelling generally, plus what the earlier rounds of this project taught the hard way; it is not a reading of the six ships.

## What a builder actually needs from a concept picture

1. **Mass before detail.** The builder blocks out the big volumes first. A picture is useful when the silhouette and the proportions of the main masses can be measured off it: how long against how wide, where the bulk sits, what tapers and what stays slab. A beauty view at a three-quarter angle lies about all of this politely; the flat top and side views are the contract. This is why the round's order (beauty first, sheet derived from the accepted beauty) only works if the sheet is a true re-projection of the beauty's masses, not a fresh invention wearing the same paint.

2. **Parts that can be counted and placed.** The builder's second pass is an inventory: this many guns, of these kinds, at these stations; this many engine bells; one hangar mouth. A useful picture makes every functional part a discrete object with a visible base or seating, so the builder can point at it and say "that one". Parts that melt into the hull decoration, or that exist in the beauty but not the sheet, are where builds go wrong.

3. **Surface broken at the scale of the ship.** A real large object carries evidence of its size in its surface: panel lines, seams, access hatches, fittings, repairs, each much smaller than the whole. The smaller and more regular that evidence, the larger the object reads. A builder uses exactly this layer to keep a model from reading as a miniature: the concept has to show the panel scale, the seam rhythm, where plates overlap, and which areas are machinery versus armour versus skin.

4. **Materials stated, not implied.** The builder needs to know what each surface IS: cast iron, rolled plate, glass, bone, flesh. A useful concept separates materials by how they take light: the roughness, the edge wear, whether a highlight is sharp or soft. If everything on the ship has the same finish, the builder has to guess, and the guess flattens the model.

5. **One light, honestly applied.** A single hard light from a stated direction tells the builder where forms turn, what is recessed, what stands proud. Soft ambient beauty lighting hides exactly the information a build needs. Shadows are measurements.

6. **The awkward angles answered.** Every ship has a view the beauty shot avoids: the belly, the stern face, the top at the bow. A concept set is useful in proportion to how few of those it leaves open. The sheet (top above side) covers two; the belly and the stern are the usual gaps, and they should be named as gaps rather than papered over.

## What makes a concept read as a cartoon or a toy

1. **Few, large, flat areas of one colour.** The single strongest cartoon tell. Real built things are assembled; an area the size of a house with nothing on it reads as coloured plastic or as an illustrator's fill. This is the "Hegemony cartoon" failure from round 7 in general form: the shape was right, the surface had too little in it.

2. **Outlines doing the work.** In an illustration, a dark contour line separates the object from the background and one part from another. In a photograph there are no contour lines, only light and shadow. A concept whose parts are separated by drawn lines instead of by shadow and overlap will read as a drawing no matter how detailed the fill.

3. **Toy proportions in the fittings.** Fittings drawn at the wrong scale make the ship small: a railing, hatch or gun barrel as thick as a corridor would be, panel gaps wide enough to fall into. Toys are moulded with chunky parts and wide seams because small hands and thick plastic need them. A concept with chunky fittings reads as a model of a ship, not a ship.

4. **Perfect cleanliness and perfect symmetry of wear.** Real objects accumulate: heat staining near engines and guns, chipped edges where things dock and strike, replaced plates that do not match. Wear placed evenly everywhere, or nowhere, reads as a render. (The reverse also fails: dirt sprayed at random reads as texture noise, not history. Wear belongs where work happens.)

5. **Candy lighting and saturated colour.** Multiple coloured lights, glowing seams everywhere, and a palette at full saturation are the language of game icons and cartoons. One hard white light on black space, with colour carried by the materials themselves, is the photographic register the style line asks for.

6. **Smooth symbol machinery.** Guns that are smooth tubes with a ring, engines that are glowing discs, sensors that are plain domes: these are symbols for machinery, and symbols read as cartoon. Working machinery has housings, mounts, cabling or its equivalent, adjustment and access built in, and parts at several sizes nested together.

7. **A finish with no micro-surface.** Photographs of metal and glass carry fine grain: brushed texture, casting pits, faint scratches, subtle variation inside one plate. A surface with perfectly even tone and perfectly smooth gradients is a 3D render's default, and it reads as one.

## What this means for scoring this round

For each of the six ships I will score the style line in the issue (photograph not illustration; many small plates and fittings, no large flat single-colour area; real materials under one hard light on black; weapons and fittings as working machinery) and add the builder's tests above: can the masses be measured, can the parts be counted, do the beauty and the sheet describe the same ship, and are the gaps (belly, stern) named rather than hidden. The two Hullbreaker edits are the control case: their references are already in the style, so any drift toward flatness or cleanliness in the results is the method failing, not the brief. For the style-only ships (S9, E9, E10) the trap is the opposite: detail added so eagerly that it moves a mount, fattens a barrel or plates over a feature, which is a shape change wearing style's clothes. For G17, the lens change is the round's one true design change, and the test is whether "grown, not bolted" survives in both views: a lens that reads as seated in the hull's own glass in the beauty can still come out as a pod in plan if the sheet is drawn carelessly.

One honest limit: this leg was written blind to the pictures, but not to the project. I chaired rounds 1 to 7 and know the picked ships and their faults. The blindness here is to this round's references and to the other legs, which is what the issue block asks for.
