# Graphics craft: the techniques a non game developer would not know to ask for

For Opus, building a Babylon.js hex fleet tactics game (phone first, desktop tier, many ships on screen, faction readable effects, cinematic camera, UI clarity). This is the craft layer underneath the art direction: the timing, grading, readability and accessibility choices that make a game look expensive, most of which a non developer would never think to request. Each technique names its Babylon.js recipe where one exists, its phone cost, and a source. Where Babylon has no built in version of a technique, that is stated plainly instead of implied.

## 1. Colour and light craft

**Tone mapping: ACES versus the standard curve.** Tone mapping converts HDR light values into displayable colours, and the curve chosen is the single biggest determinant of whether a scene looks filmic or washed out. ACES is the film industry curve: it rolls off highlights smoothly, desaturates them gently, and keeps bright beams and explosions from clipping to flat white. The standard (Reinhard style) curve is brighter and flatter, and reads cheaper on dark space scenes. Babylon exposes ACES through the image processing configuration: `pipeline.imageProcessing.toneMappingEnabled = true` with `toneMappingType = BABYLON.ImageProcessingConfiguration.TONEMAPPING_ACES`, alongside contrast and vignette controls. Pick ACES for the cinematic tier and keep it consistent across regions, or every plate and LUT decision downstream gets rejudged.
Phone cost: tone mapping is a cheap fullscreen pass; the cost is in HDR rendering itself (see the pipeline trap in the tool guides: with hdr=true you must set `pipeline.samples` or lose anti-aliasing).
Source: https://www.webcodingcenter.com/babylon-js/post-processes-and-rendering-pipelines--a-post-processed-look-for-booknest-s-storefront-window.html

**Bloom threshold and knee.** Bloom should fire only on things that are actually bright: beams, engine glows, explosions, shield flashes. The two knobs are `bloomThreshold` (only pixels brighter than this bloom) and `bloomWeight` (how strongly the bloom is added back); a widely used starting pair is threshold 0.7 and weight 0.5. The knee is the soft rolloff around the threshold: a hard threshold makes bloom flicker on and off as a ship crosses the line, so the blur kernel (bloomScale and kernel size) is what softens it in practice. Rule of thumb for this game: raise the threshold until the background plates stop glowing, then tune weight until beams read without white-out.
Phone cost: bloom is a blur over the frame, so keep the kernel small on the phone tier and reserve wide blooms for desktop.
Source: https://dev.to/elysiatools/8-babylonjs-examples-that-prove-webgl-has-arrived-8a5

**Colour grading and LUTs.** A look up table (LUT) remaps every colour in the frame, which is how a whole region gets a mood (cold belt, hot storm, sickly graveyard) without touching a single material. This project's visual expansion research already established per-region .3dl LUTs as six tiny files; the craft point is authorship: grade in a photo tool (Photoshop, DaVinci Resolve) on a screenshot of the actual game, export the LUT, and judge it on the phone screen, not the monitor. A LUT that looks moody on a desktop monitor often reads as mud on a phone.
Phone cost: a LUT pass is nearly free; authoring badly costs readability, not frames.
Source: https://doc.babylonjs.com/features/featuresDeepDive/materials/shaders/image_processing

**Exposure adaptation.** Real eyes adapt to brightness, and many engines simulate it by auto adjusting exposure. In a tactics game, do not. Exposure shifts change which ships are readable from one turn to the next, and a player misreading a shield state because the exposure moved is a gameplay bug wearing a graphics feature. Fix exposure per region and let the LUT do the mood work.
Phone cost: none, this is a decision, not a pass.

**Fog and depth cueing.** Fog is not weather here, it is a depth cue: distant ships fade slightly toward the background colour so the eye reads near versus far at a glance. Babylon offers four fog modes: `scene.fogMode = BABYLON.Scene.FOGMODE_LINEAR` with `fogStart` and `fogEnd`, or `FOGMODE_EXP` and `FOGMODE_EXP2` with `fogDensity`, plus `fogColor`. In space, use a very light linear fog (or a distance desaturation in the grade) and never a thick fog: the board must stay readable at full zoom out, and fog that eats ship silhouettes is a readability failure.
Phone cost: fog is nearly free; it is evaluated per fragment, so keep it out of the particle shader path if one is custom.
Source: https://waelyasmina.net/articles/babylon-js-tutorial-for-absolute-beginners/

## 2. Anti-aliasing craft

**MSAA versus FXAA versus TAA, and the Babylon reality.** Three tools, three prices. MSAA samples geometry edges at render time: sharp, expensive, and in Babylon it is `pipeline.samples` (4 is the usual value) once HDR is on. FXAA is a post-process blur over jagged edges: cheap, always available via `pipeline.fxaaEnabled`, but it softens fine detail and text. TAA reuses previous frames to accumulate sub-pixel samples: the best quality in modern engines, and the one with a signature failure, ghosting. Babylon ships a TAARenderingPipeline, but it is a separate pipeline, not part of DefaultRenderingPipeline. The default pipeline offers MSAA (pipeline.samples) and FXAA (pipeline.fxaaEnabled) only. If TAA is wanted, create the TAA pipeline before other post-processes, or set reprojectHistory and clampHistory to tame ghosting. On a phone GPU, MSAA or FXAA remains the right default; the TAA notes below explain why that is a survivable loss and what other engines do when they do use it.
Phone cost: MSAA samples 4 on desktop, FXAA or samples 2 on phone. Never both MSAA and FXAA at once; the double blur buys nothing.
Source: https://forum.babylonjs.com/t/enabling-hdr-in-defaultpipeline-disables-aa/14656

**TAA ghosting and its mitigations (for understanding, not for building).** Ghosting is the temporal history failing to keep up with fast motion: a fast ship leaves a faint trail because the previous frame's pixels are reprojected to the wrong place. Engines mitigate it four ways, in order of importance: per-pixel motion vectors (velocity buffers) so the history is reprojected correctly, especially for materials that move without moving their mesh; neighborhood clamping, which rejects any history sample outside the colour range of the current frame's 3x3 neighbourhood; history rejection on large lighting changes; and higher frame rate, which makes ghosting visibly weaker because the history converges faster. Playdead's open source temporal anti-aliasing for INSIDE documents the full recipe (jitter, velocity buffer, reprojection, clamping, blend) and is the cleanest study source. For this game the actionable translation is: fast ships plus a trail effect will always read as ghosting to a player, so make trails deliberate (a designed TrailMesh or beam trail) rather than accidental.
Phone cost: TAA is a history buffer plus a velocity pass; on a phone GPU it is usually the wrong trade, which is another reason MSAA or FXAA is the right call here.
Sources: https://docs.godotengine.org/en/4.3/tutorials/3d/3d_antialiasing.html, https://deepwiki.com/playdeadgames/temporal, http://stevekarolewics.com/articles/anti-ghosting-taa.html

## 3. Motion and impact craft

**Anticipation, hit-stop, impact frames.** The three beats that make a hit feel heavy, from the game feel tradition (Jonasson and Purho's 2012 GDC talk is the origin point). Anticipation is a wind-up before the hit: a turret tracking, a charge glow building, 0.3 to 0.6 seconds. Hit-stop is a brief freeze at the moment of impact: 3 to 6 frames (0.05 to 0.1 seconds) where everything pauses, which the brain reads as weight. An impact frame is a 1 to 3 frame flash or white frame at the contact point. None of these change the simulation; all of them change whether the player believes it. In a turn based game they translate directly into animation beats: a volley is anticipation (track and charge), impact (flash, shake, hit-stop pause), aftermath (debris, smoke, damage numbers).
Phone cost: zero GPU cost; these are timing choices in the animation system. The risk is pacing: too much hit-stop in a 20 ship battle makes the game feel slow, so scale hit-stop by event size (capital kills get it, fighter pings do not).
Sources: https://www.manuelsanchezdev.com/blog/game-feel-principles-animated-godot, https://fishfolk.github.io/jumpy-legacy-docs/juice.html

**Screen shake, done right.** Shake is the most powerful and most abused juice technique. Good shake is short (0.1 to 0.3 seconds for ordinary hits, up to 0.5 for a capital kill), decays from its peak, scales with the event, has a directional bias away from the impact, and is built from smooth noise (Perlin or sine) rather than random offsets, which read as jarring. Bad shake lasts too long, stays at constant intensity, fires on every action, or moves the screen far enough to lose the board. Always ship a reduce or disable toggle; shake is a motion sickness vector, not just a preference.
Phone cost: none; it is a camera offset. The cost is readability, so never shake during the player's own aiming or targeting.
Source: https://www.slashskill.com/game-feel-and-juice-the-complete-guide-to-making-your-game-satisfying/

**Camera push-ins and hit flashes.** A slow push-in (about 1.2 seconds) on a Command activation or a boss arrival tells the player "this matters" before the event resolves; the project's cinematic section already uses this timing. Hit flashes are the damage feedback counterpart: the struck ship flashes white for a frame or two, and the health bar sells the hit by dropping the fill immediately but draining a ghost bar (often orange) a beat later, so the eye catches the damage even when it is watching the target, not the HUD.
Phone cost: camera moves are free; the ghost bar is a UI animation, not a render cost.
Source: https://www.youtube.com/shorts/LeTA_m0ABGg

## 4. Readability systems

**Silhouette, rim light, outlines.** At full zoom out, a ship is a handful of pixels, and colour alone cannot carry identity. Three layers, in order of cheapness: shape language (each class reads by outline alone: wedge, ring, fin, cathedral), rim light (a Fresnel term that brightens edges facing away from the camera; in Babylon this is a Node Material fresnel block, or a HighlightLayer), and outlines (Babylon's HighlightLayer draws a coloured halo around chosen meshes using the stencil buffer, so the engine must be created with `{ stencil: true }`, then `new BABYLON.HighlightLayer('outline', scene).addMesh(mesh, color)`; `outerGlow` and `innerGlow` plus blur sizes tune it, and a newer SelectionOutlineLayer draws a crisp editor style outline). Use outlines sparingly: selection and targeting only. If every ship has an outline, no ship does.
Phone cost: HighlightLayer costs extra blurred render targets; reserve it for the selected and targeted ships, not the fleet.
Sources: https://www.webcodingcenter.com/babylon-js/post-processes-and-rendering-pipelines--the-glow-layer-and-highlight-layer.html, https://www.webcodingcenter.com/babylon-js/the-node-material-editor--a-node-material-spine-highlight-for-booknest-s-shelves.html

**Contrast budgets.** The project's existing rule (no background pixel inside the board brighter than 40 percent of an unlit hull facet) is the right shape: a contrast budget, stated as a number, enforced per region. Extend it: the faintest ship colour against the brightest background pixel in its region must clear a stated ratio, checked on a phone screenshot, not a monitor. Faction identity must also survive colour loss: never encode meaning with colour alone, which is the bridge to section 8.

## 5. LOD and streaming craft

**LOD levels and popping.** Babylon's `mesh.addLODLevel(distance, simplerMesh)` swaps in a lighter mesh beyond a distance, and `addLODLevel(distance, null)` stops drawing entirely; `mesh.useLODScreenCoverage = true` makes thresholds screen fractions (0 to 1) instead of distances, which is scale independent and usually the better choice for a game with ships of very different sizes. Instances inherit their source's levels. Build the lighter meshes offline (Blender Decimate, glTF-Transform simplify). Babylon has no built in LOD crossfade, so popping is mitigated three ways: pick thresholds where the silhouette barely changes, add hysteresis (different thresholds for zooming in versus out, so the mesh does not flicker at the boundary), or fade opacity briefly across the swap on the few hero ships where popping would be noticed.
Phone cost: LOD is a pure win; the cost is authoring time and the memory of holding two meshes per hull, which the phone tier already budgets for.
Sources: https://www.webcodingcenter.com/babylon-js/rendering-many-objects-efficiently--level-of-detail-with-addlodlevel.html, https://forum.babylonjs.com/t/some-question-about-using-lod-to-simplify-mesh/56598

**Texture streaming and mip bias.** The phone tier does not need 4K textures on a ship that covers 40 pixels. The practical rules: ship textures at the resolution the zoom-out view actually shows, anisotropy capped on mobile, and KTX2 compression (already in the tool guides) doing the heavy lifting. Mip bias (forcing a sharper or blurrier mip level) is a tuning knob for shimmering versus blur on distant ships; use it only if distant hulls visibly shimmer, and prefer fixing the texture (less high frequency detail) over biasing the sampler.

**Particle overdraw budgets.** Overdraw is the same pixel shaded many times because transparent particles overlap, and it is the classic phone killer: a particle system that is cheap in isolation becomes devastating when twenty explosions overlap. The diagnostic is simple: if frame rate drops when looking directly at effects and recovers when zooming out so the particles occupy fewer pixels, the bottleneck is fill rate, not particle count. The rules: trim transparent borders off particle textures (empty pixels still cost shading), prefer additive blending for sparks and fire (cheaper than alpha blending), avoid large layers of faint smoke, cap simultaneous systems per family, and treat an overdraw ratio consistently above about 2.0 (every pixel shaded twice) as the budget line, per mobile GPU guidance. Measure with the engine's profiling tools and with Spector.js frame captures, not by feel.
Phone cost: this is the phone cost. Overdraw is the first thing to cut on the phone tier, before particle counts.
Sources: https://bugnet.io/blog/debugging-particle-system-performance-issues, https://blog.imaginationtech.com/improving-particle-systems-on-powervr/, https://ozlin.info/2026/03/implementing-particle-systems-for-visual-effects/

## 6. UI motion craft

**Easing and duration.** The rules that separate polished UI from robotic UI: entrances ease out (fast start, soft landing), exits ease in, paired elements share easing and duration, durations stay under 300 ms (micro interactions 100 to 150 ms, standard controls 150 to 250 ms, modals and drawers 200 to 300 ms), and linear motion is reserved for continuous loops like spinners. A good default curve is cubic-bezier(0.4, 0, 0.2, 1). Animate transform and opacity only; animating layout properties (width, top, left) forces re-layout every frame and stutters on phones. Every animation must be interruptible: a player tapping twice should not lock the UI until the first animation finishes.
Phone cost: transform and opacity animations are compositor cheap; layout animations are not.
Sources: https://gist.github.com/uxderrick/07b81ca63932865ef1a7dc94fbe07838, https://namethatui.com/web/easing

**Feedback timing and HUD fade.** A button must acknowledge a press within about 100 ms or it feels dead, even if the action itself takes longer; the acknowledgement can be a scale dip, a sound, or both. During big events (capital kills, boss arrivals), fade the HUD out briefly so the cinematic moment owns the screen, then bring it back; the project's Sins-derived section already uses this, and the craft point is that the fade is what makes the moment feel directed rather than just loud.
Phone cost: none beyond the animations themselves.

## 7. Audio as effects craft

**Ducking.** When an important sound plays, everything else dips. In middleware this is sidechain compression (one bus's level drives another bus's gain); in a web game without middleware, implement it as explicit gain automation: on a flagship kill or a stinger, duck music by 6 to 12 dB for the duration, then release smoothly. The priority order is voice first, then gameplay-critical effects, then music last. Ducking is what keeps a big explosion from burying the sound that tells the player what just happened.
Phone cost: none; it is gain math.
Sources: https://qa.fmod.com/t/ducking-background-music-for-other-audio/13095, https://www.audiokinetic.com/en/courses/wwise201/?source=wwise201&id=lesson_8_mixing_side_chaining_stingers

**Stingers and adaptive music.** A stinger is a short musical phrase triggered by an event (boss arrival, flagship kill, victory) that plays regardless of the music underneath; sidechaining the music under it is what guarantees it is heard. Adaptive music has two mechanisms: vertical layering (one piece written as stems, drums, bass, brass, added or removed as intensity rises and falls, all sharing tempo and key so changes feel natural) and horizontal resequencing (the music jumps between sections at bar or beat boundaries, through a short transition, for bigger mood changes). For a tactics game, vertical layering maps cleanly onto battle intensity (add percussion when the enemy commits, brass when the boss arrives), and resequencing maps onto campaign state (menu, battle, victory). A tactics game needs very little music; it needs the right three seconds at the right moment.
Phone cost: stems cost memory, not CPU; keep the stem count small on the phone tier.
Source: http://dev.to/aplusgamesstudio/video-game-music-how-it-is-composed-and-implemented-1969

**UI sound principles.** Every action gets a sound, sounds vary slightly per play (about 5 to 10 percent pitch variation) so repetition does not grate, and quiet moments are protected: if everything is loud, nothing is. UI sounds are feedback, not decoration, and a silent button is a broken button to the player's ear.

## 8. Accessibility craft

**Colorblind safe palettes.** Start from the Okabe-Ito palette, the standard colorblind safe categorical set (also published as the Wong palette): black #000000, orange #E69F00, sky blue #56B4E9, bluish green #009E73, yellow #F0E442, blue #0072B2, vermillion #D55E00, reddish purple #CC79A7. Two habits make any palette safer: never encode meaning with red versus green alone (pair colour with a second channel: shape, icon, label, position), and test with both a grayscale check (which tests luminance contrast) and a colour vision deficiency simulation for protanopia, deuteranopia and tritanopia (grayscale alone does not replace simulation). Note the trap in the set: only black and blue reach 4.5:1 contrast against white, and yellow is especially weak, so keep labels dark and test marks against their actual backgrounds. For this game the actionable version is: faction colours must be distinguishable under deuteranopia simulation, and faction identity must also be readable by silhouette and icon when colour is removed entirely.
Phone cost: none.
Sources: https://conceptviz.app/blog/scientific-color-palette-for-research-papers-and-posters, https://scifig.ai/blog/color-palettes-scientific-figures, https://www.audioeye.com/post/colorblind-friendly-palettes/

**Photosensitivity limits.** WCAG's seizures guideline is a hard safety rule, not a nicety: content must not flash more than three times in any one-second period (Level A allows flashes below the general and red flash thresholds; Level AAA requires no more than three flashes per second outright, and technique G19 recommends designing to at most three as the easily testable rule). Red flashes are the most dangerous colour. Practical rules for this game: cap screen flashes at three per second across all simultaneous effects, keep any flashing area small, never strobe a full-screen flash for a capital kill, and ship a reduce-flash setting that caps flash brightness and rate. Test with the Trace Center Photosensitive Epilepsy Analysis Tool (PEAT) or a Harding test where PEAT is restricted.
Phone cost: none; this is a design constraint on effects.
Sources: https://www.w3.org/WAI/GL/2013/WD-WCAG20-TECHS-20130117/G19, https://case.edu/accessibility/what-accessibility/guidelines/flashing-content, https://adobe.design/ideas/improving-product-accessibility-for-users-with-epilepsy

**Motion reduction.** Respect the platform's reduced-motion preference and ship an in-game toggle that disables or damps screen shake, camera push-ins, background animation and idle bobbing. Vestibular disorders and migraines are triggered by exactly the motion that makes a game feel alive to everyone else, so the toggle is not a niche feature; it is the difference between playable and unplayable for part of the audience. The toggle must also kill auto-playing background motion, not just shake.
Phone cost: none.

## What was not verified

Babylon's exact tone mapping type names beyond TONEMAPPING_ACES (shown in the linked example) were not re-opened against the official docs in this pass; confirm the standard curve constant against the installed Babylon version before wiring it. Babylon has a built-in TAA rendering pipeline (TAARenderingPipeline), separate from DefaultRenderingPipeline; the earlier 'not found' note is superseded. The .3dl LUT workflow is established in this project's own visual expansion research rather than re-verified against Babylon docs in this pass. Forum and third-party guides (webcodingcenter, tutorialspoint) are used for several Babylon recipes; treat those as community practice, and prefer the official docs where they cover the same setting.
