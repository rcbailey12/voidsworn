# The Glasswrights (Ithar): Species Design

## 1. The One Substance: Living Glass-Flesh

A Glasswright is made of one tissue only: **lumen-flesh**, a translucent living matrix that can exist anywhere on a single continuum from soft flesh to hard optical glass, depending on how its silica fibres are laid down inside it.

Every cell in the body secretes fine silica filaments into the flesh around it. Where those filaments stay loose, tangled, and blood-fed, the tissue is soft, pale, and half-translucent skin, muscle, and organ. Where the filaments align in parallel bundles, the tissue firms into tendon, cartilage, and flexible support. Where they fuse completely and the living cells retreat to the edges, the tissue becomes true clear glass, still grown, still part of the body, still fed at its root, but hard, transparent, and optically perfect.

There is no seam anywhere between meat and glass. Skin deepens into glass the way a fingernail deepens from a cuticle. A hard part is soft flesh that has finished aligning. A soft part is glass that has not yet, or never will, fuse.

Light is the third term, and it is not added from outside. Aligned silica fibres conduct light through the body the way nerves conduct signal. Loose fibres scatter it, which is why Glasswright flesh glows faintly and looks milky at the surface. Fused glass stores it. A Glasswright who has stood in bright light carries that light banked deep in the chest, the sail roots, and the thick glass of the brow, where it sits as a pale green-white luminance. That stored light is working reserve: it feeds the eyes in darkness, powers the slow fusing of new glass, and leaks colour involuntarily when the body is under strong emotion. The leak is a tell, like a flush. It is never controlled, never patterned, and never a language.

This one system explains the whole animal:

* The skin is pale and translucent because it is flesh full of unfused glass fibre.
* The skeleton is not bone. It is dense aligned fibre, glassy cartilage that flexes slightly and never fully hardens except in the brow, the fingertips, and the sail edges.
* The eyes are places where the head flesh has fused completely clear and been polished into lenses by the body's own hand.
* The sails are back skin where fusion has run to completion, sheet glass grown out of living flesh and still hinged by the unfused tissue at its root.
* The thread they weave is the same secretion every cell makes, drawn out in bulk from the wrist and hardened in air.

Cut a Glasswright anywhere and you find the same material at a different stage of alignment. That is the species at a glance: one organism, one substance, light moving through it.

## 2. Body Plan and Carriage

The Glasswright body plan is not upright and not bipedal. It is built low, wide, and forward, around a segmented thorax made for leaning over work.

**Torso.** The main body is a thorax of five overlapping segments, each segment a plate of firmer aligned flesh under translucent skin, hinged to the next by soft unfused tissue. The thorax tilts forward about thirty degrees from vertical at rest, and can tilt to near horizontal over a bench. There is no narrow waist and no separate abdomen. The rear segment thickens and anchors the support limbs and the sail roots. The chest segment is the thickest glass in the torso: stored light gathers there, visible deep under the surface, behind the sternum plate.

**Head.** The head sits low and forward on a short, thick neck that is mostly tendon bundle, so the face looks slightly up from the lowered thorax. The skull is wide and shallow, wider across the brow than it is tall, with no protruding nose, no external ears, and no jaw hinge of the familiar kind. The whole head is lumen-flesh at high alignment: the surface skin stays soft and mobile, the brow and cheek ridges are firm, and the eye zones are fully fused.

**Support limbs.** Locomotion and stance come from four support limbs, not two legs. Two large hind supports attach at the rear thorax segment. They are long, double jointed, and end in broad three-toed feet with fused glass pads. Two smaller fore-braces attach under the front thorax segment. At rest they plant on either side of the body like the front legs of a crouching animal. Walking uses all four in a slow alternating gait. This four point stance, combined with the four arms above it, gives the carriage its spider quality: eight limbs around a segmented body, the weight spread wide, the head low between the front braces. It stays on the near side of that line because the torso remains a single recognisable trunk, the face is carried forward and expressive, and the support limbs never multiply or lengthen in the adult base form. The silhouette reads as a weaver settled over its work, not as an arachnid.

**Posture at the bench.** At work the fore-braces plant wide, the hind supports fold, the thorax drops until it is almost level, and the sails fold forward overhead. In this stance a Glasswright can stay motionless for hours, all four arms free, body light feeding down through the sail roots into the work. Rising, walking, and turning are deliberate and slow. Glasswrights do not run well and do not try; a hurrying Glasswright shortens its stride and leans further forward rather than speeding up.

**Size.** Adults stand about 150 cm at the brow in walking posture and span about 180 cm across the planted fore-braces. At the bench the body occupies a wide low footprint, roughly 200 cm front to back with the sails furled.

## 3. The Face

The face is derived from the same fusion process as everything else, concentrated in one place.

**Eyes.** Two eyes only, set side by side in the front of the head, forward facing, level with each other. Each eye is a grown lens: a disc of fully fused glass about 4 cm across, deep green through its whole thickness, set flush in the surrounding flesh so its surface is continuous with the skin of the face. There is no white, no protruding eyeball, no rim or socket hardware. Behind the lens the interior of the head is dark, light gathering tissue. The green is the colour of the body's own glass at lens purity; every Glasswright has it, varying only slightly in depth. The lenses focus by tiny changes in the tension of the flesh behind them, pulled by the same aligned fibre that moves the rest of the face.

**Lids and brow, the expression system.** Each lens is covered by a fleshy lid that slides down from above and is opaque, milky lumen-flesh. Blinking is slow and complete. Expression lives above the eyes in a heavy brow made of three segmented plates of firm aligned flesh per side, hinged like the thorax segments. The brow can lower, lift at the inner or outer end, press flat, or ripple segment by segment. Together with lid aperture, this gives the full readable range: lowering the brow and narrowing the lids for scrutiny or anger, lifting and opening for attention, pressing flat for grief, an asymmetric lift for doubt. The cheeks and the flesh around the grille move only slightly. Anyone reading a Glasswright reads the brow first, as Glasswrights do themselves.

**Mouth and voice.** Low in the face, below and between the lenses, sits the speaking mouth: a short grille of five to seven thin vertical reeds of fused glass, set flush into the flesh so the surface of the face runs unbroken across it. The reeds are not held, worn, or attached. They are the fused front of the airway, grown in place, the way the lenses are the fused front of the sight organs. Air passes across them and the flesh behind them shapes normal speech in a normal speaking voice, pitched low and slightly resonant. The grille does not move visibly when speaking and is never used for anything else. It stays small, no wider than the space between the inner edges of the lenses, and dark in its gaps, so at portrait distance it reads as a mouth and nothing else.

**Emotion tell.** Under strong feeling, the stored light in the brow glass, the cheek ridges, and the upper chest shifts colour and brightness on its own: cooling toward blue white in fear, warming toward amber in anger or shame, dimming and greying in grief, brightening green white in relief or joy. The Glasswright cannot start it, stop it, or shape it. Others may read it the way one reads a blush. It carries no words and no code.

## 4. Arms, Hands, and Spinnerets

Four arms attach to the upper thorax in two pairs, all built from the same aligned fibre musculature, jointed with tendon and cartilage only.

**Working arms.** The upper pair are the working arms: thick, long, strong, attaching at the shoulder segment. They end in broad hands with four fingers and an opposed thumb, the fingertips capped in fused glass that serves as a natural hard tool for scoring, pressing, and testing a workpiece. These arms lift, hold, turn, and brace the work.

**Precision arms.** The lower pair are the precision arms: noticeably thinner, slightly shorter, attaching at the second thorax segment, with longer, finer fingers, five to a hand, the fingertips soft and unfused for maximum sensitivity. These arms do the fine placement, the holding of thread tension, and the reading of a surface by touch. A Glasswright at a bench typically works with all four hands on the piece at once, working arms bearing the weight, precision arms doing the detail.

**Spinnerets.** At the inner wrist of each precision arm, and at the heel of each working hand, sits a spinneret: a small flush cluster of pores in slightly raised flesh, no nozzle and no fitting. The spinneret expresses the body's silica secretion as a warm viscous strand that fuses into clear glass thread the moment it is drawn and exposed to light. Pulled slowly, it makes fine thread for weaving and repair. Pulled fast, it makes thicker rod. A Glasswright draws thread directly onto the workpiece, laying it with the precision fingers, building up lenses, hull vanes, and optical structures layer by layer, each new layer fusing to the last while it is still light fed from the maker's body. This is why their glass records faithfully: the thread carries the maker's stored light, and the light conditions at the moment of laying, into the finished structure.

The largest thing woven this way is a ship's sail vane, made by teams working a single sheet for weeks, each weaver's thread fusing into one continuous optical surface.

## 5. The Sails

From the rear thorax segment grow the sails: two primary vanes, one to each side of the spine line, with in most adults a smaller third pair of root vanes low behind them. Each sail is a sheet of the body's own fused glass held in a frame of its own thicker glass spars, which are simply places where the sheet's fibre aligned in bundles instead of a plane. The root of each sail is unfused living flesh, rich in tendon, so the whole vane can rotate, tilt, fold flat against the back, or fan open. The spars joint where they meet the root and at one mid joint per main spar, all tendon and cartilage, all grown. There are no hinges, pins, or rigging.

A working adult's sails are modest: each primary vane about 90 cm long and 45 cm at its widest, held mostly furled or half open. Furled, they lie layered flat along the back like closed fans and add almost nothing to the silhouette. Open at the bench, they arc forward over the thorax to form a canopy above the work.

**The focusing edge.** The leading edge of each primary vane is thickened and polished to a long lens curve. When the sail is fanned over the bench and angled to the light, that edge gathers ambient or stored light and throws it as a bright controlled line onto the workpiece. This is the sail's first purpose and the reason for its shape: every Glasswright carries a personal, adjustable work light and magnifying light guide that needs no equipment. The weaver tilts a sail a fraction, the light line moves across the piece, flaws in the thread show instantly, and the stored light in the maker's chest tops up the beam when the room light is poor.

**Away from the bench.** Open sails gather light for the body's reserve on long watches, on ship decks, and in transit. Half open, they shade the lens eyes in harsh glare. In command settings they are a readable posture signal of the ordinary animal kind: sails raised and open for address and attention, furled flat for deference, mourning, or work. They are never weapons, never flight surfaces, and never display plumage. A Glasswright whose sails are fully spread outside work or address is either gathering light in a dark place or very unwell.

**Relation to the fleet.** The sail vanes are the same material, grown the same way, as the clear crystal and silver vanes of Glasswright ships, which are woven by Glasswright hands at larger scale. The silver in both comes from the same feature: fibres that have fused, died back from the living matrix, and turned opaque silver white, used deliberately along edges and spars for strength. A Glasswright standing before their fleet is visibly made of what their ships are made of.

## 6. Variation Within the Species

All variation happens inside the one body plan and the one substance system. It expresses in two places only: the depth of glass fusion across the body, and the sail plan.

**Workers at the bench.** A working glasswright shows fusion where the trade puts it: heavy glass fingertips on the working hands, spinneret wrists slightly thickened and glassy, sails kept trimmed to working size with plain unfrosted edges, brow glass clear from constant expression use. Bench workers keep their sails furled most of the day and their stored light runs bright from constant exposure to the focusing edges. Stance at work is the deepest version of the weaver crouch.

**Age across three to four hundred years.** Fusion is cumulative. Glasswrights are born almost entirely soft, milky opaque infants with only the lens discs and reed grille fused, because those must work from birth. Through the first century the thorax segments firm, the sail sheets grow in and clear, and the fingertips harden. In the second century the sails reach full size, stored light deepens in the chest, and the skin takes on its adult translucency as fibre density rises. In the third century and beyond, fusion creeps: sail edges begin to **bloom**, developing frosted, rainbow fringed margins where the glass has fused, micro fractured, and re fused thousands of times; spars thicken; the brow plates grow heavier and slower; thread laying becomes finer even as the working arms lose some strength. Elders are therefore recognised instantly by their sails first: larger, bloom edged, a little worn and chipped at the margins, carried half open out of habit. Age never adds new organs, new eyes, or a new body plan.

**Admirals.** An admiral must read in one glance as the same animal as a bench worker, and in the next glance as a specific person. Identity lives in sail plan, sail wear, fusion depth, and brow character, nowhere else.

* **Tamsa of the Clear Aperture**, wing admiral: the broadest and clearest sails of the four, kept almost entirely free of bloom well into age, edges polished to exceptional transparency. Tamsa's fusion runs shallow in the torso, the chest light unusually bright and clean, the whole figure reading open and unclouded.
* **Saro of Long Focus**, extreme range lance admiral: sails long and narrow, drawn out past the usual proportions, the focusing edge of each primary vane extended into an unusually long unbroken lens curve. Saro's lens eyes sit a fraction deeper in fusion, their green darker, and the brow is held habitually low and still, a face built for looking at one distant thing.
* **Vell of the Third Mirror**, duellist: one primary vane, the left, carries a broad silver backed panel where the fibres have been deliberately died back to a mirror finish across the inner face, used to throw light and read reflections in single combat. The asymmetry is sail plan only. Vell's other sail stays plain working weight, and the precision arms show the heaviest fingertip wear of the four.
* **Brin of the Fourth Lens**, ward admiral: the shortest, broadest sails, edges thickly woven over with extra thread into reinforced rims, a guardian's working rig rather than a flyer's. Brin's chest fusion is the deepest of the four, the stored light sitting visibly lower and steadier behind a thick sternum plate, and the brow segments are the heaviest, giving a slow, immovable expression.

All four share the same face, the same four arms, the same stance, the same green lenses and flush reed grille. Put them in a row and the species speaks first, the sail plan second, the person third.

**The ancient elder.** In the oldest individuals, past roughly three hundred and fifty years, fusion and habit together produce the one permitted departure. The rear thorax segments lengthen, the hind supports lengthen with them, the fore-braces take more of the weight, and the body settles permanently into the full eight point weaver stance, sails huge, bloom edged to the spar, furled down the long back like a folded cloak. These ancients rarely leave their benches or archives, move seldom and slowly, and are tended by younger weavers. This fuller spider form belongs to extreme age alone. It is never the shape of a healthy working adult, never a caste, and never a separate kind.

## 7. Life and Craft

**Work.** Glasswrights are the optical engineers of their own civilisation, and the substance system is the reason. Because their thread carries light, their glass records light at full fidelity: a hull vane woven during a battle, or a lens laid down in a forge under particular conditions, holds that light as a replayable record that the forge can read back exactly. Their ships are built the same way their bodies are, woven thread over grown spars, and their sails are the small personal version of the vanes they build at ship scale. A Glasswright inspects work by putting their own body in the light path: sail edge to gather, lens eye to read, brow and chest glass catching the scatter. Craft skill is literally bodily. The best weavers have the clearest sails and the steadiest stored light, and everyone can see it.

**Clothing.** Glasswrights wear very little, by necessity. Cloth over the sails blocks light gathering, cloth over the spinnerets fouls the thread, and cloth over the chest dims the reserve. What they wear is woven from their own glass thread: open lattice aprons over the thorax front at the bench, light harnesses for carrying tools and workpieces against the segments, and, for formal address, a draped lattice mantle hung from the sail roots that leaves the sails themselves free. Rank and role, where shown at all, are shown in the weave pattern of the apron or mantle, never in metal, fittings, or uniform cloth.

**Movement and rest.** On foot they move in the four support gait, unhurried, wide, and very stable on broken or sloped ground. They swim poorly and climb well, the glass padded feet and four hands gripping in sequence. Sleep is taken in the bench crouch taken one step further: supports folded under, thorax lowered to the ground, sails furled forward over the whole body as a glass canopy, stored light dimming to a faint green white through the layered vanes. A sleeping Glasswright looks like a closed glass shelter with a light left on deep inside it. They sleep in short cycles through the day rather than one long night, waking to work whenever their reserve has recovered, which suits a people whose bodies run on gathered light.

**Temperament in the design.** Everything about the animal is patient, forward leaning, and made for sustained close attention: the low stance that does not tire, the four hands that never have to let go of the work, the light carried in the chest for the dark hours of a long task, the face that speaks plainly and shows its feelings only in brow and involuntary colour. They are a people who stayed flesh while their kin left it behind, and who watched the light they built go wrong. The grief is in the material itself: a body made of recorded light, carrying three to four hundred years of what it has seen, still laying that light down thread by thread into ships meant to remember it correctly.
