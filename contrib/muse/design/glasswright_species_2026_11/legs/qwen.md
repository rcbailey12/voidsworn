GLASSWRIGHT SPECIES DESIGN
I. The Substance System
The Glasswright body is one tissue: living silicate, continuous from the deepest thorax plate to the thinnest sail edge. There is no seam where flesh ends and glass begins, because there is no second material. The organism is a single biological silicate matrix that varies in three axes - crystallinity, hydration, and light-saturation - and every visible structure on the body is that one tissue expressing a different combination of those three states.
Crystallinity. Where the tissue orders into tight, regular lattice, it becomes what an outside observer calls glass: hard, transparent or translucent, optically precise. Where it stays loose, hydrated, and cellular, it reads as pale flesh: soft, yielding, faintly warm to the touch. Between those poles is a continuous gradient - cartilage, tendon, the flexible joints of the sails, the spinneret channels in the wrist. A Glasswright's elbow and a Glasswright's lens-eye are the same substance at different orders, the way a human's ear cartilage and femur are the same connective tissue at different densities. Nothing is bolted. Nothing is inlaid. The glass is grown, the flesh is grown, and they are the same growth.
Hydration. Water content governs flexibility. The thorax plates are near-anhydrous, hard, faintly ringing when struck. The inter-plate membranes, the inner surfaces of the limbs, the tissue around the eyes and mouth, are hydrated and supple. Hydration is not a weakness or a primitive state; it is the tissue's living condition, the medium through which nutrients and stored light move. A dehydrated Glasswright is a dying Glasswright.
Light-saturation. The tissue metabolises light. It absorbs, records, and stores photons at full optical fidelity the way a photosynthetic organism stores solar energy, but here the storage is literal memory: the lattice traps the wavefront. A Glasswright's body glows faintly from within - pale green-white, steady, like a banked furnace seen through frosted glass - because it is full of stored light. This is not bioluminescence in the chemical sense. It is the tissue's metabolic reserve made visible. When a Glasswright feels strong emotion, the stored light shifts colour involuntarily - a flush of amber for grief, a cold blue for focus, a brief rose for pleasure - because emotion alters the metabolic rate and the lattice re-emits at a different wavelength. The colour is a tell, not a language. They cannot control it. They speak with their voices.
The derivation rule. Every part of the body that follows in this document is this tissue. The lens-eyes are this tissue at maximum crystallinity and optical precision. The lids are this tissue at high hydration. The brow is this tissue in dense, segmented, semi-crystalline plates. The reed grille is this tissue drawn into thin parallel crystalline vanes. The spinnerets are this tissue in fine capillary channels. The sails are this tissue at its thinnest, most ordered, most optically active expression. The thorax plates are this tissue at its hardest and driest. The support limbs are this tissue in dense, load-bearing crystalline struts sheathed in hydrated tissue. If you cut any cross-section of a Glasswright, you find one material in gradient. There is no second substance anywhere.
II. Anatomy
Body Plan and Carriage
The Glasswright reads, at first glance, as a low, wide, multi-limbed weaver. The overall silhouette is not upright. It is a broad, segmented thorax carried close to the ground or the bench, with the head projecting forward and slightly upward from the thorax's leading edge, and four arms reaching forward and down toward the work. Two pairs of support limbs anchor the body from beneath. The total impression is of a spider at its loom: patient, planted, deliberate, all attention gathered at the front where the hands meet the workpiece.
The thorax is the body's centre of mass and its primary structure. It is segmented: five broad, overlapping plates of dense, near-anhydrous vitreous tissue, each plate faintly translucent, each glowing with deep stored light. Between the plates, narrow bands of hydrated tissue allow the thorax to flex, breathe (they do breathe; the hydrated bands expand and contract), and absorb shock. The segmentation is not decorative. It is structural, the way a lobster's carapace is structural, and it gives the thorax a lived, grown, articulated quality. The plates are not identical: the leading plate is broader and flatter, forming a kind of chest-shelf against which the workpiece rests during close weaving; the trailing plates narrow and deepen, housing the light-storage reserves that glow most visibly here, deep in the body's core.
The stance is low and wide. The support limbs - four of them, two forward, two aft - splay outward and plant firmly. They are not delicate. Each is a strut of dense crystalline tissue sheathed in a thinner layer of hydrated tissue for grip and cushion, jointed at two points by cartilaginous bends (the same tissue at intermediate crystallinity, not a different material). The feet are broad, flat, slightly splayed pads of the same tissue, textured with fine crystalline ridges for purchase on glass floors and bench surfaces. The stance is a weaver's stance: stable, low-centred, built to hold still for hours while the arms work. A Glasswright does not tower. A Glasswright settles.
The head projects from the thorax's leading edge on a short, thick neck - more a tapered extension of the thorax than a separate column. The neck is hydrated tissue, flexible enough to tilt and turn the head through a modest arc (roughly forty degrees side to side, twenty up and down), sturdy enough to hold the head steady during precision work. The head itself is not a separate block of crystal. It is the same vitreous tissue, denser and more crystalline toward the front where the eyes sit, more hydrated toward the back and underside where it meets the neck and where the grille sits. The skull shape is broad, slightly flattened top-to-bottom, wider than it is tall, with a gentle forward taper toward the face. It reads as grown, not carved: smooth, continuous, with the faint internal lattice of the crystalline regions visible as a subtle geometric texture beneath the surface, like frost patterns in window glass.
The overall body, head to trailing support limbs, is roughly one and a half times as long as it is wide, and about three-quarters as tall as it is long at the thorax. An adult Glasswright standing (settling) at full extension reaches roughly to a human hip in height, but spreads nearly two metres wide across the support limbs. They are not small, but they are not tall. They are broad, low, and present.
The Face
The face is the front plane of the head, slightly angled upward. It is not a mask applied to the skull. It is the skull's own front surface, shaped by the tissue's crystallinity gradient into functional features.
The eyes. Two grown lens-eyes, set side by side, forward-facing, spaced roughly one eye-width apart. Each eye is a living optical element: the vitreous tissue at its highest crystallinity and most precise lattice order, forming a smooth, slightly convex lens roughly three centimetres across. The colour is deep green - the specific green of light stored long and deep in the lattice, the green of the Glasswrights' oldest memories of their sun before the Upload killed it. The green is not paint or pigment. It is the tissue's storage signature, the wavelength at which this species' lattice most stably holds light. The eyes sit flush in the face. They do not protrude on stalks, do not recess into deep sockets, do not sit in metallic or bony rims. The surrounding tissue simply transitions from the crystalline order of the lens to the softer, more hydrated tissue of the face, with no hard border, like the way the cornea meets the white of a human eye but smoother, more continuous, more grown.
The eyes are not stacked, not ringed, not multiplied. Two. Side by side. Forward. This is the species' binocular arrangement, evolved for precision depth-judgement at bench distance: the focal range where glass thread is drawn and woven. They see in fine detail at close range and in broad, light-sensitive sweep at distance. They do not see in the dark; they see by the light they carry and the light around them.
The lids. Each eye has a lid: a fold of hydrated vitreous tissue that slides across the lens from the inner corner outward, slow and deliberate, lubricating and protecting the lens surface. The lids are the primary vehicle for expression alongside the brow. A slow blink is contemplation. A quick, tight blink is irritation or pain. Drooping lids are weariness or grief. Wide-open lids, the full lens exposed, are attention or surprise. The lid tissue is slightly more opaque than the lens, a paler green-white, and its edge is faintly thicker, giving it a visible line when closed.
The brow. Above and slightly forward of the eyes, a heavy segmented brow spans the width of the face. It is not a single plate but a series of five to seven dense, semi-crystalline segments arranged in a shallow arc, each segment a thickened ridge of the vitreous tissue at high crystallinity but not full lens-order - dense enough to be hard and protective, not ordered enough to be transparent. The segments are joined by narrow flexible bands of hydrated tissue, allowing the brow to shift: to draw inward (concentration, anger), to lift (surprise, inquiry), to flatten (calm, patience), to furrow asymmetrically (suspicion, doubt). The brow is the most expressive structure on the face. It is heavy, visible, and always in motion. It shades the eyes from stray light during bench work, which is its evolved function, but in social life it is the primary signal of intent and feeling. The segments are not identical: the central segments are broader, the outer ones narrower and slightly angled back, giving the brow a natural, grown taper.
The mouth. Set flush and low in the face, below and between the eyes, a short reed grille: a row of thin, parallel, closely spaced crystalline vanes, each roughly two millimetres wide and fifteen millimetres long, set into a shallow recess in the hydrated tissue of the lower face. The vanes are the vitreous tissue drawn fine and ordered, like the tines of a comb grown from the same substance as everything else. They sit flush with the face's surface. They do not protrude. They are not a tube, not a pipe, not a held object. They are a region of the face where the tissue has formed into parallel reeds, the way a region of the face has formed into the lens of an eye. The recess behind the vanes opens into the vocal cavity: a resonant chamber in the hydrated tissue of the throat where air is shaped into speech. Glasswrights speak normally, in a normal voice - warm, slightly resonant, with a faint harmonic undertone from the reed vibration. The grille is not a translator, not a synthesiser. It is a vocal structure, like a larynx and lips combined into one grown organ. When they speak, the vanes vibrate subtly. When they are silent, the vanes are still. The grille is small enough to read as a facial feature, not a device.
Colour as tell. The glass in the face - the lens-eyes, the brow segments, the reed grille, the crystalline lattice visible beneath the skin - all carry stored light, and that light shifts with emotion. This is involuntary, metabolic, uncontrollable. A Glasswright cannot hide a flush of amber grief in the brow or a cold blue focus in the lens. This is not speech. They do not modulate it, do not encode meaning in it, cannot lie with it. It is a blush, a pallor, a flush. Other Glasswrights read it the way humans read a flushed cheek or a drained face: as information, but not as words.
The Four Arms and Spinnerets
Four arms, arranged in two pairs from the thorax's leading segments. The upper pair are the working arms: longer, stronger, with broader hands and more range of motion. They reach, hold, position, and stabilise the workpiece. The lower pair are the precision arms: shorter, finer, with narrower hands and more degrees of freedom in the fingers. They draw, weave, and place glass thread. All four arms are the same vitreous tissue in gradient: dense and crystalline in the structural bones (which are grown struts, not separate skeletons), hydrated and flexible at the joints (cartilage bends, tendon cords - all the same tissue at intermediate states), and finished in hands where the tissue is at its most dexterous and sensitive.
The working arms' hands are broad, four-fingered, with thick pads of hydrated tissue on the palms for gripping hot or sharp glass without damage. The precision arms' hands are narrow, five-fingered, with finer tips and a lighter touch. The fingers of all four hands end in slightly hardened tips - the tissue at its most crystalline, like grown thimbles - for pressing and shaping glass thread without deforming it.
The spinnerets. On the inner face of each precision wrist, just below the palm, a spinneret: a small cluster of fine capillary channels in the vitreous tissue, each channel no wider than a coarse thread, opening through a slightly raised, smooth-lipped pore. The Glasswright draws glass thread from the spinneret by extending the wrist and pulling: the tissue inside the channel heats (metabolically, from stored light released as thermal energy), softens, and extrudes as a fine, continuous strand of molten-then-cooling glass. The thread solidifies in the air within a second, drawn to the desired thickness by the speed and distance of the pull. The spinneret is not a tool held in the hand. It is a region of the wrist, grown, integral, always present. A Glasswright's wrist is a wrist with a spinneret in it, the way a human's forearm is an arm with a pulse in it. The glass thread they draw is the same substance as their own body, extruded and cooled into a working medium. They weave their own flesh into their work.
The spinnerets are used for all glasswork: drawing thread for weaving, laying fine lines for optical circuits, joining components, repairing, and - on the largest scale - weaving the crystal sail vanes. A ship's sail vane is the biggest thing a Glasswright weaves, and it is woven from their own substance, thread by thread, onto a grown frame.

III. The Sails
The sails are grown crystal vanes: the vitreous tissue at its thinnest, most extended, and most optically ordered expression. They are not membrane, not fabric, not webbing. They are the same substance as the thorax plates and the lens-eyes, drawn out into broad, thin, slightly curved sheets of high-order crystalline lattice, translucent to transparent, catching and splitting light into faint rainbow fringes at their edges - the same optical signature as the Glasswright ships' crystal and silver vanes. The body and the fleet are one material culture because they are one material.
Structure. Each sail vane is a single grown sheet, roughly triangular in plan, broader at the base where it attaches to the thorax and tapering toward a free outer edge. The base attachment is a thickened ridge of crystalline tissue - the vane's root - set into a socket in the upper thorax plates, jointed by a dense cartilaginous bend (the same tissue at intermediate crystallinity) that allows the vane to pivot, tilt, and furl. There are no masts, no rigging, no hardware, no brackets. The joint is grown tendon and cartilage, flexible and strong, and it moves the way a shoulder moves: by the tissue's own elasticity and the Glasswright's muscular (hydrated-tissue) control. Each Glasswright carries a sail plan of four to six vanes, arranged in a shallow fan across the upper thorax: two larger primary vanes flanking the midline, and two to four smaller secondary vanes set slightly lower and farther back. The vanes overlap slightly at their bases, forming a layered array.
At the bench. The sails are working rigs. When a Glasswright settles at the bench to weave, the vanes furl: they fold inward and downward along the back, tucking close to the thorax, reducing the profile so the arms have full range and the vanes do not catch on the work. One vane - always the leading secondary vane on the dominant side - remains partially extended, angled forward and down, its outer edge curved and ground to a fine focusing curve. This edge catches ambient light (forge-light, stored-light from the bench, natural light from a viewport) and focuses it into a tight, bright line on the workpiece. This is the work-light: not a lamp, not a beam from the mouth, but a focused edge of the sail, directing light exactly where the hands need it. The Glasswright adjusts the angle of this vane by small shifts of the thorax and shoulder joint, tracking the work as the hands move. At the bench, the sail is a tool, and the Glasswright uses it the way a jeweller uses a loupe: constantly, automatically, without thought.
Away from the bench. When not working, the sails serve two purposes. First, light collection: the vanes spread to catch and absorb light, feeding the tissue's metabolic storage. A Glasswright at rest in bright light will spread the vanes wide, angling them to the source, and the stored-light glow in the thorax will slowly brighten as the lattice charges. Second, thermal regulation: the thin crystalline sheets radiate excess heat. In the close warmth of a forge or a ship's interior, the vanes will half-spread to vent. The sails are not wings. They do not generate lift. They are not for flight. They are optical and thermal organs, grown for the work and for the body's maintenance, and they read as practical, modest, and integrated: a working glasswright's light-rig, not a display.
Appearance. The vanes are clear to very faintly silver-tinted, with the rainbow-edge fringe where light splits at the thin outer margin. They are not opaque, not coloured, not decorated. They glow faintly with stored light from within, the same pale green-white as the rest of the body, but brighter at the focusing edge where the lattice is densest. They are modest in size: fully extended, the largest primary vane reaches roughly sixty centimetres from base to tip, no more. They do not dwarf the body. They are proportional, functional, and always look as though they belong to the creature that grew them, because they did.
IV. Variation Within One Species
Bench Workers
The baseline Glasswright - the weaver at the bench, the optical engineer shaping lenses and recording arrays - is as described above. Four to six vanes, modest in size, clean-edged, bright and functional. The glass infusion is moderate: the crystalline regions are well-defined but not dominant, and the stored-light glow is steady and even. The body is in its working prime: hydrated tissue supple, joints fluid, spinnerets producing clean, even thread. This is the species at its most common and most visible. The bench worker is not lesser than the admiral. The bench worker is the species.
The Age Spectrum
Glasswrights live three to four hundred years. The substance system ages the way all living tissue ages: it accumulates, stiffens, deepens, and eventually wears.
Youth (first sixty years). The tissue is highly hydrated, flexible, and only partially crystallised. The lens-eyes are not yet at full optical density; the brow segments are smaller and fewer (five, not seven); the sail vanes are small, thin, and slightly flexible, not yet fully rigid. The stored-light glow is faint and unsteady, flickering with the high metabolic rate of growth. Young Glasswrights are quicker, more restless, less precise. Their glass thread is finer but less consistent. They are apprentices.
Working maturity (sixty to two hundred and fifty years). Full crystallisation. The tissue reaches its stable adult state: dense plates, precise lenses, full brow, four to six vanes at full size and rigidity. The stored-light glow is steady, deep, and even. The spinnerets produce consistent, strong thread. This is the long middle life: the master weaver, the fleet engineer, the admiral in their prime.
Elders (two hundred and fifty to four hundred years). The tissue deepens and blooms. Crystallinity increases further: the plates thicken, the brow segments grow denser and may add an eighth or ninth segment, and the sail vanes develop bloom-edges - the outer margins thicken, roughen, and fracture into fine crystalline projections, like frost spreading on glass. The bloom is not damage; it is the lattice continuing to order itself over centuries, growing more complex at the edges. The stored-light glow deepens from green-white to a richer, more saturated green, and the colour-shifts of emotion become slower and more pronounced, like deep water changing colour. The vanes grow larger with age - not dramatically, but noticeably: an elder's sail plan may span twenty percent wider than a young adult's. The vanes also wear: centuries of furling, spreading, catching forge-light leave fine scratches and a slight softening of the focusing edge. An elder's sails are bigger, bloom-edged, and worn, and they read as a life's work in glass. The body itself slows: the hydrated tissue thins slightly, the joints stiffen, the spinneret thread grows finer but slower. Elders work less and teach more, or take on the largest, slowest commissions: the great recording arrays, the deep-hull optics of capital ships.
The Four Admirals
The admirals are not a different species. They are Glasswrights whose sail plans and glass infusion have been shaped by their roles, the way a human blacksmith's arms differ from a human archer's. The substance is the same. The expression varies.
Tamsa of the Clear Aperture, Wing Admiral. Tamsa commands the fast wing: the scouts, the screen, the moving edge of the fleet. Her sail plan is six vanes, the maximum, all of moderate size, tightly jointed for quick furl and spread. The vanes are clean-edged, bright, and highly transparent - clear crystal with strong silver fringe - optimised for rapid light collection on the move. Her glass infusion is moderate and even; her glow is bright and steady. She reads as alert, quick, practical. Her brow is seven-segmented, her lenses a clear, vivid green. Her spinnerets are fine and fast. She is a weaver of speed.
Saro of Long Focus, Lance Admiral. Saro commands the extreme-range lances: the deep optics, the long burns, the patient kill at distance. His sail plan is four vanes, but the two primaries are longer and narrower than standard, with a deeper focusing curve at the edge, ground over decades to a precise optical profile. They are less broad, less showy, but their focusing edge is the finest on any living Glasswright. His glass infusion runs deep: the crystalline regions extend further into the hydrated tissue, and his stored-light glow is concentrated, intense, visible even through the thorax plates. His brow is heavy, seven-segmented, drawn perpetually inward in concentration. His lenses are the deepest green of the four admirals: old light, long stored. He reads as still, focused, patient. His spinnerets are slow and precise, drawing thread for the finest optical work.
Vell of the Third Mirror, Duellist. Vell fights close and fast: single-ship actions, boarding optics, mirror-to-mirror exchanges at knife range. Her sail plan is five vanes, asymmetric: the port-side vanes are slightly larger and more heavily jointed, armoured by extra crystalline density at their bases, because she leads with her left side in a duel. The vanes are not the clearest; they carry more internal fracture patterns, more stored-light scatter, from decades of absorbing and re-emitting combat light. Her glass infusion is uneven, denser on the leading side, and her glow is warmer, more amber-shifted than the others, because she runs hot and fast. Her brow is seven-segmented but the outer segments are chipped and regrown, rougher. Her lenses are green with faint amber threading. She reads as scarred, quick, intense. Her spinnerets are fast and strong, built for repair under fire.
Brin of the Fourth Lens, Ward Admiral. Brin commands the defensive screen: the mirrors that turn incoming light, the lenses that spread and dissipate, the wards that protect the fleet's body. His sail plan is six vanes, broad and slightly cupped, with a concave focusing geometry rather than the standard convex edge - they spread light rather than concentrate it. The vanes are the most silver-tinted of the four admirals, with strong rainbow fringe, because their lattice is tuned to reflect and redirect rather than absorb. His glass infusion is the most even and the most extensive: nearly the entire thorax surface is crystalline, and his glow is broad, soft, and steady, a wide pale-green wash rather than a concentrated point. His brow is eight-segmented, broad, calm. His lenses are green with a silver sheen. He reads as steady, wide, sheltering. His spinnerets are steady and even, built for laying broad reflective weaves.
All four read as Glasswrights. All four read as one organism. They differ in sail geometry, glass depth, glow character, and the small scars and asymmetries of their roles. They do not differ in species, in base anatomy, in the number of eyes, in the structure of the face, or in the substance of their bodies.
The Ancient Elder
Beyond four hundred years, a Glasswright does not die of old age in a single moment. The tissue continues to crystallise, to order, to grow. The hydrated fraction thins. The crystalline fraction deepens and extends. The segmentation of the thorax becomes more pronounced as the plates thicken and the inter-plate bands narrow. The support limbs grow denser, more strut-like, more planted. The arms shorten slightly relative to the thorax as the body's centre of gravity settles lower and wider.
This is where the fuller spider body lives, and only here. An ancient elder - five hundred years, six hundred, the oldest living Glasswrights - begins to read as more spider than weaver. The thorax elongates by one or two additional segments. The support limbs become more prominent, more angular, more load-bearing. The stance drops lower, spreads wider. The arms, while still four and still functional, are proportionally smaller against the greater thorax. The sail plan is vast: eight or more vanes, heavily bloom-edged, worn thin in places, thick and rough in others, spanning nearly the full width of the body. The glow is deep, saturated, slow-pulsing. The face is the same face - two green lens-eyes, segmented brow, flush reed grille - but set on a broader, heavier skull, the brow segments nine or ten, the lenses darker green, almost forest-deep.
This is not a different creature. It is the same substance system, given more time to express itself. The ancient elder is what the Glasswright body becomes when the tissue has had centuries to keep ordering, keep crystallising, keep growing. It is striking, slightly alien even to other Glasswrights, and it is reserved for the deepest old. No Glasswright under four hundred years should read this way. The full spider silhouette - the eight-point stance over the bench, the elongated thorax, the dominant support limbs - is the elder's body, and the elder's alone. It is not monstrous. It is old. It is what a weaver looks like after six centuries at the loom.
V. Life and Craft
Work
The Glasswrights are the Lattice's optical engineers, and they stayed flesh when the rest uploaded. Their work is shaping glass: growing it, weaving it, grinding it, recording light into it, and reading the light back. Every Glasswright is a weaver first. The spinneret is the species' defining tool, and the act of drawing glass thread from one's own wrist and laying it onto a workpiece is the fundamental gesture of their culture. Children learn to draw thread before they learn to speak in full sentences. The first object a young Glasswright weaves is traditionally a small lens, imperfect, kept for life.
At the bench, a Glasswright settles into the low wide stance, furls the sails except the focusing vane, and works. The four arms move in coordination: the working arms hold and position the workpiece, the precision arms draw and weave. The spinnerets produce thread; the fingers place it. The focusing vane tracks the work, keeping the light-line on the join. A master weaver can draw four threads simultaneously, two from each precision wrist, and braid them in the air before they cool. The work is slow, precise, and deeply physical: the Glasswright's body is the jig, the clamp, and the tool. They do not use external hardware. They do not use brackets, clamps, or mechanical aids. The hands, the spinnerets, and the sail-light are the entire toolkit.
The glass they weave is optical memory. A recording array is a woven lattice that traps wavefronts: light enters, the lattice stores it at full fidelity, and the forge can read the replay later. The Glasswrights' ship hulls are woven this way: clear crystal and silver, faceted edges splitting light into rainbow fringes, the hull itself a recording surface that holds the light of every battle the ship has seen. The body does the same thing at small scale. The stored light in a Glasswright's thorax is not abstract energy. It is memory. The oldest Glasswrights carry centuries of light inside them: the light of the sun before the Upload killed it, the light of the first forges, the light of battles they wove through. They glow with their own history.
Clothing
Glasswrights do not wear clothing in the human sense. The body is the garment. The vitreous tissue is the surface, and it is not hidden. However, working Glasswrights wear aprons: broad, flexible sheets of woven glass-fibre, draped over the thorax and tied at the inter-plate bands, protecting the hydrated tissue of the belly and inner limbs from forge-heat and stray thread. The apron is the same material as the body - woven glass - but it is a made object, not a grown one, and it is replaceable. Admirals wear longer aprons, finer-woven, sometimes with the fleet's sigil recorded in the weave as a permanent optical inscription. Beyond the apron, there is no clothing. The sails are not clothing. The brow is not a hat. The body is worn as it grows.
Movement
Glasswrights walk on the four support limbs in a slow, deliberate, eight-beat gait: fore-left, aft-right, fore-right, aft-left, repeating. The gait is stable, unhurried, and silent - the crystalline foot-pads grip glass and metal floors without clicking. They do not run. They do not need to. In urgent situations, the gait quickens to a rapid trot, but the body plan does not support speed. They are built for stillness and precision, not chase. On ships, they move through wide, low corridors designed for their spread, and they grip the deck with the foot-pad ridges in zero gravity, anchoring before they work.
When they need to reach a high place, they do not climb. They are lifted, or they weave a temporary glass ladder and ascend it slowly. The body is not built for verticality. The low, wide, planted stance is the natural posture, and a Glasswright standing fully upright on two limbs is unstable, uncomfortable, and rare.
Rest
Glasswrights rest by settling: folding the sails tight along the back, drawing the arms inward, lowering the thorax until the belly-plate rests on the ground or the bench, and dimming the stored-light glow to a low, slow pulse. They do not sleep in the mammalian sense. They enter a low-metabolic state in which the tissue repairs, the lattice re-orders, and the stored light cycles slowly through the body. A resting Glasswright is still, silent, and faintly glowing, like a banked coal. They rest in groups when possible, settling in clusters at the edges of the forge or the ship's common hall, their glows dimming in rough synchrony. The sight of a dozen Glasswrights settled in rest, their sail-vanes folded, their thorax plates rising and falling with slow breath, is the species at its most peaceful and most alien: a field of low, broad, softly luminous weavers, still as stones, warm as glass.
Grief
The Glasswrights carry two guilts: the sun, which their machine killed, and the Fleshwrights, their own splinter, whom they made and then could not save. These are not abstract history. They are within living memory. Two or three generations separate the oldest living Glasswrights from the ones who built the Lattice, who tried to stop the Upload, who watched the sun go dark. The grief is in the body. The stored light in an elder's thorax includes the light of that sun, the green-white of a star that no longer exists, held in the lattice of a living being who remembers it. The colour-shifts of emotion run deep and slow in the old, and grief is the most common colour: a long, slow amber that settles over the plates and the sails and the face like a stain. They do not speak of it often. They weave it into the glass. The recording arrays they build for the fleet are not just tactical records. They are acts of memory, of holding the light against the dark. Every Glasswright knows this. Every Glasswright carries it in the lattice.
VI. Design Constraints
This is a single organism, not an assembly. The substance system governs every part. If a component cannot be explained as the vitreous tissue in a specific state of crystallinity, hydration, and light-saturation, it does not belong in the design.
The face has two eyes. Not more. Not stacked. Not ringed. Not multiplied. Two forward lens-eyes, side by side, deep green, flush in the flesh. The brow carries expression. The reed grille sits flush in the lower face and speaks in a normal voice. The grille is not held, not inserted, not a tube. It is grown.
The body is not human, not grey alien, not elf. The body plan is low, wide, segmented, multi-limbed, and reads as alien in its architecture. The alienness is in the anatomy, not in decoration. No amount of glass effect on a human silhouette produces a Glasswright.
There is no hardware. No brass, no brackets, no aperture rims, no jeweller's loupes, no camera mechanisms, no steampunk. Every joint is grown tendon and cartilage. Every tool is grown. The spinneret is a wrist. The sail is a vane. The lens is an eye. Nothing is attached. Everything is continuous.
The sails are not naval. No masts, no rigging, no cloth, no keels. The sails are grown crystal vanes, jointed, furling, practical, modest. They are working light-rigs, not ship sails. They echo the fleet's crystal and silver vanes because they are the same substance and the same culture.
The body is not a construct. It is not crystal shaped into a person. It is not an Eclipse upload, not a machine, not a statue. It is a living being, warm, breathing, growing, aging, and dying. The glass is alive. The light is metabolic. The grief is real.
Colour in the glass is emotion, not language. It cannot be controlled, encoded, or spoken. It is a blush. The voice speaks. The glass feels.
No other faction's motifs appear here. No archive imagery, no crystal-construct bodies, no song-words, no data-registries, no oaths, no trophies, no scrap. This is light, glass, and grief. This is the Glasswrights. Nothing else.
