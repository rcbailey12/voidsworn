# The Glasswrights (Ithar): Species Design

## 1. The substance: lumen-flesh

A Glasswright is made of one tissue only: lumen-flesh, a living silicate matrix that runs on a single continuum from soft flesh to hard optical glass. There is no seam where flesh ends and glass begins, because there is no second material. What changes from place to place on the body is the tissue's state, set by three things:

- **Crystallinity.** Where the tissue orders into a tight, regular lattice, it becomes glass: hard, transparent or translucent, optically precise. Where it stays loose and cellular, it reads as pale flesh: soft, yielding, faintly warm. Between the poles lies a continuous gradient, and the gradient is the joints, the tendons, the cartilage, the spinneret channels. An elbow and a lens-eye are the same substance at different orders.
- **Hydration.** Water content governs flexibility. The thorax plates are nearly dry and ring faintly when struck. The membranes between plates, the inner limbs, the tissue around the eyes and mouth, are hydrated and supple. Hydration is the tissue's living condition, the medium nutrients and stored light move through. A dehydrated Glasswright is a dying one.
- **Light-saturation.** The tissue metabolises light. It absorbs, records, and stores light at full optical fidelity: the lattice traps the wavefront, so storage is literal memory. A Glasswright glows faintly from within, pale green-white and steady, because it is full of stored light. This is metabolic reserve made visible, not chemical bioluminescence.

Light is also how the silver happens. Silica fibres that have fused completely, died back from the living matrix, and left circulation turn opaque silver-white. Glasswrights grow this deliberately along sail edges and spars for strength. The silver on a body vane and the silver on a ship's vane are the same event at two scales, which is why the being and the fleet read as one material culture.

Colour in the glass is an involuntary tell. Strong emotion changes the metabolic rate, and the stored light re-emits at a different wavelength: amber for grief, cold blue for focus, a brief rose for pleasure. The Glasswright cannot start it, stop it, or shape it. It is a blush. They speak with their voices.

The derivation rule governs everything below: cover any single part of this body, the face, a hand, a sail, and the same story explains it. One tissue, at a stated crystallinity, hydration, and light-saturation. Nothing is bolted on, inlaid, or attached.

## 2. Body plan and carriage

The Glasswright is not upright and not bipedal. The silhouette is a low, wide, forward-leaning weaver: a broad segmented thorax carried close to the ground, the head projecting forward from its leading edge, four arms reaching forward and down toward the work, four support limbs planted beneath. The total impression is a spider at its loom, patient and deliberate, with all attention gathered at the front where the hands meet the workpiece.

**Thorax.** Five broad, overlapping plates of dense, low-hydration tissue, each faintly translucent, each glowing with deep stored light. The bands between plates are narrow, hydrated, and flexible: the thorax flexes on them, breathes on them (the bands visibly expand and contract), and absorbs shock on them. The segmentation is a density pattern of the one tissue, never arthropod plating. The leading plate is broader and flatter, a chest shelf the workpiece rests against during close weaving. The trailing plates narrow and deepen, housing the light-storage reserves that glow most visibly in the body's core.

**Support limbs.** Four, in two pairs, two forward and two aft, splayed wide and planted firmly. Each is a strut of dense crystalline tissue sheathed in hydrated tissue for grip and cushion, jointed at two points by cartilaginous bends of the same tissue at intermediate crystallinity. The feet are broad, flat, slightly splayed pads textured with fine crystalline ridges for purchase on glass floors and bench surfaces. The stance is stable and low-centred, built to hold still for hours. A Glasswright does not tower. A Glasswright settles.

**Head and neck.** The head projects from the thorax's leading edge on a short, thick neck, more a tapered extension of the thorax than a separate column, flexible enough to tilt and turn through a modest arc and sturdy enough to hold steady during precision work. The skull is broad, slightly flattened top to bottom, wider than it is tall, with a gentle forward taper. It is the same tissue, denser and more crystalline toward the face, more hydrated toward the back and underside. It reads as grown, not carved: smooth and continuous, with the lattice of the crystalline regions showing as a subtle geometric texture beneath the surface, like frost patterns inside window glass.

**Size.** An adult at full extension stands about a metre at the top of the thorax and spreads nearly two metres across the planted support limbs. They are not small and they are not tall. They are broad, low, and present.

## 3. The face

The face is the front plane of the head, angled slightly upward. It is not a mask applied to the skull. It is the skull's own surface, shaped by the tissue's gradient into features.

**Eyes.** Two grown lens-eyes, side by side, forward facing, spaced about one eye-width apart. Each is the tissue at its highest crystallinity and most precise lattice order: a smooth, slightly convex lens about three to four centimetres across. The colour is deep green, the green of light stored long and deep in the lattice, the wavelength at which this species' tissue most stably holds light. The eyes sit flush. They do not protrude, do not recess into deep sockets, and have no rims. The surrounding tissue transitions from lens order to soft face tissue with no hard border. Two eyes, never more, never stacked or ringed: a binocular arrangement evolved for depth judgement at bench distance, fine detail at close range, broad light-sensitive sweep at distance. They do not see in the dark. They see by the light they carry and the light around them.

**Lids.** Each eye has a lid of hydrated tissue that slides across the lens from the inner corner outward, slow and deliberate, lubricating and protecting it. The lids are a primary vehicle of expression: a slow blink is contemplation, a quick tight blink is irritation or pain, drooping lids are weariness or grief, the full lens exposed is attention or surprise.

**Brow.** Above and slightly forward of the eyes, a heavy segmented brow spans the face: five to seven dense, semi-crystalline segments in a shallow arc, joined by narrow hydrated bands so the brow can draw inward, lift, flatten, or furrow asymmetrically. The brow is the most expressive structure on the face and is always in motion. It shades the eyes from stray light at the bench, which is its working function, and carries intent and feeling in social life. The central segments are broader, the outer ones narrower and angled back, a grown taper.

**Mouth and voice.** Low in the face, below and between the eyes, sits a short reed grille: a row of thin, parallel, closely spaced crystalline reeds set into a shallow recess in the hydrated lower face, flush with the surface. The reeds do not protrude. They are not a tube, a pipe, or a held object; they are a region of the face where the tissue has formed into parallel vanes, the way another region has formed into a lens. Behind them opens the vocal cavity, a resonant chamber in the throat where air is shaped into speech. The voice is made there, and the grille is its outlet: speech passes through the reeds with a faint harmonic undertone, warm and slightly resonant, a normal speaking voice. When a Glasswright speaks, the reeds vibrate subtly. When silent, they are still. The grille is small, a facial feature, never a device.

## 4. Arms, hands, and spinnerets

Four arms rise from the thorax's leading segments in two pairs, all the same tissue in gradient: dense crystalline struts inside, hydrated cartilage and tendon at the joints, the most sensitive and dexterous tissue at the hands.

**Working arms, the upper pair.** Longer and stronger, built to reach, hold, position, and stabilise. Their hands are broad, four fingers and an opposed thumb, with thick hydrated pads on the palms for gripping hot or sharp glass. Every fingertip ends in a slightly hardened cap, the tissue at high crystallinity, for pressing and shaping thread.

**Precision arms, the lower pair.** Shorter and finer, working closest to the bench surface. Their hands are narrow, five slender digits with a lighter touch, fingertip caps smaller and finer. These hands draw, weave, and place.

**Spinnerets.** Every wrist carries a spinneret on its inner face, largest at the precision wrists: a small cluster of fine capillary channels opening through a smooth-lipped pore. The mechanism is the substance system itself. Stored light released as metabolic heat softens the tissue inside the channel, and drawing the wrist away extrudes a fine continuous strand of the body's own tissue, molten at the pore and cooling clear in the air within a second. Speed and distance of the pull set the thread's thickness. The spinneret is not a tool held in the hand. It is a region of the wrist, grown and always present. The thread a Glasswright weaves is their own substance, which is why their glass records faithfully: the thread carries the maker's stored light, and the light conditions at the moment of laying, into the finished structure. The largest thing woven this way is a ship's sail vane, laid thread by thread onto a grown frame by teams over weeks.

## 5. The sails

The sails are the tissue at its thinnest, most extended, most optically ordered expression: grown crystal vanes echoing the fleet's clear crystal and silver vanes because they are the same growth.

**Plan.** Four to six vanes in a shallow, layered fan across the upper thorax: two larger primaries flanking the midline, and two to four smaller secondaries set slightly lower and farther back, overlapping at their bases. Each vane is a single grown sheet, roughly triangular, broader at the root and tapering to a free outer edge, clear to faintly silver tinted, with rainbow fringes where light splits at the thin margins and died-back silver along the edges and spars. The largest primary reaches about sixty centimetres from root to tip. The vanes are modest and proportional. They never dwarf the body. Roots are thickened ridges of the same tissue set into the thorax plates, jointed by dense cartilage bends, grown tendon and cartilage only. No masts, no rigging, no hardware.

**At the bench.** The vanes furl by folding back and down along the thorax, stacked close, out of the arms' way. One vane stays out: the leading vane on the dominant side, angled forward and down, its outer edge ground by growth to a fine focusing curve. That edge catches forge-light, viewport light, or the body's own stored light and focuses it into a tight bright line on the workpiece: the work-light. It is never a beam from the mouth. Small shifts of the thorax track the line across the work as the hands move. At the bench the sail is a tool, used constantly and without thought.

**Away from the bench.** Spread, the vanes gather light, feeding the body's storage until the thorax glow brightens. Half spread, they vent heat in the close warmth of a forge or a ship's interior. They are also sense organs: a Glasswright in open space stands with the fan half open, reading the light around it. They are not wings and generate no lift. And they carry the grief: the species watched its own machine kill its sun, and the sails still open toward where the sun was, by a habit older than the loss. The gesture is the faction's whole grief in one motion, a hand held out to light that should be there. It is what the organ is for, at the deepest layer of the culture.

## 6. Variation within one species

Variation lives in exactly two places: the depth of glass fusion across the body, and the sail plan. Anatomy does not vary.

**Bench workers.** The baseline. Four to six clean-edged vanes kept at working size, fusion heavy where the trade puts it: glass fingertips, thickened spinneret wrists, brow glass clear from constant use. Stored light runs bright from constant exposure to focusing edges. The bench worker is not a lesser Glasswright. The bench worker is the species.

**Youth, the first sixty years.** The tissue is highly hydrated and only partly crystallised, close to the Starwright root. Lens-eyes are not yet at full optical density. Brow segments are fewer, about five. Vanes are small, thin, slightly flexible. The glow is faint and unsteady. Young Glasswrights are quicker, more restless, less precise, and their thread is finer but less consistent.

**Working maturity, sixty to two hundred and fifty years.** Full crystallisation and the stable adult state: dense plates, precise lenses, a five to seven segment brow, vanes at full size and rigidity, a steady deep glow, consistent strong thread. The long middle life: the master weaver, the fleet engineer, the admiral in their prime.

**Elders, two hundred and fifty to four hundred years.** Crystallinity keeps increasing. Plates thicken, brow segments may reach eight or nine, and the sail margins bloom: the lattice at the edges has fused, micro-fractured, and re-fused so many thousands of times that it roughens into frosted, rainbow-fringed bloom edges, more lens surface and more light-gather, visibly worn, chipped, and scratched by centuries of work. The glow deepens to a richer, more saturated green, and its colour shifts run slower and stronger. Elders work less and teach more, or take the largest, slowest commissions: the great recording arrays, the deep-hull optics of capital ships.

**The four admirals.** Same species, same anatomy, same face. Each differs in sail plan, fusion depth, glow character, and the wear of their role.

- **Tamsa of the Clear Aperture**, wing admiral, the fleet's youngest at ninety. Six vanes, the maximum, all moderate, tightly jointed for quick furl and spread, clean-edged and exceptionally transparent with strong silver fringe. Fusion moderate and even, glow bright and steady, lenses a clear vivid green. She reads alert, quick, practical: a weaver of speed.
- **Saro of Long Focus**, lance admiral, the oldest of the four. Four vanes, the two primaries longer and narrower than standard, their focusing edges ground over decades to the finest optical profile on any living Glasswright. Fusion runs deep, the glow concentrated and intense through the plates, the lenses the deepest green of the four, his brow heavy and held drawn inward. He reads still, focused, patient.
- **Vell of the Third Mirror**, duellist. Five vanes, asymmetric: the port side larger and more heavily jointed, because she leads with her left. Her left primary carries a broad panel of died-back silver across its inner face, a true mirror grown from the same tissue, used to throw light and read reflections in single combat. Her glass carries internal fracture patterns from absorbed combat light, her glow runs warmer and more amber, her brow segments are chipped and regrown. She reads scarred, quick, intense.
- **Brin of the Fourth Lens**, ward admiral. Six vanes, broad and slightly cupped, with concave geometry that spreads and dissipates light instead of focusing it, the most silver-tinted lattice of the four and the strongest rainbow fringe. Fusion is the most even and extensive, nearly the whole thorax surface crystalline, the glow a broad soft pale-green wash. Brin reads steady, wide, sheltering.

**The ancient elder.** At the far end of the elder band, past roughly three hundred and fifty years, the fuller spider form arrives, and it belongs there and nowhere else. The rear thorax segments lengthen, the support limbs grow more prominent and angular, the stance drops lower and spreads wider into the full eight-point weaver stance, and the arms sit proportionally smaller against the greater thorax. The sail plan is vast: eight or more huge, bloom-edged, worn vanes furled down the long back. The face is the same face on a broader, heavier skull, brow of nine or ten segments, lenses a forest-deep green. This is not a monster and not a caste. It is the same substance system given the longest time to express itself: a living bench and a living record, revered and slow. No healthy working adult ever reads this way.

## 7. Life and craft

**Work.** Every Glasswright is a weaver first. The fundamental gesture of the culture is drawing thread from one's own wrist and laying it onto a workpiece. Children learn to draw thread before they speak in full sentences, and the first object a young Glasswright weaves is traditionally a small lens, imperfect and kept for life. At the bench the body is the jig, the clamp, and the tool: working arms hold and position, precision arms draw and weave, the focusing vane keeps the light line on the join, and a master can draw from both precision wrists at once and braid the threads before they cool. The glass they weave is optical memory. A hull woven this way holds the light of every battle the ship has seen, and the forge reads the replay. The body does the same at small scale: an old Glasswright's thorax holds centuries of light, including, in the deepest elders, light from before the sun died.

**Clothing.** Very little, by necessity: cloth over the sails blocks light gathering, cloth over the spinnerets fouls the thread, cloth over the chest dims the reserve. What they wear is made of their own woven glass thread: open lattice aprons over the thorax front at the bench, light harnesses for tools and workpieces, and for formal address a draped lattice mantle hung from the sail roots that leaves the sails free. Rank and role show in the weave pattern, never in metal or fittings. Admirals wear longer, finer aprons, sometimes with the fleet's sigil recorded into the weave as a permanent optical inscription.

**Movement.** Glasswrights walk on the four support limbs in a slow, deliberate gait, fore and aft pairs alternating, silent on ridged foot-pads that grip glass and metal. They do not run; in urgency the gait quickens to a trot and that is all the body plan allows. There is no upright stance: standing on two limbs is unstable, uncomfortable, and rare, and nothing in their lives requires it. Admirals command settled, as workers work settled, and presence is carried by sail plan, glow, and brow. On ships they move through wide, low corridors built for their spread, and anchor by the foot-pads before working in zero gravity. They do not climb; they are lifted, or weave a temporary glass ladder and ascend it slowly.

**Rest.** A resting Glasswright settles: thorax lowered until the belly plate rests, arms drawn in, sails folded forward over the whole body as a layered glass canopy, stored light dimmed to a low, slow pulse. They do not sleep as mammals sleep; the tissue repairs, the lattice re-orders, and the stored light cycles. They rest in groups when they can, settling in clusters at the edges of the forge or the common hall, their glows dimming in rough synchrony: a field of low, broad, softly luminous weavers, still as stones, warm as glass.

**Grief.** They carry two guilts, the sun their machine killed and the Fleshwrights their splinter, and both are within living memory: two or three generations separate the oldest living Glasswrights from the people who built the light. The grief is in the material. Grief is the most common colour in an elder's glass, a long slow amber settling over plates, sails, and face. They do not speak of it often. They weave it into the glass, and every recording array the fleet carries is also an act of holding light against the dark.
