# #500 Blind Rey leg: the Glasswright ship, made by artists (Glasswrights only)

Written before drafting any prompt and before reading any other leg, from Ryan's words in issue 500 ("artistic, thoughtful in creation and looking to create beauty with function, even in these warships") and first principles. Honest limit: the issue itself contains the Board's three routes and names the reference pictures, and I viewed the references to scope the job, so this leg is blind to the other models' legs and to any finished prompt, not to the brief. Its use: an independent set of routes and treatments for the chair, and the swap candidate for G16 if the chair wants one.

## The principle

A Glasswright warship should look like the finest scientific instruments ever made, scaled up and armed: objects where the working part is the beautiful part, because the maker's culture does not separate the two. The test for every element: could a master craftsman sign it? If a part reads as stock (a generic turret, a plain dome, a bare tube), it fails, however clean it is. If a part reads as decoration glued onto a weapon, it also fails. The ornament is the engineering, finished to a shine.

## Crafts to draw on, and what each one gives the ship

1. Astrolabes and orreries: engraved concentric rings, graduated chapter rings, arcs that carry real mechanism. Gives: the frame around lenses, scales along the spine, rings that read as instruments, not hoops.
2. Watchmaking: bridges with beveled edges, jewel bearings as seats for moving parts, fine screws, Geneva striping on flat metal. Gives: the spine as a movement bridge; lens seats as jewel settings; small parts finished, never moulded-looking.
3. Lighthouse Fresnel lenses: concentric prismatic rings that gather light and throw it in a direction. Gives: the stored light a visible job; bow lenses that plainly feed on it; engine glow with structure instead of a plain glare.
4. Leaded and stained glass: the pane lines are the structure (cames), colour lives in the glass itself, edges glow where light passes. Gives: the hull skin a grammar where the frame lines flow with the ship instead of tiling it; restrained colour (deep green, amber, violet at a few panes, like cathedral glass).
5. Art Nouveau ironwork and glass (Guimard, Lalique): the whiplash line as a structural rib, glass shades grown from the metal. Gives: ribs and mounts that sweep from bow to stern and gather round each lens; an end to the honeycomb tile, which reads insectile.
6. Violin making: the scroll, purfling lines that follow the edge, varnish depth. Gives: the rim treated as a crafted edge with an inlaid line; a bow that ends in a deliberate flourish, not a point.
7. Brass telescope making: focusing helicoids, knurled rings, objective cells. Gives: the two forward pulsars as objective lenses in cells a viewer instantly reads as things that aim light forward.
8. Organ building: ranked pipes, each sized to its note. Gives: the flak and small guns as a tuned rank of small lenses and bells, graduated in size, obviously one family.
9. Japanese sword furniture (tsuba, inlay): one dark accent metal, restrained inlay, nothing wasted. Gives: the second metal discipline: a warm dark bronze used only at moving joints and mounts, never sprayed over the hull.

## The weapons, treated one by one

- Pulsar F, pulsar F (the pair): two large objective lenses set in the shoulders of the bow, in worked silver cells with focusing rings, bores plainly on the ship's forward axis. They are the ship's eyes and its main guns in one object, the biggest jewels on the ship. A viewer should say "those fire forward" without being told.
- Pulsar T: one lens in an armillary mount on the spine: two or three engraved rings at angles, so it plainly swings and tilts to aim. The mount is the ornament.
- Turret T (the gun): a small lens in a low worked mount of the same family, not a grey box with a barrel.
- Flak T: a rank of four to six small lenses or bells in graduated sizes along the spine, like organ pipes or a glockenspiel: one system, visibly tuned, point defence read at a glance.
- Engines: the drive rings ground as Fresnel steps, so the stern light has rings and structure; the rings are set in tracery, not clamped in plain cylinders.
- The spine: an engraved bridge with a graduated scale and jewel seats where the mounts sit; it is the ship's ruler and its signature.
- The skin: panes in flowing tracery, sizes varying with the hull's stress (small near the bow shoulders, long amidships), light running along the cames.

## Three routes of my own

R1. The Orrery (instrument route): silver rings and arcs carry the frame; the top pulsar sits in a full armillary sphere of three rings; scales engraved along the spine; the ship reads as a navigational instrument that happens to be armed.
R2. The Leaded Reliquary (tracery route): the whole glass skin held in sweeping silver tracery, a few panes coloured deep green and amber, the bow lenses cradled where the tracery gathers; quiet, ecclesiastical, unmistakably made by hand.
R3. The Kept Light (Fresnel route): the stored light is the artwork; the hull glass is ground in radiating facets that gather light toward the bow; the stern is a rose of drive rings in tracery, throwing the light out in a patterned fan.

## Chair note to myself

My three routes converge with the issue's G14, G15 and G16 (instrument, leaded glass, lighthouse), reached independently, which is evidence the three named routes are the right three families. The one element none of the issue's routes names is the stern: R3's rose of drive rings in tracery, and the flak as a graduated rank. If a swap for G16 is wanted, my candidate is R3 with the rose stern kept and the hull facets quieter than a full Fresnel wrap, so the route stays a ship and not a lamp. Decide after the writer legs.
