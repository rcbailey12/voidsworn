# Issue 1063 Glasswright and Eclipse economy brainstorm. Model's response, verbatim.

Embers of the Hollow Sun: Fleet Maintenance & Growth Proposals

Faction 1: Glasswrights (Ithar)

Identity Summary: Master optical engineers. Ships are works of art constructed from layered blown glass with seamlessly grown lenses and stored light inside. They feature high shields, high speed, and zero armor. When damaged, optics crack and shatter as panes; when destroyed, stored light spills out. Hulls are the most expensive and precious per class.

Approach 1: Kiln Annealing & Facet Polishing
- How it works: Instead of buying off a shelf, players spend credits and a time/node-based currency called Refraction Crystal (earned proportionally from battles and salvage) to send a damaged ship into mobile Annealing Kilns. Minor hull damage is mended via thermal re-fusing (cheap and fast), while shattered hull sections or destroyed ships must be regrown from raw silica blanks over multiple sector jumps.
- Other games & lessons: XCOM (weapon and soldier recovery time). This teaches that tying fleet availability to logistical downtime forces the player to manage reserves, but risks frustrating players if downtime feels punitive after a loss.
- What the player decides: Whether to push forward with damaged, fragile ships or spend precious sector time resting/annealing at specialized nodes.
- What it would break: If annealing takes too long, the steep price ladder of Glasswright hulls could create an insurmountable death spiral for a losing fleet.
- Build size: Medium.

Approach 2: Light Harvesting & Prism Refueling
- How it works: Glasswright ships store light as their lifeblood. Between battles, players collect Luminance at specific star nodes. This stored light is then used to repair hulls, recharge the Stored-light reservoir, and even purchase new hulls at a Prism Foundry. New ships are not bought; they are grown by focusing harvested light into a glass mold.
- Other games & lessons: FTL (fuel and drone parts economy). Resource scarcity forces tactical route planning. It teaches that a second resource tied to map movement creates meaningful choices about which nodes to visit.
- What the player decides: Choosing detour nodes to harvest light versus taking direct, high-combat paths to progress faster.
- What it would break: If Luminance drops too low on a losing run, the faction's shield-heavy doctrine falls apart, leaving players with no way to repair hulls.
- Build size: Small.

Approach 3: Glassblowing Consortia & Artisanal Guild Contracts
- How it works: New hulls and Mark upgrades are not bought instantly; they are commissioned from artisan guilds at shipyard nodes. Players pay a deposit in credits, then wait a set number of jumps for the ship to be hand-crafted and delivered. Higher Mark tiers require rarer materials and longer wait times.
- Other games & lessons: BattleTech (contract manufacturing and parts wait times). This adds strategic depth, as players must plan fleet growth several sectors ahead.
- What it would break: Rigid delivery timers might clash with the fast-paced, fluid movement of a roguelite run, making the player feel locked out of progress.
- Build size: Medium.

Approach 4: Shard Reclamation & Melt-Down Recycling
- How it works: When a Glasswright ship is destroyed, a portion of its shattered hull is recovered as High-Purity Shards. These shards can be melted down at a shipyard to repair other ships at a discount or combined to grow a replacement hull of the same class at reduced cost.
- Other games & lessons: Homeworld (salvaging enemy or wrecked ships for resource units). This rewards aggressive play and salvaging, turning a loss into a partial recovery.
- What the player decides: Whether to prioritize aggressive salvage collection during/after battles or focus on preserving the fleet to avoid needing shards at all.
- What it would break: If reclamation yields are too high, it removes the tension of losing expensive, fragile ships.
- Build size: Small.

Glasswrights: Recommended Direction
Approach 1 (Kiln Annealing & Facet Polishing) combined with Approach 4 (Shard Reclamation).
Using Annealing Kilns aligns directly with their existing in-game module naming conventions and emphasizes repair-over-replacement for their precious hulls. Shard Reclamation provides a soft floor for recovery after a loss, preventing a death spiral. This combination preserves the flat, reliable income model: credits pay for annealing time, while shards provide a modest, capped bonus from losses.

Faction 2: Eclipse (Deathless)

Identity Summary: Uploaded Starwrights inhabiting machine bodies in a system they made dark. Ships consist of black shade plates layered over archive vaults and dark decks, with a tiny core of trapped light inside. Ships break in layers, peeling away band by band. They have no shields, moderate armor (.2 to .35), and in-battle hull regen.

Approach 1: Archive Restorations & Digital Redundancy
- How it works: The fleet relies on the existing Archive backup system. Between battles, players spend credits to purchase Archive Slots (one per ship). A destroyed ship with a slot is restored at the next shipyard/drydock at its last backed-up Mark level, using its stored pattern. This extends the existing "Back up the fleet" (45 credits) dock action into a permanent between-battle economy.
- Other games & lessons: Total War (immortal lord recovery) or Into the Breach (pilot survival mechanics). These systems teach that persistent characters/units reduce the sting of permanent loss while still costing resources to restore.
- What the player decides: Whether to invest scarce income into upgrading Archive bandwidth (allowing more ships to be restored per sector) or spend on immediate repairs.
- What it would break: If Archive restoration is too cheap, it completely invalidates the threat of ship loss and the tension of the roguelite run.
- Build size: Small.

Approach 2: Shade Plate Stripping & Modular Layering
- How it works: Eclipse ships break in layers (shade plates peeling away). Between battles, players repair by replacing individual shade plates rather than the whole hull. Players can also strip plates from destroyed enemy ships (flagged "ripped" in code) to use as patch material. This creates a modular repair system where armor can be swapped between ships.
- Other games & lessons: Armored Core (part swapping and mechanical tuning). This highlights the modular, machine-like nature of the faction and allows for flexible fleet customization.
- What the player decides: Allocating resources between heavy outer armor plating (survivability) and inner core/vault upgrades (damage and abilities).
- What it would break: Complex layer-swapping UI could bloat between-battle management screens if not kept simple.
- Build size: Medium.

Approach 3: Core Harvesting & Dark-Sun Resonance
- How it works: The tiny trapped sun core inside each ship powers its regeneration and abilities. Between battles, players harvest Core Resonance from defeated enemies and dark nodes. This resonance is used to fuel the Repair drones module, accelerate in-battle regen for the next fight, and grow new hulls by waking dormant hulls from the hull yard event using an Archive copy.
- Other games & lessons: FTL (reactor upgrades and power management). This ties the faction's upkeep directly to their core-light identity.
- What the player decides: Balancing energy expenditure between powering fleet repairs and fueling strategic abilities like reclaim.
- What it would break: If core energy is tied too strictly to winning, losing fleets will starve; the flat income model must provide a baseline resonance floor.
- Build size: Medium.

Approach 4: Reclamation Swarm Scrap-Processing
- How it works: Expanding on their existing "Reclamation swarm" doctrine, nanite clouds scour battlefields for scrap. Between battles, players direct the swarm to either repair hulls (consuming scrap) or construct new hulls from salvaged enemy hulls. This automates some of the repair process but requires player direction on priorities.
- Other games & lessons: Stellaris (automated construction and debris analysis). This provides a smooth, low-friction way to convert battlefield debris into fleet growth.
- What the player decides: Directing swarm priorities between rapid armor repair for active ships versus slow construction of new hulls.
- What it would break: Over-automation can reduce player engagement if the between-battle loop feels entirely passive.
- Build size: Small.

Eclipse: Recommended Direction
Approach 1 (Archive Restorations) integrated with Approach 3 (Core Harvesting).
This setup builds directly on existing Eclipse mechanics-such as the "Back up the fleet" dock action and the dormant hull yard event-while giving the faction a distinct, identity-driven economy. Archive Slots provide a reliable recovery floor (a lost ship can return at a cost), while Core Resonance provides a small, capped bonus for aggressive play without creating a win-more trap. It keeps in-battle reanimation and regen separate: Archive restores between battles, reanimation happens only in battle.

Summary of Risks & Mitigations
- Death Spirals: Both factions feature expensive or specialized units. Mitigation: Ensure the flat victory income (set by sector and fight kind, half on partial win) provides a reliable floor for repairs, with only a small performance bonus. Cap secondary resources (Refraction Crystal, Luminance, Shards, Resonance) so they never replace the base income.
- Mechanic Bloat: Keeping in-battle mechanics (Glasswright shields/blink, Eclipse reanimation/regen) distinct from between-battle economies is critical. Each proposed system should touch, but not replace, existing mechanics like "Annealing kilns," "Repair drones," and "Archive restore."
