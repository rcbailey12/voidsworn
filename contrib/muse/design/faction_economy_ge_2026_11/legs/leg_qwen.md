Embers of the Hollow Sun - Fleet Economy Brainstorm
    Glasswrights (ithar) and Eclipse (deathless)
    Council voice: design proposals, November 2026
    
    How to read this
    Every proposal below is designed around the owner's income correction: credits (or the faction equivalent) arrive as a flat amount per win set by sector and fight kind, halved on a partial win, with only a small performance kicker. No proposal should let a winning fleet snowball or a losing fleet spiral. Each entry names its own floor so a fleet that just lost can still limp forward. Build sizes are rough agent-effort estimates: small (a few agent-days), medium (one to two agent-weeks), large (three-plus weeks or multi-system work).
    
    I. GLASSWRIGHTS (ithar)
    The Glasswrights build ships the way a master glassblower builds a vessel: slowly, in layers, with light sealed inside. Their ships are the fewest and most expensive per class in the game. They cannot Brace. They live by shields and speed, and when they break they shatter - optics shear off as whole panes, the hull snaps, stored light spills. Recovery should feel like a workshop, not a drydock. The fantasy is restoration, not replacement.
    
    Approach 1 - The Annealing Berth
    How it works. Repair is no longer a single credit-to-hull transaction. A Glasswright ship's hull damage is sorted into three tiers when it reaches a node. Hairline damage (hull above 70 %) can be annealed at any Glasswright-tagged shipyard node for a small flat credit cost, restoring the ship fully; the kiln heats the glass just enough to seal micro-cracks. Deep-crack damage (30-70 %) requires a dedicated Kiln node (a new node type, or a flag on existing shipyard nodes) and costs more credits; the glass must be brought close to its softening point and re-shaped, and the repair is still full but the price reflects the risk. Shattered (below 30 %) cannot be annealed at all; the pane structure is too compromised. The ship can only be rebuilt through Approach 2 (cullet regrowth) or replaced through Approach 3 (commission). A free floor is kept: any Glasswright ship above 70 % hull anneals free at a Kiln node, so a fleet that scraped through a fight still gets patched without spending its win-purse.
    What other games teach. Darkest Dungeon forces you to choose between cheap, partial healing at the hamlet and expensive full recovery; the decision is time and gold versus risk. XCOM 2's wound timers mean a soldier who took a hit sits out a mission or two even after healing, making you feel the cost of damage beyond the battlefield. FTL lets you repair hull at stores but limits store availability, so you cannot always patch when you want. The lesson across all three: making repair a choice with tiers rather than a vending machine makes the player value the ship. For Glasswrights, the tier language should be glass language - anneal, re-shape, shatter - not mechanical language.
    What the player decides. After each battle the player looks at each damaged hull and sorts it: is this ship annealable here, does it need a Kiln node further along the route, or is it past saving? The decision is routing and triage. Do I detour two jumps to a Kiln node to save my cruiser, or press on with a cracked frigate and hope shields cover the gap? Because Glasswright ships cannot Brace, a hull sitting at 40 % is genuinely dangerous, and the player feels that.
    What it would break. The current flat "1 credit per 2 hull, restored in full" rule disappears for Glasswrights. Shipyards need a Glasswright tag and Kiln nodes need to exist on the sector map. The UI must show damage tiers, not just a hull bar. Any system that assumed repair is always available at any shipyard node needs a faction gate.
    Build size. Medium. New damage-tier logic, a Kiln node flag (or new node), updated shipyard UI for the faction, and the free-anneal floor. No new resource, no new ship state beyond what hull already tracks.
    
    Approach 2 - Cullet Recovery and Regrowth
    How it works. When a Glasswright ship is destroyed in battle, it shatters. The wreckage is not generic scrap; it is cullet - shards of layered glass that still carry traces of stored light. Cullet becomes a Glasswright-only secondary resource. After any battle in which a Glasswright ship was lost, the player recovers a fixed amount of cullet from the shatter (this is the floor: losing a ship always gives you something back, so a bad fight is not a dead end). Cullet is also earned in small flat amounts from wins, in line with the income correction, representing salvaged optics from enemy wrecks that can be re-melted.
    At a Glasswright forge node (a variant of the shipyard), the player spends cullet plus a modest credit fee to regrow a hull. The regrown ship is imperfect: recycled glass carries bubbles and inclusions, so it enters service at Mark 0 regardless of the original's Mark, and it carries a permanent cosmetic scar (a visible seam in the glass) plus a small stat penalty, say 5-10 % reduced hull maximum. This makes cullet-regrowth a recovery path, not an upgrade path. You can also spend cullet to discount a major repair at a Kiln node (the shards are melted into the existing glass, filling gaps), which gives cullet a second use and prevents it from being a dead resource when your fleet is intact.
    What other games teach. Subnautica asks you to gather raw materials from the environment and craft them into components; nothing is bought, everything is made, and the materials feel earned. Kerbal Space Program lets you recover parts from destroyed rockets and reuse them, but the recovered parts are slightly degraded. XCOM's salvage-from-missions loop means every fight, even a loss, leaves something in your lap. The lesson: a loss-recovery resource softens the blow without erasing it, and imperfection in the rebuilt thing makes the player value the original more. For Glasswrights, the fantasy is that a shattered masterwork can be re-melted, but it will never be the same piece.
    What the player decides. Do I spend my cullet now on a quick imperfect regrowth to fill a fleet gap, or bank it for a discount on a deep-crack anneal at a Kiln node? Do I accept a Mark-0 regrown destroyer with a hull scar, or save the cullet and go into the next fight one ship short? The decision is speed versus quality, and the answer changes depending on how far the next Kiln or forge node is.
    What it would break. Introduces a faction-specific resource (cullet) alongside credits, which the UI must display and the economy must balance. The "buy a new hull off the shelf" path is replaced by regrowth for Glasswrights, so the shipyard purchase flow needs a faction branch. The permanent scar and Mark-reset on regrown ships need to persist across saves and be visible in the fleet screen. The flat income correction means cullet-from-kills and cullet-from-wins must be tuned so that a player who never loses a ship still trickles in some cullet (for repair discounts) but never enough to mass-regrow.
    Build size. Medium-to-large. New resource, new node interaction, regrowth logic with Mark reset and permanent modifier, UI for cullet display and forge node, and balance tuning against the flat income.
    
    Approach 3 - The Commission
    How it works. Glasswright ships are not on a shelf. You cannot walk into a shipyard and buy a frigate. Instead, at an Atelier node (a rare, flagged node type, perhaps one per sector), the player commissions a new hull. You pay a credit price higher than the current shelf price (reflecting bespoke craftsmanship), choose the hull class, and the ship enters a build queue measured in jumps: a frigate might take 2 jumps, a cruiser 4. During those jumps the ship is not in your fleet; you are one hull down. When the timer expires, the ship arrives at your next node, perfect, at the Mark you commissioned. You can have one commission active at a time (two if a specific event or commander perk unlocks a second bench). This makes fleet growth slow, deliberate, and precious, matching the identity that every Glasswright ship is a work of art.
    A free floor is not needed here because commissions are for growth, not recovery; recovery is handled by Approaches 1 and 2. But the commission queue should survive a lost battle (you don't lose your commission if you lose the fight), so a player who is rebuilding can still look forward to the ship on the way.
    What other games teach. Stellaris makes you queue ship construction at starbases and wait turns; the delay makes each ship feel like an investment. Homeworld's build queue means you plan your fleet composition jumps in advance. Civilization's unit production takes multiple turns, so you commit early and live with the choice. The lesson: a build timer converts a purchase into a plan, and the player starts thinking two or three nodes ahead instead of reactively. For Glasswrights, the timer is the glassblower's furnace cycle; you cannot rush the glass.
    What the player decides. Which hull class to commission, when to start it, and whether to route through the Atelier node now or later. The player also decides whether to commission a cheap frigate to fill a gap quickly or save for a cruiser that takes twice as long but changes the fleet's capability. Because the ship is not in your fleet during the build, you must fight short-handed, which is a real cost.
    What it would break. The instant-purchase model at shipyards is gone for new Glasswright hulls. The sector map must guarantee Atelier node placement (or risk the player never growing their fleet). The build queue needs to persist across saves and display clearly. Mark purchases (currently bought at shipyards alongside hulls) need to be folded into the commission: you choose the Mark at commission time, not after.
    Build size. Medium. New node type, build-queue timer, UI for commission screen and countdown, and sector-map placement rules. No new resource; credits still work, just at a different node and with a delay.
    
    Approach 4 - Light Wells and the Stored-Light Economy
    How it works. Glasswright ships store light inside their hulls. That light is not just lore; it becomes a faction resource. Light is earned in a flat trickle per jump (a small amount whether you fight or not, representing ambient starlight collected by the fleet's optics) plus a flat bonus per win in line with the income correction. Light is spent in three ways. First, it refills the Stored-light reservoir, the shield-regen module: if a ship's shield regen has been degraded by hull damage (below a certain hull threshold, shields recharge slower because the glass is cracked and leaks light), spending light restores full regen rate. Second, it is a component in annealing: deep-crack repairs at a Kiln node cost credits plus a fixed amount of light, because you cannot re-shape glass without light-heat. Third, it overcharges the prism lance before a battle: spending a large chunk of light lets the cruiser's main gun fire at increased damage for the first two turns, representing the hull's stored light let out in a focused burst.
    The floor: the per-jump trickle means a fleet that lost badly still collects light simply by moving through the sector. A fleet sitting at low hull can still restore its shield regen at a node for a small light cost, keeping the Glasswright playstyle (shields and speed) functional even after a bad fight.
    What other games teach. FTL's fuel resource means you always have a baseline ability to move, but extended travel drains it, creating routing tension. Homeworld's resource gathering (harvesting asteroids) gives a slow, reliable income that is separate from combat rewards. Slay the Spire's energy resets each fight but the meta resources (gold, cards) persist; the lesson is that a small reliable trickle keeps the player from being stuck. The key teaching: a secondary resource should have at least two meaningful sinks so the player is always choosing between them, and the trickle should be large enough to matter but small enough that wins still feel rewarding.
    What the player decides. The three-way spend is the core decision. Do I pour light into shield regen so my frigates can blink-and-tank next fight? Do I bank it for a deep-crack anneal I know I need at the next Kiln node? Or do I spend it on a prism-lance overcharge to burst down a boss? The answer shifts every sector depending on what nodes are ahead and what damage the fleet is carrying.
    What it would break. Adds a second resource to the Glasswright economy (light alongside credits and possibly cullet from Approach 2). The shield-regen module's behaviour changes: it is no longer a fixed number but can be degraded and restored, which touches the battle system's shield calculations. The prism-lance overcharge is a new pre-battle spend that the battle system must honour. If Approach 2 (cullet) is also adopted, the player is tracking three resources, which risks cognitive overload; the UI must be very clear.
    Build size. Medium-to-large. New resource, per-jump trickle, three distinct spend hooks (shield-regen restore, annealing component, prism-lance overcharge), UI, and battle-system integration for the overcharge. If cullet is also in play, the combined economy is a large build.
    
    Recommended direction - Glasswrights
    Adopt Approach 1 (Annealing Berth) and Approach 3 (The Commission) together as the backbone. Annealing replaces the flat repair rule with a tiered, glass-language system that makes the player triage after every fight and routes them toward Kiln nodes. The Commission replaces shelf-purchases for new hulls and makes fleet growth a deliberate, timed investment. These two cover repair and replacement without introducing a second or third resource, keeping the economy legible.
    Then add a light version of Approach 4: light is earned as the flat per-jump trickle and the small per-win bonus, and it is spent only on annealing deep-crack repairs (as a component cost alongside credits). This gives light a single clear sink, keeps the Glasswright identity of light-as-fuel present in the economy, and avoids the three-sink complexity of the full Approach 4. Skip Approach 2 (cullet) for now; it is the richest idea but adds a second resource and a permanent-modifier system that would be better saved for a content drop or expansion once the base economy is stable.
    The result: repair is tiered and craft-flavoured, new ships are commissioned and take time, light is a small but flavourful component of major repairs, and the whole loop runs on the flat income correction without win-more spirals. A fleet that just lost can anneal its hairline cracks free, limp toward a Kiln node for the deep ones, and look forward to a commissioned hull two jumps out.
    
    II. ECLIPSE (deathless)
    The Eclipse are uploaded minds in machine bodies. Their ships are black shade plates wrapped around archive vaults and a trapped core of light. They do not have shields; they have armour and in-battle hull regen. They already reanimate destroyed ships in battle and have an Archive-restore dock action. The identity is backup, restore, reclamation, layered peeling, and the slow industrial work of keeping a dead system's machines running. Recovery should feel like data management and heavy engineering, not craftsmanship.
    
    Approach 1 - Archive Depth and Restore Fidelity
    How it works. The existing "Back up the fleet" dock action (45 credits, restores one extra ship next battle) becomes the seed of a broader Archive system. Before each battle, at any node, the player sets their Archive depth for the fleet. There are two depths. Shallow costs a small flat credit fee (say 15 credits) and means that if a ship is destroyed in the coming battle, the Archive restore brings it back at Mark 0, with no abilities unlocked, hull at 60 %. Deep costs more (say 50 credits) and the restored ship returns at its full Mark, full abilities, full hull. The player sets depth per fleet, not per ship, to keep the decision simple. If the player cannot afford either depth, the default is no restore: destroyed ships stay destroyed (this is the same as today, so nothing is lost).
    The floor: a fleet that just lost a fight can always afford Shallow depth on its next port call, because 15 credits is within the flat income of even a partial win. A destroyed ship is never truly gone as long as the player can scrape together the Shallow fee. The ship comes back diminished, but it comes back. This is the fantasy: the mind is in the Archive; the body can be rebuilt, even if the rebuild is rough.
    This system is explicitly the between-battle layer. The in-battle reanimate mechanic (a destroyed Eclipse ship can return during the same fight) is untouched and operates separately. The in-battle reclaim ability on the cruiser is also untouched. The touchpoint is: if a ship reanimates in battle, it does not need an Archive restore afterward; the Archive restore only applies to ships that ended the battle destroyed. The event "dormant hull yard" (see Approach 2) is also separate: that is about finding new bodies, not restoring existing minds.
    What other games teach. Soma's central mechanic is copying consciousness; the question of whether the copy is "you" is the whole game. Hades uses meta-progression between runs: you die, you lose your build, but your persistent upgrades carry forward. XCOM lets you rebuild a soldier's gear after they die, but the soldier themselves is gone; the tension is between the investment in the person and the investment in the equipment. The lesson for Eclipse: the mind is persistent (the Archive), but the body (the hull, the Mark, the abilities) is what you can lose. Making restore fidelity a choice means the player is deciding how much of the body they are willing to lose.
    What the player decides. Before each battle, set Shallow or Deep. The decision is a risk assessment: if I expect a hard fight, do I pay for Deep so my cruiser comes back intact? Or do I save credits and accept that a loss means rebuilding from Mark 0? Over a sector, the cumulative cost of Deep adds up, so the player is also budgeting across multiple fights.
    What it would break. The binary alive/dead state of a ship becomes a spectrum: alive, destroyed-and-restored-Shallow, destroyed-and-restored-Deep, destroyed-and-gone. The fleet screen must show which ships are restored and at what fidelity. Mark and ability tracking must support a "reset to Mark 0" state for Shallow restores. The existing "Back up the fleet" action is absorbed into this system and should be removed or renamed to avoid confusion.
    Build size. Medium. Extend the existing Archive restore action, add depth tiers, modify the restore outcome (Mark 0 vs. full), add the pre-battle depth selection UI, and handle the interaction with in-battle reanimate (reanimated ships skip the restore).
    
    Approach 2 - Dormant Hull Yards and Awakening
    How it works. The existing event (a drifting Eclipse hull yard with stored hulls and no minds, where "a copy from the Archive would wake one of them") becomes a repeatable node type rather than a one-off event. Dormant Hull Yard nodes appear on the sector map at a fixed density (one per sector, perhaps two in later sectors). At a Dormant Hull Yard, the player finds one or two empty hulls of a specific class. To wake a hull, the player spends an Archive copy, a new resource. Archive copies are earned as a flat reward per win (in line with the income correction) and also found at certain events or prize nodes. The number of copies in your inventory is your pool of "spare minds" ready to be loaded into a body.
    The woken hull enters the fleet at a fixed Mark determined by the yard's age (older yards yield Mark 0; rare deep-sector yards yield Mark 1 or 2). The hull is an Eclipse hull, so it has no shields, has armour, and carries the faction's in-battle regen and reanimate traits. This is the primary way to grow the Eclipse fleet: not by building new ships at a shipyard, but by finding empty bodies in the dark and waking them.
    The floor: Archive copies trickle in from wins, so a player who is fighting (even winning partially) is accumulating copies. A fleet that lost a ship can wake a replacement at the next Dormant Hull Yard if it has a copy. If it has no copies, it can still use Approach 1 (Archive restore) to bring back the destroyed mind. The two systems cover each other.
    What other games teach. Homeworld's derelict-ship events let you find and add ships to your fleet through exploration rather than construction. Stellaris's precursor and anomaly events reward exploration with unique ships. FTL's sector events sometimes offer free ships or crew. The lesson: fleet growth through discovery feels different from fleet growth through purchase. It makes the sector map feel like a place with history, and it rewards routing decisions (do I detour to the Hull Yard?). For Eclipse, the fantasy is walking through a dark warehouse of sleeping machines and choosing which one to wake.
    What the player decides. Which dormant hull to wake (if the yard offers a choice of class), whether to spend an Archive copy now or save it for a better yard later, and whether to detour to a Hull Yard node or push toward the next battle. The player also decides whether to wake a hull for the fleet or sell the Archive copy at a shipyard for credits (a small credit return, giving copies a fallback value).
    What it would break. The "buy a new hull at a shipyard" path is replaced for Eclipse fleet growth. Shipyards still handle repair and Mark upgrades (see Approach 3) but not new hulls. The sector map must guarantee Dormant Hull Yard placement. The Archive copy resource needs UI, inventory tracking, and a clear source. The "ripped" flag on captured enemy hulls needs to be addressed: captured hulls are not Eclipse hulls and cannot be woken with an Archive copy; they can only be sold or scrapped (see Approach 3).
    Build size. Medium. New node type, new resource (Archive copies), wake interaction, sector-map placement, and UI. The existing dormant-yard event is a template and can be generalised.
    
    Approach 3 - Shade Plate Reclamation and Layer Rebuild
    How it works. Eclipse ships break in layers: shade plates peel away band by band, exposing archive vaults, dark decks, and the core. Between battles, repair is not a single hull-number transaction. At a Reclamation node (a variant of the shipyard, or a flag on existing nodes), the player rebuilds shade plates layer by layer. Each Eclipse hull has a fixed number of shade-plate layers (say 3 for a frigate, 4 for a destroyer, 5 for a cruiser, 6 for the ark). Hull damage maps to lost layers. Rebuilding one layer costs a flat credit amount. The player can rebuild as many or as few layers as they can afford. A ship at 2 of 4 layers is functional but fragile: its effective hull is reduced and its in-battle regen (the 2-to-8 per turn) is halved because the exposed inner decks are taking damage faster than the drones can patch.
    Shade plates can also be stripped from destroyed ships, including "ripped" captured hulls. Stripping a wreck at a Reclamation node yields 1-2 shade plates (a small resource) that can be used to rebuild your own ships at a discount. This gives captured hulls a use beyond selling: you can crack them open for plates. The "ripped" flag in the code already distinguishes captured hulls; this system gives that flag a mechanical consequence.
    The floor: rebuilding the first layer on any ship is free at a Reclamation node (the repair drones do the basic work). So a fleet that took heavy damage can always patch each ship up by one layer at no cost, keeping them functional if degraded.
    What other games teach. BattleTech uses location-based damage: you lose an arm, a leg, a torso section, and repair is per-location. MechWarrior continues this with component-level repair. Dead Space's upgrade system is node-based: you spend limited nodes on specific branches. Darkest Dungeon's partial healing means you can patch a character to "good enough" without full recovery. The lesson: layered repair makes the player choose between "good enough" and "full strength," and it makes damage visible and specific. For Eclipse, peeling back shade plates to expose the archive vaults underneath is the faction's visual identity; the repair system should make the player feel those layers.
    What the player decides. How many layers to rebuild per ship, and whether to strip a captured or destroyed hull for plates. The decision is budget allocation across the fleet: do I fully rebuild my destroyer, or patch both my destroyer and my cruiser by one layer each? The player also decides whether to sell a "ripped" capture for credits or strip it for plates, which depends on whether they need plates or credits more.
    What it would break. The single hull-number model becomes a layer model for Eclipse ships. The in-battle regen rate becomes conditional on layer count. The UI must show layers, not just a hull bar. The "1 credit per 2 hull" repair rule is replaced by per-layer costs. Captured-hull handling changes (strip vs. sell). This is the most invasive change of the four Eclipse proposals because it touches the hull model itself.
    Build size. Large. Layer tracking per hull, conditional regen, Reclamation node interactions, stripping mechanic, captured-hull rework, and UI overhaul for the fleet screen.
    
    Approach 4 - Reclamation Swarms and Drone Allocation
    How it works. The "Repair drones" damage-control module and the "Reclamation swarm" doctrine item are the seeds. Between battles, the Eclipse fleet carries a finite pool of repair drones, measured as swarm units (say, 10 units for a small fleet, scaling with command-point cap). At any node, the player allocates swarm units to damaged ships. Each allocated unit repairs a fixed amount of hull per jump (say, 4 hull per unit per jump). The drones work passively as the fleet travels; the player does not need to visit a repair node. After the repair is complete, the drones return to the pool automatically.
    This means Eclipse ships can heal between battles without spending credits, but the healing is slow and capped by the swarm pool. A fleet with three damaged ships and 6 swarm units must choose: spread 2 units across each ship for a slow even patch, or concentrate all 6 on the cruiser to get it battle-ready in one jump while the frigates stay cracked. The "Reclamation swarm" doctrine item increases the pool by a fixed amount, making it a meaningful upgrade.
    The floor: the drone pool is always present and always free. A fleet that lost a fight can allocate its drones at the next node and start healing immediately, no credits required. The healing is slow, but it is guaranteed. This is the Eclipse fantasy: the machines tend to themselves, slowly, in the dark.
    Credits still matter: they are spent at Reclamation nodes for instant repair (bypassing the slow drone work), for shade-plate rebuilds if Approach 3 is adopted, and for Mark upgrades. The drones handle the slow background healing; credits handle the urgent fixes.
    What other games teach. FTL's repair drones heal hull over time during combat; the lesson is that slow, passive repair feels different from instant repair and creates a "do I wait or do I act" tension. Factorio's automation means you set up a system and let it run; the satisfaction is in the system, not the individual action. Rimworld's colonist work queues let you assign labour to tasks and watch it happen over time. Stellaris's construction ships move slowly and build over multiple turns. The lesson: passive, allocated repair makes the player feel like a fleet commander managing assets, not a customer at a repair shop. For Eclipse, the drones are the fleet's own immune system.
    What the player decides. How to allocate the swarm pool across damaged ships, and whether to let the drones work slowly or spend credits for instant repair at a node. The player also decides whether to invest in the "Reclamation swarm" doctrine item to expand the pool. The allocation is re-set at every node, so the decision is ongoing.
    What it would break. Repair is no longer only a credit transaction; it is also a time-and-allocation system. The UI needs a swarm allocation screen. The per-jump repair tick must run during sector travel. The "Repair drones" module and "Reclamation swarm" doctrine item change from passive combat bonuses to active economy levers, which may require rebalancing. If Approach 3 (shade plates) is also adopted, drones might rebuild layers instead of raw hull, which is a cleaner integration but adds complexity.
    Build size. Medium. Swarm pool resource, allocation UI, per-jump repair tick, integration with existing drone module and swarm doctrine item. Does not require a new node type.
    
    Recommended direction - Eclipse
    Adopt Approach 1 (Archive Depth) and Approach 4 (Reclamation Swarms) together as the backbone, and add Approach 2 (Dormant Hull Yards) for fleet growth.
    Archive Depth replaces and extends the existing "Back up the fleet" action. It gives every destroyed ship a path back (Shallow restore is the floor), makes the player think about risk before each battle, and fits the uploaded-mind identity perfectly. The touchpoint with in-battle reanimate is clean: reanimate handles mid-fight losses, Archive Depth handles post-fight losses. They do not overlap.
    Reclamation Swarms handle between-battle hull repair for free, slowly, through drone allocation. This replaces the flat credit-per-hull repair for Eclipse and gives the faction a distinctive "the machines tend themselves" feel. Credits are still spent at Reclamation nodes for instant repair and Mark upgrades, so the credit economy is not abandoned, just complemented.
    Dormant Hull Yards replace shelf-purchases for new Eclipse hulls. Fleet growth is exploration-driven: you find sleeping bodies in the dark and wake them with Archive copies. This fits the existing event, extends it into a repeatable system, and makes the sector map feel like a dead civilisation's graveyard.
    Hold Approach 3 (Shade Plate Reclamation) for a future pass. It is the most flavourful idea but the most invasive: it changes the hull model from a number to layers, which ripples through the battle system, the UI, and the repair economy. It is better introduced once the Archive, Swarm, and Hull Yard systems are stable. When it does arrive, it should integrate with Reclamation Swarms (drones rebuild layers, not raw hull) and with captured-hull stripping (the "ripped" flag finally has a mechanical payoff).
