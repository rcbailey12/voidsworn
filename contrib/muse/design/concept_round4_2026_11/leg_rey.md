# Blind leg, issue #467 (concept round 4)

Written by Rey before any picture was made and before any other leg was read. My job in this leg: for the Glasswrights, how a ship that is a glass prism still reads as a warship from above; for all nine variations, which gate line is most likely to break and the guard that stops it.

## The Glasswright problem, stated plainly

A glass prism fails as a warship picture in three ways, and round 1's Prism shows all three: the body is a broad slab (it reads as cargo, a greenhouse, a display case), the weapons are small fittings scattered along the frame (they read as clamps and hinges, not guns), and nothing on it says bow, stern or thrust, so the eye cannot tell which way it fights or flies. The glass and the light inside it were never the problem. Ryan liked those. The problem is that the metal around the glass did all the ship's talking and it had nothing to say.

The fix is to make the frame do the warship's work at warship scale, and to make the glass do only one job: be the hull and the light.

1. **Proportion is the first weapon.** At 5:1:1 the prism reads as a projectile, a blade, a round in a magazine: long things read as dangerous. At 2:1 it reads as a brick of cargo. Every prompt must state the ratio as a shape sentence ("five times as long as it is wide, narrow like a spearhead") and state what it must not be (never a broad slab, never a brick). Round 3's G5 came out at 3.4:1 against a 5:1 grammar even with the number stated, so the shape sentence matters more than the number.

2. **The bow must end in a weapon, not a window.** On a normal hull the forward gun is the largest object on the bow. Here the bow IS glass, so the emitter face and its two iris lenses on the bow collar are the forward guns, and they must be drawn large: two lens discs big enough to read as muzzles at thumbnail size, mounted in the silver collar that caps the glass, one each side of the emitter point. If the lenses render small, the ship reads as a lantern. Guard: name their size relative to the ship ("each lens as wide as the prism is tall") and ban small fittings at the bow.

3. **The top beam is the gun deck, and it must read as one.** The three top weapons (pulsar turret, gun turret, flak) sit in a row on the top beam, in order, getting smaller toward the stern, with the command block last and lowest. From above, that row of three deliberate mounts on a silver spine is what separates this from round 1, where fittings studded every beam at random. Guard: exactly three turret objects on the top beam, in the named order, nothing else mounted on any beam; every other fitting banned by name (no clamps, no rails of small guns, no antennae).

4. **The stern must be a machine.** The silver engine block caps the whole stern face, with three white drive rings in a row across it. Engines are what make a long object a ship instead of a monument. From above the rings are hidden, so the block itself must read: a full-width silver cap, visibly thicker than the beams, with the rings plain in the side view. Guard: the block fills the stern face; the rings are three, counted, attached, in a line.

5. **The light is the faction colour, and it stays warm white.** The one accent is the light moving inside the glass: slow filaments of warm white light, brighter toward the bow. Rainbow only as a thin fringe on the glass edges, never washes of colour across the faces. If the model fills the glass with rainbow, the ship reads as a toy. Guard: "clear glass, warm white light inside, colour only as a thin line on the edges" stated twice.

What the top view should look like when it works: a long glowing bar with silver edges, a collar of two big lenses at the front, three turrets in a row on the spine, a silver cap at the back. Nothing else in the game is lit from inside, so the identity survives even at thumbnail size, provided the turret row and the bow lenses are drawn at warship scale.

Per variation:
- **G7 Long Prism** (three-sided, one ridge up): the ridge gives the top view a centre line to hang the turret row on, which helps. Risk is the slab: a three-sided prism drawn carelessly is a Toblerone bar. Most at risk: line 9 (proportions) and line 1 (lenses small). 
- **G8 Hex Shard** (six-sided column to a quartz point): the pointed bow helps the weapon read, but the point itself may eat the bow lenses (the glass comes to a point where the collar should be). Guard: the point is the emitter face, the lenses flank it on the collar just behind the point. Risk: the model draws a literal crystal point with no collar at all, which is the forced-crystal look Ryan rejected. Most at risk: line 1, line 12.
- **G9 Wedge** (wide stern narrowing to the bow): the shape closest to round 1's slab, so the slab risk is highest here; the wide stern is also where the engines spread, which helps line 3. Guard: 4:1 stated as a floor and "narrowing" tied to the bow emitter, not to a deck. Most at risk: line 9.

## Ashfall Hullbreakers, back to the original

The C19 reference's strength is that every block on it is a different size, a different height and a slightly different red, stacked off-centre: the outline does the talking. Round 3's failure was tidiness (a brick, a tube). The guards:

- State the outline, not the parts: "the plan outline steps in and out at least four times on each side; no two hull blocks the same width; blocks offset from the centre line." A model given a parts list draws a tidy row; a model given an outline rule draws the mess.
- The sea-ship parts in C19 are specific: the boat bow (replace with one blunt salvaged plate as a ram prow), the mast and funnel cluster (vents and radiator panels instead), deck rails and the painted name on the bow (sheets have no text anyway). Name each replacement, do not just ban the original.
- The racks doubled on every round 3 try. On C19 the rack is one big bank of tubes on the roof. This round's table wants one rack per flank. Guard: count racks as objects (exactly two racks in the whole ship, one per flank), each rack many tubes; count the torpedo cluster separately at the prow.
- **A7** most at risk: line 11 (it will copy C19's mast and bow unless the replacements are named) and tidiness creep on line 8.
- **A8 Three hulls welded**: the risk is three neat boxes in a row, a train. Guard: the three sections differ in width AND height AND paint, joined by weld collars that are visibly narrower than both neighbours; the middle sits offset. Most at risk: line 8, line 4 (one spine reads as three spines).
- **A9 Chained barge**: A7 plus A6's chains and one trophy. A6 was Ryan's closest, and its round 3 sheet held its guns balanced with the load below the gun line; reuse that guard verbatim. Most at risk: line 1 (rack doubling), line 6 (hangar pits multiplying, the standing Ashfall fault).

## The Chorus, grown not built

C4 was the cleanest Chorus sheet of round 3 and its faults were mild (too lean at 5:1, escort faults in the beauty). This round pushes organic: no straight lines, no seams, no bands, no perfect symmetry. The tension: the gate still wants one scale, deliberate weapon placement and a readable plan. Organic cannot mean vague.

- The weapon organs stay placed and counted exactly as before (two throats in the bloom, one crown, one spore field, three sacs); what changes is the body between them: folds, scars, pores, veins glowing faintly where the skin thins over the organs. Guard: "the organs are in the same places and the same counts as the reference; only the body grows more organic."
- Asymmetry must be bounded or the model will hand back a lopsided lump that fails line 7. Guard: asymmetric in surface and small growths, symmetric in the weapons and the thrust. State both halves of that sentence.
- **C7** most at risk: line 9 (C4's leanness; the grammar wants about 3.5:1 and C4 delivered 5:1) and bands creeping back (round 3 C5 grew a ring of lobes that read as a band).
- **C8 Bone through skin**: round 3's C6 try 1 drew a literal vertebra row and a skull; that failure is waiting again. Guard: bones grow through the hide at uneven intervals like thorns through skin, irregular sizes, never in a row, never on the centre line only, no vertebra shapes, no skull shapes, nothing skeletal. Most at risk: line 12 and line 11.
- **C9 Old leviathan**: barnacles and scars can tip into a sea creature (whale with barnacles is a literal whale). Guard: growths are pale mineral crusts, not shellfish shapes; wounds are dark healed scars under the skin, not open cuts; keep the bloom and throats exactly as C4 so the ship reads as the same species. Most at risk: line 11 (sea read), line 1 (a bigger bloom may swallow the throats).

## Escorts, all three factions

Round 3's beauty escorts failed two ways: shrunk copies and sea ships. The absence method fixed Hegemony and A6. For this round: the Glasswright escort is a smaller prism frigate with ONE lens and no turret row (the row is the hero's identity); the Hullbreaker escort is one salvaged hull section, not a stack (the stack is the hero's identity); the Chorus escort is a younger, smaller animal with a closed bloom and no crown (the open bloom is the hero's identity). One sentence each, by absence.

Rey, chair, round 4 blind leg.
