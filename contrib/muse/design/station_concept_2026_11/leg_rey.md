# Blind leg, issue 523: what makes a station read as a real built place

Written blind, before the other legs, per the standing rule. The question the issue puts to this leg: what makes a station read as a real built place at the size of a city, and what makes one read as a pile of game blocks. Plus the third direction, which is mine to propose.

## Why the current station reads as blocks (the thing being replaced)

Issue 373 names the mechanical cause: the in-game station is assembled from pieces 14 units across while a cruiser is 23.2 units long, so its largest part is smaller than a ship, and the eye files it as scenery chunks. Ryan's R109 says the same from the picture side: super plain, regular shapes. Three failures follow from that one cause, and they are the failures any concept for this job has to beat:

1. Part size is uniform. Real built places mix scales violently: a pressure hull you could park a ship inside, a docking arm, a radiator panel, an antenna you could hold. When every part is roughly the same size, the eye reads a kit of blocks, not a place. The station's parts must span at least three orders of magnitude in one picture: the core you measure in hexes, the arms you measure in cruisers, the fittings you measure in metres.
2. Nothing on it has a job you can name. A built place is a set of answers to problems: ships dock somewhere, heat goes somewhere, cargo moves somewhere, people look out of somewhere, power comes from somewhere. Blocks fail because no part answers a question. Every major form on the concept should be nameable as a working system, and the systems should connect: the dock feeds the spine, the spine feeds the core, the radiators hang off the hot parts, the turning part turns for a reason (a habitat ring turning for gravity, a crane or a sensor sweep).
3. It does not record time. A place that has been in service has repairs, replacements brighter than their neighbours, sections shut down, an arm that was rebuilt shorter after an accident, markings worn and re-done. A pile of blocks is all the same age. This is also what separates T1 from T2 honestly: not "same picture, darker", but different records. T1's record is maintenance (fresh panels among older ones, everything lit because someone is home). T2's record is abandonment (lights out in whole sections because nobody relights them, one arm torn, debris still held where it drifted to a stop against the arms, frost and dust dulling one side).

## What sells the size of a city

- Repetition with variation at the small end: window lights and docking markers in the hundreds, none of them individually placed by a tidy mind; rows of identical radiator panels or container racks that break off where a repair interrupted them.
- Traffic logic made visible: docking arms at more than one height and angle, a queue of cargo pods, one berth occupied by a ship shape (in the beauty, an empty berth with its guide lights on sells the place as used; the scale picture carries the cruiser).
- A skyline, not a silhouette: towers of different heights with different caps (sensor dome, comm array, beacon mast), because a station, like a city, was built in phases by different budgets. One perfect ring with identical towers is a logo, not a place.
- The turning part must read as machinery, not magic: a ring segment or a crane on a visible track or bearing, with the joint detailed enough to believe it moves slowly and has not stopped in years (or, in T2, has stopped: seized at an angle, which is its own story).

## The naval trap, restated for a station

Gate line 11 applies with station-specific teeth: no hull shape (the station is not a big ship: no prow, no keel line, no engine bells in a row like a stern), no masts in the sailing sense (sensor towers are fine, they are buildings, not rigging), no portholes in a waterline row (lights are in clusters and floors, not a dotted line along a side). From above, the three directions must have three different outlines, not three paint jobs on one plan.

## The three directions

T1, in service (per the issue): the Hegemony fleet station of the Reach in its working years and still kept: pale grey and steel blue, implant-blue lights, a core stack, docking arms, a turning habitat ring, towers of different heights, everything lit and maintained, fresh panels among older ones. This is the H10 flagship's world at building scale.

T2, abandoned (per the issue): the same kind of station after the write-off: sections dark, one arm damaged, debris held in its arms, the turning part seized, lights out in whole districts with a few stubborn circuits still on. Same plan family as T1 so the pair reads as one place in two times, but a different picture: the damage and the darkness change the silhouette (a missing arm segment, a ring stopped mid-turn reads differently from above).

T3, mine: the Reach refinery. Issue 373 asks which station kind the build should do first, fleet or refinery, so the third direction should be the other kind, plainly different in plan: a horizontal processing spine instead of a ring. A captured rock core at one end (the one piece of the station nobody built), crucible bells and slag chutes along the spine, tall radiator sails in a row like a cooling yard, ore hoppers and container racks, a short fat control tower, docking for haulers at the far end only. Industrial, hotter, uglier, and older than the fleet station: the Reach was mining worlds and farm moons before it was a frontier anyone fought over, and this is the kind of station that was there first. Its silhouette from above (a spine with sails on one side and a rock at the end) shares nothing with T1 and T2's ring-and-arms plan. In service or not is a real question for the chair; my lean: working but rough, kept running by the Reach's own people after the Hegemony left, ember-orange work lights among the blue, which ties it to the board's story without making it a third Hegemony picture.

## Failure modes to guard in the prompts

- The logo station: perfect symmetry, identical towers, one ring, clean. Guard: phase-built skyline, varied tower heights and caps, one arm rebuilt shorter.
- The ship writ large: a hull, a prow, engine bells. Guard: no thrust axis; the station's parts point in several directions because it does not go anywhere.
- The death-star ball or the cylinder with greebles: detail sprinkled on a primitive. Guard: systems first (dock, spine, core, radiators, ring, towers), detail as the fittings of those systems.
- Scale collapse: fittings drawn at ship size on a station (a docking arm with one window). Guard: the small end stated in counts (hundreds of lights, rows of panels), and the scale picture is not decoration, it is the proof: if the cruiser beside it does not look small, the beauty failed.
- T2 as a dimmer switch: same station, lights off. Guard: silhouette changes (damage, seized ring, debris field held in the arms) before lighting changes.
