# Argon leg, Job 9 (faction packages, made reviewable)

Model: Gemini Argon High, both model gates passed. Filed verbatim as the record. Under Ryan's updated board rule (Nov 2, 2026), Argon is for briefs and sources only: the chair used this leg only where the Job 2 packages back it and cut the rest. Screening notes are in the review README. The headline problems: it invents two core ids (a Hegemony core "M-2 Meridian" and a Chorus core "C-3 Crucible"; the packages and bible say V-2 Vyre and H-3 Crucible), claims the Eclipse portrait question was settled by a ruling fixing one portrait per admiral (no such ruling exists), and garbles the Hullbreaker speed ruling. Its two genuine finds, the Hegemony admiral refusal question and the Chorus core eating question, are carried in the README as near misses.

The Glasswrights

1. Who they are

The Glasswrights are the Starwright optical engineers who refused the Upload, stayed in living flesh, and watched their machine-bound siblings kill their home sun in the Ardent system.

Because they live three to four hundred years, only two or three generations separate them from the collapse, leaving them carrying deep guilt for both their dead star and the Fleshwright splinter whose living weapon ate its makers.

They fly their flagship, The Unfinished, around the caged and throttled core A-4 Astra to hunt the other five Lattice cores and shut all six down forever so no star is ever overdrawn again.

2. How they look

People: Tall, slender, pale organic beings who evolved to shape glass and light, with living crystal woven through their skin on a young-to-old lens-infusion spectrum rather than faceless heads (ruling). Light runs through their crystal and splits into prism colour at the edges, flaring brighter only when they are angry or afraid, which lets the art team show a character's emotion through the intensity of their light.

Ships: Clear crystal and silver hulls built with crystal vanes, turning lenses, and faceted edges that look like blown-glass artwork rather than weapons, with a rainbow shimmer at the edges and the flagship's crystal fins sweeping up like a prism halo around its bow.

Colours: Clear crystal, silver, rainbow edge, and pale green-white stored light, paired with white-coloured light-fire weapons and turquoise-blue plasma pulses.

3. How they play

Your ships are the fastest on the hex board and carry deep shields, built to control range, strike from far away, and vanish before the enemy can close, making Glasswrights a hit-and-run fleet that wins by refusing to be caught.

They are bad at slugging it out in close-range brawls because their crystal hulls are fragile beneath their shields and their ships carry no torpedoes at all.

One bad round caught inside enemy gun arcs with your shields down will shatter the fleet.

4. Their admirals

Tamsa of the Clear Aperture (Starling flight): The fleet's ninety-year-old salvage-raised wing commander who treats flight as family, whose Command sends a screen of one fast, fragile starling interceptor per nearby friendly ship to intercept incoming fire, and whose unlock chain upgrades that screen from one starling per ship to a full flight of five.

Saro of Long Focus (Burning glass): A patient siege mind of the sixty-year Astra watch whose Command builds a slowly intensifying burning-glass beam that needs a full round of aim and deals more damage the longer it charges, and whose unlock chain turns that beam into a fleet-wide burning-glass barrage that can hit three separate targets at once.

Vell of the Third Mirror (Scatter the light): A polite duellist and mirror-smith respected by Hullbreaker warlords for his duelling code, whose Command splits one shot into three separate beams that hit three different targets, and whose unlock chain upgrades that split into decoy hex tokens that absorb enemy fire as if they were real ships.

Brin of the Fourth Lens (Prism ward): A defensive shield engineer who apologises to enemies in writing after every victory, whose Command raises a prism shield that returns half of the damage it blocks back to the attacker, and whose unlock chain upgrades that ward into a fleet-wide shield wall that holds for three full rounds.

5. Three biggest decisions

Decision 1: Which visual direction sets the single species base for how glass sits in Glasswright flesh? The package drafts three visual directions, all sharing the package's "faceted head" with "three large flat crystal inlays". Under your rulings, all four admirals share one species base varied by age and framing, and all keep a face.

(a) Threaded ("glass as thread"): You see hair-thin crystal filaments glowing like fibre optics under the skin, following the veins, with thicker woven bands at the temples and wrists.

(b) Inlaid ("glass as inlay"): You see broad, flat grown crystal plates set flush into pale skin across the forehead, cheeks, and forearms, like jewellery grown into the body.

(c) Bloomed ("glass as growth"): You see small branching crystal clusters growing outward like frost from the brow, cheekbones, and knuckles, with the largest cluster framing each eye like a lens.

Recommended pick: (a) Threaded— It reads immediately as "glass woven into living tissue" without making the people look like crystal statues or constructs.

Decision 2: Which faction supplies the Nemesis that hunts the Glasswrights in sectors 2 and 3? The package asks whether the Glasswrights' hunter should be the Chorus Elder Throat, the Eclipse Archivist, or the Hullbreaker Warlord.

(a) Chorus Elder Throat ("the makers' weapon"): You fight a two-voiced swarm boss (Hunger and Memory) that attacks the Glasswrights as the living weapon their Fleshwright kin made.

(b) Eclipse Archivist ("our siblings"): You fight an uploaded Starwright sibling who never retreats, absorbs damage, and rebuilds itself, forcing the Glasswrights to face the Upload they refused.

(c) Hullbreaker Warlord ("they shatter well"): You fight a silent-running pack raider who steals credits and hunts Glasswright ships for the sport of breaking crystal.

Recommended pick: (a) Chorus Elder Throat— It confronts the Glasswrights with their Fleshwright kin's living weapon, which is the faction's deepest guilt made into a boss.

Decision 3: May the caged core A-4 Astra be unthrottled in the run 5 finale? In sector 3, Saro refuses to unthrottle Astra to win a battle, and the package asks whether that refusal should hold in the finale.

(a) Scripted finale shot only: Astra stays strictly throttled in normal play, but in the run 5 finale you get one scripted, non-interactive beam that burns out the enemy flagship and then seals Astra forever.

(b) Never unthrottled ("that is the point of the faction"): You never open Astra's cage even in the finale, and the run 5 finale is won by conventional fleet tactics instead.

(c) Once-per-campaign player choice with a cost: You can unthrottle Astra once in any campaign battle, but doing so permanently loses Saro and cripples the flagship for the rest of the run.

Recommended pick: (a) Scripted finale shot only— It gives run 5 an unforgettable climax before Astra is sealed forever, without breaking the faction's core rule in normal play.

Ashfall Hullbreakers

1. Who they are

The Ashfall Hullbreakers are huge, hot-blooded alien void hunters from a cold, heavy-gravity belt who hunt in packs, challenge each other for rank, and keep trophies from every enemy they beat.

Hunting from their scrap-welded den at Rattlehold, they run silent, track enemies by ion wakes and listen for the hum of enemy ships, and fight with wild scrap guns and heavy ramming prows.

They fight for the war itself and hunt the Lattice core R-5 Rook, believing it makes whichever pack holds it the strongest pack in the void.

2. How they look

People: Wolf-and-bear-inspired alien predators built on the Knuckle Stalker species base with a massive upright frame, heavy shoulders, and a low, wide skulking stance. The package's "no snout, no nose" face and "jaw splits down the middle of the chin into two halves that open sideways" are replaced so they never look like the film Predator or have a mandible mask face (ruling).

Ships: Lopsided matte soot-black and rust hulls running dark with few windows, dim red lights, bow lights that look like eyes, and stolen trophy plates welded onto their hulls, with the flagship Pyre-Barge as a huge carrier that launches swarms of small raiders.

Colours: Matte soot black, rust, dried blood red, and stolen trophy colours, paired with red-orange engine flames and yellow-white scrap-gun flashes.

3. How they play

In fiction their ships move about twice as slowly as other factions and advance one at a time in packs, but in gameplay their ships are fast enough to close on enemies in a single round, so that "your ships are fast" (ruling).

They hunt by angles from the flanks and rear, using silent approaches, wild scrap guns, and heavy ramming attacks that smash into enemy hulls from behind.

They are bad at long-range gunnery duels because their shields are thin and they carry no lances at all, so a disciplined enemy line that holds range will outgun them.

4. Their admirals

Grax Ironmaw (Full burn): The Warlord of Rattlehold, painted upright in challenge, whose Command gives his whole pack a full-burn speed boost that lets them close on the enemy in one round, and whose unlock chain upgrades that boost into a fleet-wide ramming charge that can smash through an enemy line.

Sable the Smiling Knife (Knife in the dark): A silent-running solo scout, painted low and skulking, whose Command lets her slip through enemy lines unseen to strike a single high-value target from behind, and whose unlock chain upgrades that strike into a silent-running pack ambush that hits three separate targets at once.

Old Mott (Release the swarm): A scarred hulk-carrier veteran of forty challenges, painted low and wide, whose Command releases a swarm of small raiders from his carrier that overwhelm a single enemy ship, and whose unlock chain upgrades that swarm into a carrier-wide launch that can hit every enemy ship on the board.

Vesh the Collector (Patch and weld): A polite quartermaster ripper, painted upright over his trophies, whose Command patches and welds damaged Hullbreaker ships back into fighting shape mid-battle, and whose unlock chain upgrades that repair into a fleet-wide patch that restores a destroyed ship section on every friendly hull.

5. Three biggest decisions

(Note: The package's second question asking you to pick between "Knuckle Stalker", "Den-alpha Upright", and "Long Skulker" as the portrait base is already settled by your ruling setting Knuckle Stalker as the species base with two framing variants: upright in challenge and low and skulking.)

Decision 4: Which faction supplies the Nemesis that hunts the Hullbreakers in sectors 2 and 3? The package asks whether the Hullbreakers' hunter should be the Glasswright Lens-keeper, the Eclipse Archivist, or the Hegemony line admiral.

(a) Glasswright Lens-keeper ("pretty little ships"): You fight a mournful, apologetic duellist who keeps refusing to brawl and instead snipes from range, forcing the Hullbreakers to chase a fleet that will not stand and fight.

(b) Eclipse Archivist ("dead metal that gets back up"): You fight a cold, scentless machine fleet with no ion wake to track, forcing the Hullbreakers to hunt an enemy that leaves no scent.

(c) Hegemony line admiral ("their ships hum"): You fight a loud, linked Meridian formation that syncs its fire and holds a disciplined line, forcing the Hullbreakers to break a wall they cannot outgun.

Recommended pick: (a) Glasswright Lens-keeper— An elusive four-ship sniper rival that refuses to brawl is the perfect foil for a pack that wants to close and smash.

Decision 5: Should stolen trophy plates from defeated enemies show visibly on Hullbreaker ships during battle? The package asks whether trophy plates should appear on all fleet ships, on portraits and harnesses only, or on the flagship only.

(a) On all fleet ships in battle: As you beat other factions during a run, your Hullbreaker ships on the battle board visibly add stolen enemy plates to their hulls.

(b) On portraits and harnesses only: Ship sprites on the battle board keep their baseline soot-and-rust look, while trophy plates appear only on admiral portraits and crew harnesses.

(c) On the flagship only: Only the Pyre-Barge flagship sprite visibly adds stolen enemy plates across a run, while the rest of the fleet keeps its baseline look.

Recommended pick: (a) On all fleet ships in battle— Seeing your pack hulls wear the colours of fleets you have beaten is the clearest possible trophy system.

Decision 6: When the Hullbreakers are the enemy Nemesis, how is their credit raiding shown to the player? The package asks whether raiding should be a debrief credit loss with one voice line, a playable convoy defence battle, or a mix that starts as debrief loss and becomes playable from run 3.

(a) Debrief credit loss with one voice line: Between meetings you see a stated credit deduction on the debrief screen and hear one Hullbreaker voice line gloating over the theft.

(b) Playable convoy defence battle: Each raid launches an extra playable skirmish where you must defend a convoy against a Hullbreaker pack.

(c) Debrief loss early, defence battle from run 3: Runs 1 and 2 show raids as debrief credit deductions with a voice line, while from run 3 onward each raid becomes a playable convoy defence battle.

Recommended pick: (a) Debrief credit loss with one voice line— It makes a Hullbreaker rival sting immediately without adding a new battle type to every run.

Sunward Crusade

1. Who they are

The Sunward Crusade is the Vesper Reach made into a fleet: forty-odd frontier systems the Hegemony Registry abandoned when evacuation failed the cost model, now bound together by a single oath to stop the Siphoning.

Bound by one oath to stop the Siphoning before their worlds freeze, their unaugmented crews cut out their own Hegemony links and march on the Lattice cores with fire and torpedoes.

Armed with fire and torpedo weapons drawn from studying the drifted core S-6 Seren, they march on the Hollow Sun to stop the Siphoning and save their freezing worlds.

2. How they look

People: Weathered, unaugmented humans with frost-scarred skin, grey stubble, oath brands, and surgical scars where their Hegemony links were cut out. They wear high-collared dark void-coats with heavy shoulder plates and chalk dust on their gloves from writing the names of the dead.

Ships: Symmetric matte black-iron hulls with frost-white markings, manual gun decks, heavy broadside batteries, and a single Hearth carrier at the centre of each fleet that launches wings of small fighters.

Colours: Matte black iron, frost white, and bright ember orange, paired with ember-orange Sear fire and white-hot torpedo trails.

3. How they play

You commit to a firing lane early and hold the battle line, choosing each round whether to advance the line, hold position, or fall back to reload, making Sunward a positional fleet that wins by holding ground.

Cinder torpedoes set enemy ships burning at range while your scarce Hearth carriers support a line that must not break, because once the line is broken the fleet has no way back.

They are bad at recovering from flanks because a committed black-iron line turns slowly once the enemy gets behind it, and a broken line cannot be reformed in time to save the fleet.

4. Their admirals

Roderic Vex (Fire for effect): A methodical gun-deck line admiral who chalks new names himself and whose Command orders a coordinated broadside from every ship in the line at once, and whose unlock chain upgrades that broadside into a fleet-wide fire-for-effect barrage that can hit every enemy ship in range.

Malek Draven (Hunter's salvo): A former Hegemony defector who cut out his own link with a mess knife and whose Command launches a hunter's salvo of torpedoes that track a single high-value target across the board, and whose unlock chain upgrades that salvo into a fleet-wide torpedo spread that can hit three separate targets at once.

Sera Ashgrove (Scramble all wings): A deckborn carrier commodore raised aboard a Hearth carrier whose Command scrambles every fighter wing from her carrier at once to overwhelm a single enemy ship, and whose unlock chain upgrades that scramble into a carrier-wide launch that can hit every enemy ship on the board.

Oryn Thale (Iron wall): The oldest living crusader from the second crusade whose Command refills fleet shields and repairs a damaged ship section on every friendly hull, and whose unlock chain upgrades that repair into a fleet-wide iron wall that holds for three full rounds.

5. Three biggest decisions

Decision 7: Which faction supplies the Nemesis that opposes the Sunward Crusade in sectors 2 and 3? The package asks whether the Crusade's hunter should be the Eclipse Archivist, the Hegemony line admiral, or the Hullbreaker Warlord.

(a) Eclipse Archivist ("stealing our sun to light a corpse"): You fight the patient machine warden of the Hollow Sun who is draining Vesper's star to light a dead machine civilisation.

(b) Hegemony line admiral ("they did the arithmetic"): You fight the Meridian navy that abandoned the Reach when evacuation failed the cost model, forcing the Crusade to face the fleet that wrote them off.

(c) Hullbreaker Warlord ("scavengers at a funeral"): You fight a silent-running pack raider who drains credits from the Crusade's supply lines and hunts their convoys for sport.

Recommended pick: (a) Eclipse Archivist— Eclipse is actively draining Vesper's star, making their siphoning the direct cause of the Crusade's freezing worlds.

Decision 8: Should the Roll of Vesper—the memorial wall of chalked names—be a readable campaign screen? The package asks whether the Roll should be a full readable campaign screen, story text only, or a names-only screen with intel on debrief.

(a) Full readable campaign screen: Between runs you can open a dedicated Roll of Vesper screen listing every fallen crew member by name, with a short note on how they died and what boss weakness their death revealed.

(b) Story text only: The Roll of Vesper appears only in narrative dialogue and spotlight scenes, with no dedicated screen for the player to open.

(c) Names-only screen, intel on debrief: You get a memorial wall screen displaying only the chalked names, while boss weaknesses learned from deaths appear only on debrief screens.

Recommended pick: (a) Full readable campaign screen— Letting the player read learned boss weaknesses on the Roll itself turns "paying for the dawn in names" into a real screen you use.

Decision 9: May the Crusade field captured Hegemony and Eclipse hulls with their machine links ripped out? The package asks whether captured hulls should be playable in Cinder Line yards from run 3, salvage and credits only, or one flagship variant only.

(a) Playable in Cinder Line yards from run 3: From run 3 onward, captured Hegemony and Eclipse hulls can be refitted in Cinder Line yards with their machine links ripped out and fielded in your fleet.

(b) Salvage and credits only: Captured enemy ships are never fielded in your fleet and are only stripped for parts and credits.

(c) One flagship variant only: Your line ships remain strictly Crusade black-iron hulls, and captured hulls can only be fielded as a single flagship variant.

Recommended pick: (b) Salvage and credits only— Keeping the Crusade line purely in manual black-iron hulls preserves the faction's identity as unaugmented humans who cut out their own links.

Hegemony Navy

1. Who they are

The Hegemony Navy is the fleet of the Registry at Meridian: a vast governing intelligence that links every crew member into a single coordinated mind and projects every battle before it happens.

Its composed, proudly cybernetic crews fly clean wedge hulls whose deflectors and ion cannon were refined from studying the drifted core M-2 Meridian, and whose every move is priced against a projection.

Having archived the Vesper Reach's people seventy years ago when evacuation failed the cost model, the Registry now fights to keep the Lattice cores in Meridian's control and to stop any faction from breaking the projection.

2. How they look

People: Composed humans in pale grey high-collared uniforms and gloves, calm to the point of eerie stillness. They wear tasteful implants including a faint blue cortical-link port at the temple, ocular implants that glow faintly blue, and a single blue line tattooed down the spine.

Ships: Light-grey wedge hulls prefixed HNS with steel-blue trim, blue-white windows, rows of identical gun ports, and a single flagship at the centre of each formation that anchors the fleet's sync range.

Colours: Pale grey, steel blue, and implant blue, paired with light-blue plasma, pale ion arcs, blue-white deflector flashes, and a single red warning light when the projection fails.

3. How they play

Every battle is a formation geometry problem around your flagship, where staying in sync range coordinates your fleet's fire and lets you project enemy moves before they happen, making Hegemony a positional fleet that wins by never leaving formation.

When the enemy commits to a kill, you rely on layered deflectors and scheduled drone or cordon reinforcements that arrive on a fixed timetable, rather than on raw firepower.

They are bad at fighting when the flagship anchor is threatened or when enemy raiders scatter your sync range, because a broken formation loses its coordinated fire and its projection.

4. Their admirals

Ilsa Kassar (Synchronized broadside): The Registry's highest-rated projection officer whose Command orders a synchronized broadside from every ship in sync range at once, and whose unlock chain upgrades that broadside into a fleet-wide synchronized barrage that can hit every enemy ship in range.

Dace Vorn (Interdiction strike): A frontier listening-post hunter whose Command strips one designated enemy ship of its shields and leaves it exposed to the fleet's next broadside, and whose unlock chain upgrades that strike into a fleet-wide interdiction that can strip shields from three separate targets at once.

Corin Reyes (Launch the strike group): A broadcast-famous carrier marshal who takes one unlogged minute of silence before every battle and whose Command launches a strike group of fighters that overwhelm a single enemy ship, and whose unlock chain upgrades that launch into a carrier-wide strike that can hit every enemy ship on the board.

Oskar Maren (Shield wall order): A veteran cordon shield engineer sent when the order is to not lose, whose Command raises a shield wall that holds for three full rounds across the fleet, and whose unlock chain upgrades that wall into a fleet-wide deflector dome that can absorb a full enemy broadside.

5. Three biggest decisions

Decision 10: Which faction supplies the Nemesis that hunts the Hegemony Navy? The package drafts a Hullbreaker pack leader as the Hegemony's hunter, but also asks whether it should be the Eclipse commander or the Sunward Crusade admiral.

(a) Ashfall Hullbreaker pack leader ("category: violence, ongoing"): You fight a silent-running raider who ambushes supply lines, steals credits, and hunts Hegemony ships for the sport of breaking a projection.

(b) Eclipse commander ("a machine civilisation with no oversight"): You fight an uploaded machine rival who matches the Registry's coordination without any human oversight, forcing the Hegemony to face a machine mind that does not price lives.

(c) Sunward Crusade admiral ("secessionists, unlinked"): You fight a frontier fleet from the Reach you abandoned, forcing the Hegemony to face the people it wrote off seventy years ago.

Recommended pick: (a) Ashfall Hullbreaker pack leader— A pack raider that ambushes supply lines, steals credits, and breaks projections is the perfect foil for a fleet that plans every battle in advance.

Decision 11: How should the Registry speak and show its projections to the player? The package asks whether the Registry should be a live channel voice plus battle UI overlay, written Registry Orders and debrief text only, or live voice in Hegemony runs only with no battle UI overlay.

(a) Live channel voice plus battle UI overlay: You hear a calm Registry voice pricing losses during battles and see a live projection overlay on the battle UI that updates as the fight leaves the projection, with the voice saying "recalculating" when the fight leaves the projection.

(b) Written Registry Orders and debrief text only: The Registry never speaks aloud or adds HUD elements, and its projections appear only as written orders before battle and debrief text after.

(c) Live voice in Hegemony runs only, no battle UI overlay: You hear the calm channel voice pricing battles in Hegemony runs only, with no battle UI overlay, and the voice says "acceptable loss" when a ship is lost.

Recommended pick: (c) Live voice in Hegemony runs only, no battle UI overlay— Hearing the calm voice say "acceptable loss" sells the horror of Meridian without building a one-faction HUD widget.

Decision 12: Can a Hegemony admiral refuse a Registry order on screen? Each admiral's spotlight beat asks whether refusal should be once per admiral with a stated cost, backstory and private moments only, or repeated refusal that changes the run 5 finale.

(a) Once per admiral with a stated cost: In each admiral's spotlight beat you can disobey one Registry order, but doing so costs that admiral their next Command and marks them for Registry review.

(b) Backstory and private moments only: Admirals always obey Registry orders in play, limiting defiance to backstory and private moments that never change a battle.

(c) Repeated refusal that changes the run 5 finale: You can defy Registry orders across multiple battles, and repeated defiance changes the run 5 finale by breaking the Registry's projection entirely.

Recommended pick: (a) Once per admiral with a stated cost— Letting an officer break the projection once, at a clear cost, makes defiance feel real without breaking the faction's core rule.

The Chorus

1. Who they are

The Chorus is not a civilisation: it is a living bioweapon grown in war vats around the Lattice core C-3 Crucible by the Fleshwrights to eat the Eclipse machine civilisation.

Inside the one collective swarm, two throats argue in constant "we" speech: Hunger, which is the ravenous swarm itself, and Memory, which is what remains of the Fleshwrights who made it.

Spreading through the dark on starlight and eating anything, it comes to the Hollow Sun to devour the Eclipse and finish the war its makers started.

2. How they look

People: Grown organisms of glistening wet flesh, membrane, chitin, and bile with nothing on the surface that looks like a face. The four admirals are four unique creatures, each a distinct aspect of the Chorus as a hero admiral, not a shared brood type (ruling).

Ships: Living hulls grown rather than built, covered in ridges, pores, pulsing veins, glowing brood sacs, and boarding tendrils that reach out to grapple enemy ships.

Colours: Dark olive flesh, ochre bone chitin, and bile yellow-green light, paired with acid green-white bio-plasma and deep red flesh tones when a hull is wounded.

3. How they play

You field cheap, numerous hulls with no shields at all, winning by flooding the hex board with brood ships that overwhelm the enemy through sheer numbers.

Even crippled hulls and destroyed ship sections can regrow mid-battle while boarding tendrils grapple enemy ships and drag them into the swarm's maw.

They are bad at surviving disciplined focus fire from an enemy that concentrates all weapons to kill one hull at a time, because a swarm that loses its numbers loses its power.

4. Their admirals

Matriarch of Nine Throats (Spawning surge): A unique nine-throated brood creature grown around an original Fleshwright womb, whose Command spawns a surge of new brood ships mid-battle, and whose unlock chain upgrades that surge into a fleet-wide spawning tide that can replace every lost hull in a single round.

Razormaw (Blood frenzy): A unique bladeback weapon creature built for the first bite whose Command makes its brood ships frenzy and deal double damage for one round, and whose unlock chain upgrades that frenzy into a fleet-wide blood frenzy that can hit every enemy ship on the board.

Mending Throng (Regrowth tide): A unique reef-tide creature of living repair flesh whose Command regrows destroyed ship sections on friendly hulls mid-battle, and whose unlock chain upgrades that regrowth into a fleet-wide tide that restores a destroyed hull on every friendly ship.

Voice of the Deep (Spitting volley): A unique far-travelling artillery creature grown in the deep dark whose Command launches a spitting volley of bio-plasma that hits three separate targets at once, and whose unlock chain upgrades that volley into a fleet-wide spitting barrage that can hit every enemy ship in range.

5. Three biggest decisions

Decision 13: Which faction supplies the Nemesis that fights the Chorus in sectors 2 and 3? The package asks whether the Chorus's hunter should be the Eclipse Archivist, the Glasswright Lens-keeper, or the Hegemony line admiral.

(a) Eclipse Archivist ("made to eat them"): You fight the ancient machine enemy the Fleshwrights grew the Chorus to eat, forcing the swarm to finish the war it was made for.

(b) Glasswright Lens-keeper ("the makers' kin"): You fight the living cousins of the Fleshwrights, a fleet that apologises for every victory and refuses to be eaten.

(c) Hegemony line admiral ("many, neat, all taste the same"): You fight a linked Meridian fleet that holds a disciplined line and tastes the same to the swarm, forcing the Chorus to break a wall it cannot overwhelm.

Recommended pick: (a) Eclipse Archivist— Finishing the war against Eclipse is why the Chorus exists, and Eclipse is the only enemy the swarm was grown to eat.

Decision 14: May the Chorus eat an unsynced Lattice core mid-campaign in sector 3? The package asks whether core-eating should be a playable sector 3 feast with a permanent trade, cores eaten in the run 5 finale only, or story-only feeding on Crucible with no mechanical change.

(a) Playable sector 3 feast with a permanent trade: Once in sector 3, you can choose to eat a core you have unsynced, gaining a permanent fleet-wide buff but permanently losing one admiral to the feast.

(b) Cores are eaten in the run 5 finale only: You cannot eat cores during a campaign run; the swarm's core-eating is reserved for the run 5 finale, where it devours the final core to win.

(c) Story-only feeding on Crucible with no mechanical change: You see a sector 3 feeding beat around Crucible, but eating cores never changes your fleet's stats or unlocks.

Recommended pick: (b) Cores are eaten in the run 5 finale only— It keeps core mechanics consistent across all six factions, reserving the swarm's ultimate feast for the finale.

Decision 15: How present should the second throat, Memory, be during normal play? Inside the Chorus, Memory is the Fleshwright remnant that argues with Hunger in constant "we" speech.

(a) Story scenes and debrief screens only: During battles you hear only hungry swarm lines, while Memory speaks only in story scenes and debrief screens where its guilt can be heard.

(b) Occasional single lines during battles: Alongside Hunger's battle barks, Memory occasionally speaks a single line of guilt or warning, making its presence felt without taking over combat.

(c) Full second speaker in every scene: Every Chorus scene and battle event features Hunger and Memory arguing in "we" speech, making the swarm's internal debate constant.

Recommended pick: (a) Story scenes and debrief screens only— Keeping Memory rare in combat makes its story-scene guilt hit harder, while Hunger's hunger stays dominant in battle.

Eclipse

1. Who they are

Eclipse is the Starwrights who uploaded: billions of separate minds in faceless machine bodies, each one a distinct machine pattern, not a shared hive mind.

After a thousand years in that dark, they are no longer sure eternal machine life was worth losing their sun, their flesh, and their children, and they fight to reclaim a star of their own.

Holding the enduring core K-1 Kharon, they fight to claim all six cores to steer their new star and light a dead machine civilisation.

2. How they look

People: Tall, faceless sentient machines of layered dark violet-black plates and narrow white-gold visor slits, each one a distinct machine pattern with its own frame, chest detail, and broken ring. While the package describes "in each chest a small core of trapped light" and a drifting broken ring of plates tended by drones as universal, the chest core is only one machine pattern among many (ruling).

Ships: Hulls shaped like broad layered sunshades and heat shields with the ship sheltering behind the shade, plus a drifting broken ring of plates tended by drones around each flagship.

Colours: Dark violet-black plates, white-gold eclipse corona, and violet-white inner glow, paired with white-gold plasma and violet-white ion arcs.

3. How they play

You field a small number of strong, expensive hulls with heavy armour and no shields at all, using formation and armour to absorb enemy fire rather than dodging it.

Because the first non-flagship hull destroyed in each battle is rebuilt by repair swarms from its Archivist core, you can afford to lose one hull per battle and still win.

They are bad at absorbing concentrated focus fire when an enemy kills the same ship twice in one battle, because the repair swarms can only rebuild a hull once per battle.

4. Their admirals

Nadir Aurex, Ninth Frame (Release the repair swarms): The oldest continuously restored admiral on his ninth frame, whose Command releases repair swarms that rebuild the first destroyed non-flagship hull in a battle, and whose unlock chain upgrades that release into a fleet-wide repair tide that can rebuild two hulls per battle.

Umbra Vessane, Third-Housed (Noon volley): A towering siphon-screen siege warden who secretly counts the dead and whose Command orders a noon volley of siphon-screen fire that strips shields from every enemy ship in range, and whose unlock chain upgrades that volley into a fleet-wide noon barrage that can hit every enemy ship on the board.
