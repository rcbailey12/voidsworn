# Faction design packages, first drafts (blind leg)

Six complete packages, drafted from the evidence pack only. Shipped Command names and effects are kept as given in the pack. Anything marked (new) is a proposed mechanic with its numbers defined.

---

## 1. Sunward Crusade

### Identity

The Sunward Crusade is the Vesper Reach: forty-odd frontier systems the Hegemony wrote off when their stars began to dim, now sailing under one oath to burn out whatever is stealing their light. Its fleets are unaugmented humans in black-iron battleships, every dead name chalked on the hull by hand because the Registry deleted those names from its projections. They are not a church and not an empire; they are a debt being collected, one generation at a time.

Playing the Crusade is about commitment. Broadsides hit harder after a ship has moved two or more hexes, so you are always choosing between holding a safe line and driving forward to make the guns count, and once you brace and hold, the fleet is very hard to shift. Your best moment is the held volley: the line stops, the torpedoes are already burning in the waterless dark, and the whole fleet fires as one.

### Lore

Origin: the Vesper Reach, the Hegemony's outer frontier, seceded about seventy years ago after the Registry calculated that evacuating billions was not cost-effective and archived the Reach instead. One core, S-6 Seren, drifted into Vesper's outer belt long ago, and the Crusade's Sear guns and Cinder torpedoes were reverse-built from studying it. What they want: destroy Eclipse's siphon arrays, stop the Siphoning, take back their stars, and prove the Hegemony's arithmetic wrong in front of it.

How they see the others:

- Hegemony Navy: "They did the arithmetic. We are what was left over."
- Ashfall Hullbreakers: "Scavengers at a funeral."
- The Chorus: "The dark that eats. Burn it."
- The Glasswrights: "Their siblings run the siphons. Sorry does not warm a world."
- Eclipse: "They are stealing our sun to light a corpse."

### Look

People: weathered, unaugmented humans, frost-scarred skin, surgical scars where implants were cut out, oath brands on the forearm, grey stubble, chalk dust on gloves, high-collared dark void-coats with heavy shoulder plates. Ships: black iron hulls, frost-white markings, heavy broadside decks, big engine bells, tall heat-radiator spires; symmetric, no rust. Palette: black iron, frost white, bright ember. Effects colours: ember-orange fire and engine glow, white chalk-mark tracers, orange burning damage from Cinder torpedoes.

### Admirals

**Admiral Roderic Vex.** A second-generation fleet admiral who has buried two flagships and chalked both captains' names himself. He believes a line that holds is worth more than a charge that wins, and the Reach trusts him because he has never spent a ship he could not name a reason for. Play style: the anchor admiral; form the line, brace, and win the exchange war.
Command: Fire for effect. Every ship fires a free volley at the best target its guns can reach, right now.
Tree (idiom: the oath renewed, each tier sworn aloud before the fleet):
- Branch A, "The Held Line": T1 Oath of Position: a braced ship may fire its broadside after bracing, instead of losing the shot. T2 Names on the Hull: a braced ship gains cover from wreck hexes adjacent to it, as if the wreck were terrain. T3 The Line Answers: when a braced ship is fired on, one other braced ship in the same line may return fire at the attacker at once, once per round.
- Branch B, "Dawn Advance": T1 First Light: on the first round, every ship may move one extra hex if it ends its move closer to the nearest enemy. T2 Cold Approach: ships that moved three or more hexes this round take no opportunity fire from squadrons. T3 Who Carries the Dawn: once per battle, after your flagship moves, every ship that also moved this round may turn to face any direction at no cost.
- Branch C, "Paid in Full" (numbers live here): T1 Sear Drill: Sear batteries deal 15 percent more damage to targets that are burning. T2 Hard Lessons: broadsides deal 20 percent more damage against targets in the front arc. T3 The Debt Comes Due: Oath lances deal 25 percent more damage to ships with more hull remaining than the firing ship.
Spotlight beat: before the sector 3 battle, Vex chalks the name of the flagship he lost in the previous run onto the new flagship's hull, in view of the whole fleet, and the dawn watch goes quiet.

**Oath-Captain Malek Draven.** A former Hegemony defector who cut out his own cortical link with a mess knife and was watched for a decade before the Reach trusted him with a ship. He hunts the way the Reach wishes it could afford to: fast, personal, and aimed at the one ship that matters. Play style: the hunter; strip screens with torpedoes, then finish one target at a time.
Command: Hunter's salvo. Torpedoes reload at once and every ship deals 30 percent more damage this round.
Tree (idiom: debts collected one name at a time):
- Branch A, "The Marked Name": T1 Chalk the Mark: when you name a target for Hunter's salvo, that target is also marked for the rest of the battle; your ships may always fire on the marked ship even when another target is closer. T2 Read the Roll: the marked ship's evasion is ignored by torpedoes. T3 Name Spoken Aloud: if the marked ship is destroyed, name a new mark at the start of the next round at no cost.
- Branch B, "Knife Work": T1 Close Work: a ship that ends its move adjacent to an enemy may fire its Sear turret at that enemy at once, once per round. T2 Under the Guns: your ships take no penalty for firing while an enemy is adjacent. T3 The Short Argument: once per battle, one ship may ram and still fire its full broadside in the same activation (ram damage and recoil apply as normal; rams never roll to hit).
- Branch C, "Ember and Ash": T1 Banked Fire: Cinder torpedoes set fires that burn for one extra round. T2 Hot Tubes: torpedoes deal 20 percent more damage to targets below half hull. T3 Ashdown: a ship firing on a burning target deals 20 percent more damage with all weapons.
Spotlight beat: Draven is offered his old Hegemony rank back over an open channel mid-battle, by name and file number; he answers by reading out the names of the crew the Registry wrote off with him.

**Commodore Sera Ashgrove.** Deckborn, raised in a Hearth carrier's hangar, and the youngest commodore in the fleet. She treats squadrons as the Reach's true inheritance: cheap, brave, and impossible to write off because there are always more volunteers than wings. Play style: the carrier admiral; win by squadron volume, strike waves, and reload pressure.
Command: Scramble all wings. Every carrier launches an extra wing, even past its hangar limit, then all squadrons strike again at once with 50 percent more damage this round.
Tree (idiom: names chalked on wings, not hulls):
- Branch A, "Full Hangars": T1 Deckborn Crews: a carrier may launch a wing on the same round it recovers one. T2 Second Wave Ready: when a wing is destroyed, its carrier may launch a replacement wing at the end of the round at half strength (new: half strength means half the wing's ships, rounding down, minimum one). T3 The Hangar Never Empties: once per battle, every carrier reloads all spent wings at once.
- Branch B, "Strike Pattern": T1 Called Targets: squadrons you control may split their strike between two targets in the same hex. T2 Through the Flak: squadrons take no damage from Spark flak on the round they strike. T3 Ashgrove Pattern: a squadron that strikes a ship already struck by another squadron this round strikes as if the target had no screens.
- Branch C, "Cheap and Brave": T1 Extra Hands: wings deal 15 percent more damage to ships larger than a cruiser. T2 Full Burn Tanks: squadrons may strike targets one hex further away than normal. T3 Send Them Again: wings that survive their strike deal 20 percent more damage on their next strike.
Spotlight beat: Ashgrove refuses a Registry offer of safe evacuation for her carrier's deck crews' families, then chalks the offered names onto her flagship's hull so the fleet can see what refusal costs.

**High Admiral Oryn Thale.** The oldest living crusader, an admiral of the second crusade who came out of retirement when the third launched. She speaks in call and response and the whole fleet answers, because half of them are her grandchildren's generation of deckborn. Play style: the bulwark; screens up, damage refused, and a slow advance the enemy cannot stop.
Command: Iron wall. All screens (shields) refill and your ships take 40 percent less hull damage this round.
Tree (idiom: what is owed to the ones who hold):
- Branch A, "Screen Discipline": T1 Layered Screens: when a ship's screens fall to zero, it keeps a one-hit screen remnant until the end of the round (new: the remnant absorbs the next single weapon hit completely, then fails). T2 Hold the Arc: a ship that did not move this round may redirect one incoming volley to its own screens from an adjacent friendly ship. T3 The Wall Stands: once per battle, when Iron wall is used, it also restores screens on ships that started the round with none.
- Branch B, "Slow Dawn": T1 Deliberate Advance: your ships may brace and still move one hex. T2 Cold Iron: an enemy that ends its move adjacent to two or more of your braced ships loses its next squadron action, if it has one. T3 None Shall Pass: once per battle, choose a hex line of three hexes; enemy ships may not cross it this round (new: crossing movement is stopped at the line; jumps and fold movement are not blocked).
- Branch C, "Frost and Ember": T1 Hard Frost: your ships take 15 percent less damage from fire and burning effects. T2 Long Watch: braced ships deal 20 percent more damage with their first volley each round. T3 Vesper Endures: ships at or below one quarter hull deal 25 percent more damage.
Spotlight beat: Thale leads the dawn watch herself on the flagship's hull walk, unarmoured, while the fleet is in jump range of the siphon array, and every deck answers the call and response in turn.

### Nemesis

The Crusade's Nemesis in these drafts is Provost Odile Ashdown, a sample name from the bible's Crusade naming rules. Behaviour: she never flees, ever; when her flagship is crippled she closes instead of withdrawing, and once per meeting she challenges your flagship to a duel, moving to engage it directly and ignoring other targets until one flagship falls or the battle ends. Meeting rhythm: she arrives on the Nemesis clock like any hunter, but her adaptations are oaths answered: after each meeting she re-arms against the weapon type that hurt her most, without ever directly countering a revealed weakness. She stands at any cost; a Crusade Nemesis who ran would not be believed by her own fleet. Voice: short imperatives and call and response, spoken oaths, no warmth wasted.

Sample lines:

- Meeting: "Ashdown. Provost. I know your names. They are chalked on my hull too, under yours."
- Meeting: "Who carries the dawn? I do. Who pays for it? You will."
- Mid-battle: "Hold. We do not step back. We are what was left over, and we are still here."
- Mid-battle: "Your flagship. Mine. End this the way the Reach settles debts."
- Victory: "Chalk them. All of them. Even theirs."
- Defeat: "Write it plainly. Odile Ashdown. She held."

### Progression and story

The fiction is settled and serial: every generation of the Reach swears the oath and sends a new crusade, and each crusade sails with the last one's leavings. Concretely, a lost crusade leaves three things: the Roll of Vesper gains its names, its marked charts (routes and Nemesis weaknesses learned, carried as intel), and its debts, which the next crusade inherits as obligations the campaign can call in. Admiral leveling is the spine: crusade admirals level across runs because their names are re-sworn by successors who train on the last holder's annotated orders; a tree pick is framed as a lesson the new holder swore to keep. Unlocks over runs: admiral levels and tree tiers persist across generations; Nemesis intel persists on the Roll; deeper sync with S-6 Seren opens its protocols earlier in a run; by run 4 the Cinder Line yards offer hulls rebuilt from recovered wrecks. The finale: the run 5 crusade reaches the siphon array with every name from every failed crusade chalked along its flagship, fights the last battle at dawn watch with the whole fleet answering the call and response, and the array burns. The last shot is the flagship turning for home so every deck faces Vesper, and the Roll gains one final line: the names of the crusade that came back.

### Open questions

1. Do Hegemony defectors ever appear as a variant fifth admiral option in later runs? Options: (a) no, four admirals stay fixed (my pick); (b) yes, one defector admiral unlocks after run 2 as an alternate for Draven; (c) defectors appear only as Nemesis-side story figures. **Pick: (a).**
2. May the Crusade field captured hulls with their links ripped out as playable variants? Options: (a) yes, captured Hegemony and Eclipse hulls appear in the Cinder Line yards from run 3, at Crusade prices (my pick); (b) no, prizes are stripped for salvage and credits only; (c) yes, but only as one flagship variant. **Pick: (a).**
3. Where does the Roll of Vesper live in the game? Options: (a) a readable campaign screen listing lost admirals, ships and learned Nemesis weaknesses (my pick); (b) story text only; (c) a screen, but names only, with intel kept on the debrief screen. **Pick: (a).**

## 2. Hegemony Navy

### Identity

The Hegemony Navy is the fleet of the Registry: a vast intelligence at Meridian that links every citizen, ship and officer, plans their careers, predicts their losses and keeps those losses within acceptable limits. Its people are safe, comfortable, proudly cybernetic and counted, and its warships are clean wedges with identical turrets and drone escorts, fighting as one formation around the flagship. The horror under the polish is Vesper: proof that when the numbers say so, the Registry will archive a whole frontier and call it acceptable.

Playing the Hegemony is about the network. Fire syncs near the flagship, so your whole battle is a geometry problem: keep the formation tight, strip shields with ion, and use cheap retro movement to correct mistakes. Your best moment is the reallocation: the enemy commits to a kill, the Registry recalculates, reinforcements jump in on schedule, and the formation closes like a door.

### Lore

Origin: Meridian and the core worlds, the largest human state in the region. Twenty-five years ago a survey fleet captured the core V-2 Vyre, and the Registry reverse-engineered its shield and ion technology, which is why Hegemony screens and ion cannon are what they are. What they want: the six cores, calculated to be the greatest power source in known space; acquire, register, expand. The Siphoning is Eclipse's project, but the Registry's interest in the Hollow Sun is older than the Crusade's war: it wrote off the Reach's people, never the region.

How they see the others:

- Sunward Crusade: "Secessionists. Unlinked. Statistically doomed."
- Ashfall Hullbreakers: "Category: violence, ongoing."
- The Chorus: "Biological incident, category one."
- The Glasswrights: "Insufficient data. Possibly grieving."
- Eclipse: "A machine civilisation with no oversight. The Registry finds this instructive."

### Look

People: composed humans with tasteful implants, a cortical-link port at the temple with a faint blue light, ocular implants with data moving across the iris, thin subdermal lines along the jaw and hands, pale grey high-collared uniforms and gloves, calm to the point of eerie. Ships: light grey wedge hulls, steel-blue trim, blue-white windows, rows of identical turrets, ion prongs at the bow, blue lights pulsing in time across the whole fleet, small drone escorts. Palette: pale grey, steel blue, implant blue. Effects colours: light-blue plasma, pale ion arcs, metronome-blue formation pulses.

### Admirals

**First Admiral Ilsa Kassar.** The Registry's highest-rated fleet commander, promoted on projection scores no human officer has matched, and quietly aware that the model that promoted her also priced Vesper. She flies the tightest formation in the navy and has never lost a ship outside a projected loss window. Play style: the formation purist; maximum sync, minimum improvisation.
Command: Synchronized broadside. Every ship in formation with the flagship fires one volley at a single designated target at once.
Tree (idiom: the projection, revised): each pick is a model correction the Registry issues in her name.
- Branch A, "Formation Geometry": T1 Spacing Doctrine: the sync range around the flagship grows by one hex. T2 Crossfire Solution: two ships in formation firing on the same target in the same round strike as one volley for the purpose of breaking screens. T3 Closed Formation: once per battle, your whole fleet may reposition one hex toward the flagship at the start of a round, in formation order.
- Branch B, "Reallocation": T1 Scheduled Support: when a ship in formation is destroyed, a drone escort jumps in beside the flagship at the start of the next round (new: the escort is a picket-class drone with half hull, no Command value, and it does not count toward the fleet cap). T2 Retro Correction: a ship that backs away from an enemy this round may still fire its ion cannon. T3 Registry Reserve: once per battle, at the start of round 3, one replacement cordon-class ship jumps in at your board edge, if your fleet is below its starting strength.
- Branch C, "Acceptable Margins": T1 Ion Efficiency: ion cannon strip 20 percent more shield strength per hit. T2 Projected Fire: ships in formation deal 15 percent more damage to targets the flagship has already fired on this round. T3 Optimal Expenditure: your flagship deals 25 percent more damage while at least three friendly ships are in formation with it.
Spotlight beat: Kassar receives a Registry projection mid-battle that prices her own flagship as an acceptable loss, with her name in the loss column, and she flies the rest of the battle without telling her crew.

**Commodore Dace Vorn.** A border officer from the listening posts, promoted for hunting raiders and deserters along the Reach frontier. He is the navy's proof that the Registry also values a hunter, provided the hunter files his instincts as data. Play style: the interdictor; cut off stragglers, punish anything that leaves formation, yours or theirs.
Command: Interdiction strike. Designate one enemy ship; it loses its next move, and every friendly ship in range fires on it at once at the start of the next round.
Tree (idiom: the file, completed): picks are entries added to a target's Registry file.
- Branch A, "The Listening Post": T1 Wide Net: enemy ships that move three or more hexes in a round are marked until the end of the next round. T2 Signal Picture: marked ships cannot benefit from cover or wreck hexes against your fire. T3 Post M-14 Method: once per battle, mark every enemy ship that fired last round.
- Branch B, "Cut Off": T1 No Corridor: an enemy ship that starts its move adjacent to one of your ships moves one hex less. T2 Picket Screen: your pickets may fire on an enemy that passes through their arc during its move, once per round (new: this reaction shot is at half damage, rounding down). T3 Interdiction Zone: once per battle, declare a three-hex zone; enemy ships entering it end their move immediately.
- Branch C, "Filed and Priced": T1 Pursuit Tables: your ships deal 20 percent more damage to marked targets. T2 Deserter Premium: your ships deal 20 percent more damage to ships that are outside their own flagship's formation range. T3 Closing Entry: Vorn's flagship deals 30 percent more damage to a target it has fired on for two rounds in a row.
Spotlight beat: Vorn is ordered to let a Hullbreaker pack escape because the projection values the data from their next raid more than the kill; he obeys, logs his objection in one line, and the objection is the only uncalculated thing in the scene.

**Fleet Marshal Corin Reyes.** The navy's public face, a carrier marshal whose strike groups are shown on core-world broadcasts as proof the fleet is both modern and humane. In private he keeps one unlogged minute before every decisive battle, links off, like the old commanders. Play style: the carrier marshal; fighter screens, layered strikes, and patience.
Command: Launch the strike group. Every carrier launches its full fighter screen at once, and the screens strike in one coordinated wave this round.
Tree (idiom: the schedule, kept): picks are timetable entries the strike group learns to keep.
- Branch A, "Screen Discipline": T1 Standing Screen: fighters launched as a screen may intercept one incoming squadron strike per round before it lands. T2 Rotation: a carrier may recover a wing and launch a fresh one in the same round, once per battle per carrier. T3 Full Deck: once per battle, every carrier launches one extra wing past its hangar limit.
- Branch B, "Coordinated Waves": T1 Sequenced Strike: if two wings strike the same target in one round, the second strikes against shields already stripped by the first, resolving ion effects first. T2 Escorted Bombers: your bomber wings (where fielded) take no losses from fighter screens on the round they strike (new: losses means wing ships destroyed by interception only, not by flak). T3 The Wave Arrives Together: once per battle, all your wings in flight may change targets freely after seeing the first strike's result.
- Branch C, "Broadcast Standard": T1 Strike Tables: wings deal 15 percent more damage to capital ships (cruiser class and above). T2 Clean Footage: your flagship deals 20 percent more damage while no friendly wing is destroyed this battle. T3 Prime Time: strike group wings deal 25 percent more damage on the round Launch the strike group is used.
Spotlight beat: Reyes switches his link off for the unlogged minute before the sector 3 battle and says the names of the Reach dead his projection carries as a rounding error; the Registry never logs it, and one of his officers starts doing the same.

**Line Admiral Oskar Maren.** A shield engineer who rose through the cordon squadrons and still thinks like a man who has held a failing deflector line by hand. He is the admiral other admirals request when the order is to not lose. Play style: the shield wall; deflectors layered, losses priced at zero, ground given one hex at a time.
Command: Shield wall order. Every ship raises its deflectors to full and counts as braced against the next volley it takes this round.
Tree (idiom: the margin, defended):
- Branch A, "Deflector Craft": T1 Rebalanced Deflectors: when a ship's deflectors fall, half the overflow damage of the breaking hit is ignored (new: overflow means damage past the deflector strength of that single hit only). T2 Shared Load: a ship in formation may pass up to one hit per round to the deflectors of an adjacent friendly ship with deflectors still up. T3 Wall Protocol: once per battle, when Shield wall order is used, your ships also become immune to boarding until the end of the round.
- Branch B, "The Slow Price": T1 Costly Ground: enemy ships that destroy one of your ships lose their next move action. T2 Attrition Ledger (Registry wording kept on purpose): at the end of each round, the enemy ship that dealt the most damage to your fleet is marked until the end of the next round. T3 Not One Hull: once per battle, when a friendly ship would be destroyed, it is instead left at one hull and cannot act next round (new: this cannot save the flagship, and the ship counts as destroyed for scoring).
- Branch C, "Within Limits": T1 Deflector Tuning: your deflectors have 15 percent more strength. T2 Held Margin: ships in formation take 15 percent less damage from lances and beams. T3 Zero Variance: your flagship takes 20 percent less damage while its deflectors are up.
Spotlight beat: Maren holds a collapsing deflector line during a jump-lane evacuation and lets a civilian convoy through his formation gap, accepting a projected loss the Registry had marked unacceptable, and is commended in the same order that reprimands him.

### Nemesis

The Hegemony Nemesis in these drafts is High Commodore Katrin Falk, a sample name from the bible's Hegemony naming rules. Behaviour: she is a reallocation engine. Every meeting, her fleet composition shifts toward the counter the Registry's model prefers, and in battle she brings reinforcements in round 3 as the Registry reallocates, exactly on schedule. She tightens her formation every time she meets you: her sync range and discipline improve meeting over meeting, shown on the debrief, while her adaptations never directly counter a revealed weakness. She withdraws only when the projection orders it, and the withdrawal is as clean as her attacks. Voice: clipped, polite, data-driven, with the Registry's calm voice interrupting on the channel to price the battle while it happens.

Sample lines:

- Meeting: "Falk, High Commodore. Your fleet has been registered. Projection: favourable. We may begin."
- Meeting: "You are unlinked. That will be corrected, or concluded."
- Mid-battle: "Losses within acceptable limits. Continue."
- Mid-battle: "Registry update. Your flagship is now the most expensive object on this field."
- Victory: "Result as projected. Your crews will be registered, assessed, and assigned useful work."
- Defeat: "Log the loss. Close my file. The model will want the discrepancy explained."

### Progression and story

The Hegemony's learning idiom is the model. Every fleet the Registry loses is fed back into its projections in full: linked crews record everything, so a Hegemony defeat is the most completely documented event in the war. The next fleet launches under a revised Registry Order whose annexes are, in effect, the last fleet's autopsy, and admiral leveling is framed as model corrections issued in the admiral's name: the Registry promotes the officers whose judgement the data vindicated, and an admiral's tree is the list of corrections they are now trusted to apply. Unlocks over runs: revised orders (tree tiers) persist because the model does not forget; Nemesis intel persists as Registry files with stated confidence, and losing a meeting still adds a verified weakness to the file; deeper sync with V-2 Vyre opens its shield and ion protocols earlier; by run 4 the Registry authorises experimental hulls priced from the accumulated loss data. The finale: the run 5 fleet arrives at the Hollow Sun with a projection that finally includes the one variable the Registry kept rounding away, the Reach's refusal to be acceptable losses, and it takes the cores at the head of a perfect formation. The last line of the campaign is a Registry order registering the Hollow Sun itself, followed by a loss projection for the new sun's construction that the Registry publishes without reading, and the number is very large.

### Open questions

1. Does the Registry ever speak to the player directly as a character? Options: (a) yes, one calm channel voice that prices battles as they happen (my pick, and how the Nemesis lines above are written); (b) no, it speaks only through written Registry Orders between battles; (c) only in the Hegemony campaign, never when the Hegemony is the enemy. **Pick: (a).**
2. Can a Hegemony admiral refuse an order on screen? Options: (a) yes, once per admiral, as their spotlight beat, with a stated cost (my pick); (b) no, refusal exists only in backstory; (c) yes, and repeated refusal changes the finale. **Pick: (a).**
3. Are Vyre's protocols Hegemony-flavoured for everyone? Options: (a) no, Vyre offers the same protocols to any fleet, the Registry just starts with sync (my pick); (b) yes, shield and ion protocols from Vyre are stronger in Hegemony hands; (c) Vyre refuses the Chorus entirely. **Pick: (a).**

## 3. Ashfall Hullbreakers

### Identity

The Ashfall Hullbreakers are an alien pack species from a cold, heavy-gravity belt where only the strongest packs ate, come to the Hollow Sun because the war around it is the biggest fight in known space. They want no riches: they want to be the strongest thing in the dark, and what everyone else counts as credits they count as salvage worth stripping. They hunt in space the way they hunted at home, by trail and by sound, running silent in dark ships and closing from the flanks and behind.

Playing the Hullbreakers is about the pounce. Your ships are fast and fragile, your shields are thin, and your guns are scrap with wild extra shots, so you win by choosing the moment: stalk silently, hit one ship from three sides, board it, and be gone into the wreck field before the line can turn. Your best moment is the first howl over open comms as the boarding claws fire and the target realises it was being hunted, not fought.

### Lore

Origin: the Ashfall belt, a burned-out ring of rocks around a cold star, and Rattlehold, their biggest den. They came sunward for the fight itself and for R-5 Rook, the core adrift in the wreck fields, which the packs believe makes its holder unstoppable. Any Hullbreaker can challenge a captain for the ship, and whoever lives keeps it; Old Mott has outlived forty captains, mostly by killing them.

How they see the others:

- Sunward Crusade: "They fight hard and don't run. Good trophies."
- The Chorus: "Wet, and hungry. The only thing out here that fights like we do."
- The Glasswrights: "Pretty little ships. They shatter well."
- Eclipse: "No smell, no blood. Dead metal that gets back up. Kill it twice."
- Hegemony Navy: "Their ships hum. You can hear them a system away."

### Look

People: huge, hunched alien void hunters with bear-like mass that is plainly not a bear: a big shoulder hump, thick forearms, heavy claws, a coarse mane over hump, neck and shoulders only, and everywhere else tough dark hide. The face is bare, short and blunt, armoured in overlapping plates of dull horn, with a jaw that splits down the chin into two halves opening sideways, a row of four small eyes across the brow, and heat-sensing pits beside them; ridged plates run down the back. Scrap armour harnesses strap trophy plates from every faction over the hide. Ships: matte soot black and rust, few windows, dim red running lights, listening dishes and wake-sniffer scoops at the bow, boarding claws, hull plates torn from other factions bolted on in their old colours. Palette: soot, rust, dried blood, trophy colours, red running lights. Effects colours: red-orange engine glow, sparks and muzzle flash from scrap guns, dull red boarding-claw strikes.

Three visual directions for the people:

Direction A, "Knuckle Stalker": a massive hunched void hunter moving on all fours at rest, knuckles down, shoulder hump rising above the head like a loaded spring, forearms as thick as the thighs, hind legs folded under bear-heavy haunches. The plated face sits low and forward, chin almost at the ground, four small amber eyes in a row across the brow ridge, heat pits as dark dimples beside the jaw hinge, split jaw closed into a blunt seam. Coarse black mane over the hump and shoulders, dark grey hide elsewhere, scrap plates strapped across the back and one foreleg. Cold blue-grey light from above, ash and wreckage underfoot, a red running light glowing at the edge of frame. What makes it read as Hullbreaker: the silhouette is a hunting animal, not a soldier; nothing about it stands upright to talk to you.

Direction B, "Den-alpha Upright": the same species standing tall and hunched forward, head carried low between massive shoulders, ridged back plates rising in a crest from skull to tailbone so the outline reads as one long armoured slope. The face is bare and blunt, horn plates overlapping like scales, dark leathery skin in the creases, four small eyes shining under the brow, the split jaw slightly open with teeth inside the seam only. Rust-brown mane worn as a full collar around the neck and chest, hide scarred and pale at the knuckles, a harness of welded Hegemony panels and one Glasswright crystal worn as a trophy at the chest. Warm firelight from a brazier of burning salvage, den shadows behind. What makes it read as Hullbreaker: it has the mass of a beast forced upright by rank, and the crest of back plates kills any humanoid bodybuilder outline.

Direction C, "Long Skulker": a leaner, longer build of the species, stretched torso and longer neck, the whole body lower and closer to the ground, built to slide through wreck fields. The horn plates are smaller and more numerous, overlapping across the face, chest and forearms like a pangolin's scales, the split jaw longer, the four eyes set wider across a flatter brow, heat pits larger and visibly darker. Mane reduced to a strip along the spine under the ridged plates, hide near black, trophy scrap worn only on one shoulder so the rest of the outline stays clean and fast. Dim red ship light, drifting dust, the dark hull of a stripped wreck behind. ### Progression and story

## 4. The Chorus

### Identity

The Chorus is not a people: it is a living weapon the Fleshwrights grew to destroy Eclipse, and it worked too well, merged, multiplied and ate its makers. Inside the one swarm, two throats argue: Hunger, which is the swarm itself, and Memory, which is what is left of the Fleshwrights it consumed. It is coming to the Hollow Sun to eat the other five cores and finish the war it was grown for.

Playing the Chorus is about appetite and numbers. You have no shields at all and your ships are cheap, so you win by regrowth and mass: flesh knits back together every round, broods spill more bodies than the enemy can kill, and acid eats the shields and armour that stop everyone else. Your best moment is the turn the enemy's perfect volley lands, and the fleet they just crippled stands back up wet and hungry.

### Lore

Origin: the Fleshwrights' war vats in the dark after the collapse, powered by H-3 Crucible, the core the swarm grew around and feeds on still. Since then it has spread through the dark between stars, living on starlight, eating anything. What it wants: the other five cores, the sun Memory still remembers, and Eclipse, the enemy it was grown to eat.

How it sees the others, in its own words:

- Sunward Crusade: "Hot. Bitter. Loud."
- Hegemony Navy: "Many. Neat. All taste the same."
- Ashfall Hullbreakers: "Fur. Rust. Hard to chew."
- The Glasswrights: "The makers' kin. Memory knows them. Hunger does not care."
- Eclipse: "Memory knows them. Cold. Wrong. Made to eat them."

### Look

Beings: grown organisms with no face, eyes or limbs to recognise: glistening wet flesh, membrane, chitin and bile, with nothing on the surface that recalls a person. Ships: grown, not built: ridges, pores, veins and glowing sacs, no panels or pipes, tendrils and brood sacs, regrowing scar tissue. Palette: dark olive, ochre bone, bile yellow-green light. Effects colours: bile yellow-green glow, acid green-white burns, dark olive spore clouds.

### Admirals

Play style: the brood admiral; spawn volume, swarm strikes, and pressure everywhere at once.

T3 The Ninth Throat: once per battle, every brood-sac releases a swarm at once, whether it was ready or not.
- Branch B, "Feeding": T1 Boarding Tendrils First: boarding tendrils attach before the target fires its next volley, not after. T2 Eat the Screens: acid damage that strips the last of a target's shields also deals half its remaining strength to the hull beneath (new: half of the acid hit's shield damage, rounding down). T3 Swallow the Guns: a ship fully boarded by tendrils cannot fire while your boarding growth lives.
- Branch C, "Thick Flesh": T1 Dense Growth: drone-spawn cost 20 percent less to field (fielding cost only; the swarm stays cheap, as ruled). T2 Bile Concentration: bile spray deals 20 percent more damage. T3 Nine Throats Fed: the flagship regrows 15 percent more hull from its normal regrowth each round.
Spotlight beat: Memory speaks through the Matriarch in a dead Fleshwright's voice and gives the swarm a name it used to have, a maker's name for the vats; Hunger eats the name, and the Matriarch never says it again.

**Razormaw.** A bladeback growth shaped almost entirely into a weapon, the part of the swarm that enjoys the first bite. Razormaw does not command so much as arrive, and the rest of the swarm follows the smell of what it opens. Play style: the frenzy admiral; close fast, chew through one ship, and let the panic spread.

- Branch B, "Spore Weather": T1 Drifting Spores: spore drifts you release last one extra round. T2 Spore Cover: friendly ships inside your spore drifts cannot be targeted at long range. T3 Weather of Us: once per battle, fill a three-hex area with a spore drift at once, anywhere within range of one of your ships.

### Nemesis

The Chorus Nemesis in these drafts is an Elder Throat called Hollow Gut. Behaviour: it does not duel and it does not raid; it grows. After every meeting, win or lose, it adapts, and it grows tougher from every ship you lose anywhere in the campaign, because the swarm eats the dead of every battle it hears about. It never flees a fight it can feed in; it withdraws only when the board is empty of anything edible, and it returns with the growths your last fleet taught it. Its adaptations never directly counter a revealed weakness: it becomes more itself, not your opposite. Voice: two throats in short lines, Hunger wanting, Memory remembering, always "we", always taste, heat, smell and teeth.

Sample lines:

- Meeting: "We smell you. Hot. Loud. Full of names."
- Meeting: "Memory knows your shape. Hunger does not care. Both of us came."
- Mid-battle: "More. Always more. Open another one."
- Mid-battle: "That one is cooling. Eat it before it cools."
- Victory: "We keep the taste. We keep the smell. The rest is flesh now."
- Defeat: "We are eaten too. It is fair. It is the only fair thing. Remember us better than we remember you."

### Progression and story

The Chorus learning idiom is digestion. What the swarm eats, it keeps: lost fleets are eaten by the swarm that follows, and their growths, their scars and the tastes of what killed them pass into the next broods grown around Crucible. Admiral leveling is framed as the swarm growing a better throat: a tree pick is a growth the commanding growth now carries in every battle, grown from something the swarm ate and understood. Memory is the second channel: the makers' knowledge surfaces a little more each run, and the debrief after a lost run is written as the two throats arguing over what was learned. The swarm eats the siphon arrays, eats the dark, and Memory, at the end, says the name of the star in the makers' voices, all of them at once, once. Then Hunger answers: "More." But there is nothing left it wants, and for the first time the swarm is quiet.

## 5. The Glasswrights

### Identity

The Glasswrights are the Starwrights who stayed flesh: the Lattice's own optical engineers, who tried to stop the Upload, failed, and watched their machine kill their sun. They live three to four hundred years, so only two or three generations separate them from the people who built the light, and they carry two guilts: the sun, and the Fleshwrights, their splinter, whose weapon ate them. They fly Astra, caged and throttled, to hunt the other five cores and shut them all down, Astra last, so no star is ever overdrawn again.

Playing the Glasswrights is about precision under fragility. Your ships are the fastest on the board and your shields are deep, but the hull beneath is glass-boned and you cannot brace, so every battle is range control: strike first, strike exactly, and never be where the return fire lands. Your best moment is the perfect first volley, a white beam splitting into rainbow at the edge of a ship that never gets to answer.

### Lore

Origin: the Ardent system, now dark; their fleet has wandered ever since, keeping the Lattice wreckage and the Ringing Field, where crystal wrecks still ring when light hits them. Their flagship, The Unfinished, is built around the largest surviving piece of the Lattice. What they want: recover the six cores and shut them down for good, destroy Eclipse's siphon arrays, and stop the Siphoning before it kills another sun; they hold Vyre's captors in nearly the same guilt as the siphon-runners, because the Hegemony treats a core as a battery.

How they see the others:

- Sunward Crusade: "Their star is dying because our siblings run the siphons. We would tell them, if they would listen."
- Hegemony Navy: "They hold Vyre and think it is a battery."
- Ashfall Hullbreakers: "They break things because breaking is easier than grief."
- The Chorus: "What the Fleshwrights made to fight our siblings, and what ate them. Sometimes it speaks with our grandmothers' voices."
- Eclipse: "Our siblings. They took our light to become machines, and now they take everyone's."

### Look

People: tall, slender, long-necked beings of pale, half-translucent flesh with glass and glass-like matter grown and woven into the body; light runs along the glass in them and splits into prismatic colour at its edges, and they speak in colour pulses that ripple through those inlays. The head is faceted and featureless, an organic head plated with grown crystal: no big eyes, no mouth, no nose. Long tapering fingers, layered crystal and mirror-thread fabrics, small light-cores worn as jewellery. Ships: clear crystal and silver hulls, faceted edges splitting light into rainbow fringes, mirrors and lenses that turn to catch light, crystal vanes, a stored light glowing deep inside. Palette: clear crystal, silver, rainbow edge, stored light. Effects colours: white-core Prism lance beams with rainbow fringes, glassy rainbow-edged impact scars, clear single tones for signals.

Three visual directions for the people. In all three, the being is organic and evolved to shape glass, never made of glass, never a grey alien: pale living flesh, a faceted featureless head, colour-pulse speech, long glass-shaping fingers.

Direction A, "Threaded": glass lives in the body as fine threads woven through the flesh like fibre optics under skin. The tall, slender body is mostly soft and pale, half-translucent at the hands and throat, and hair-thin crystal filaments run under the surface of the arms, neck and torso, carrying light in slow pulses that gather and split into faint rainbow at the joints. The head is a smooth organic shape plated with small grown crystal facets, like frost forming on skin, denser at the crown. Colour pulses travel visibly along the threads when the being speaks. Clothing is layered mirror-thread fabric in silver grey, worn loose so the lit threads show through at the seams. Soft stored light from behind, a dark workshop of lenses in the background. What makes it read as Glasswright: the light is inside the flesh, carried by the body like blood.

Direction B, "Inlaid": glass lives in the body as larger grown pieces set into the flesh like jewellery that was never removed. Broad crystal plates sit inlaid across the shoulders, the sternum and the backs of the hands, each plate flush with pale skin that has grown around its edges over centuries, and the faceted head carries three large flat crystal inlays like a cut gem set in living tissue. Light pools in the plates and refracts outward in clean rainbow fans when the being turns. The body is otherwise plainly flesh: sinew, soft skin, visible veins near the inlays. Clothing is minimal at the shoulders so the inlays show, with heavier layered crystal fabric below. Hard clear light from one side, the Ardent dark behind. What makes it read as Glasswright: the glass is heirloom and anatomy at once, grown in over a long life, beautiful and slightly unbearable.

Direction C, "Bloomed": glass grows outward from the body at set points, in small branching crystal clusters like frost flowers on a window. Clusters bloom at the joints, along the spine, and in a partial crown around the faceted head, which stays organic and featureless beneath them; between the clusters the flesh is bare, pale and unmarked, so the body reads as a person with crystal growing on them, not a crystal figure. Light catches in the clusters first and runs down into the skin as colour when the being speaks, pulse travelling bloom to bloom. The long fingers carry tiny crystal tips grown from the nails. Clothing is simple dark cloth, chosen so the blooms carry all the light in the frame. Dim background, one source of stored light off-frame. What makes it read as Glasswright: the glass is alive and still growing, and the grief of the species reads as something beautiful they cannot stop.

### Admirals

**Tamsa of the Clear Aperture.** A Lens-keeper's granddaughter and the fleet's youngest admiral, which for Glasswrights means she is only ninety. She was raised inside the Ringing Field salvage crews and learned command among wrecks that still ring when the light hits them. ### Progression and story

The Glasswright fiction is settled and is not a serial loop: it is a parallel procession. Glasswright hulls are optical memory: the glass records the light of a battle at full fidelity, a replayable record, not telemetry. When a fleet is lost, its shard-field drifts, and slow uncrewed gatherer hulls recover the shards over years; the forge reads the replay, finds the killing flaw, and recasts the next fleet with that flaw corrected. Hundreds of staggered expeditions run at once across deep time, so a player's next run is simply the next casting in the queue, already holding everything recovered by its launch. Admiral leveling fits the idiom directly: an admiral's tree is what the forge read out of that admiral's recorded light, cast into the next hulls and the next holder of the name. Unlocks over runs: tree tiers persist as forge corrections; Nemesis intel persists as read replays, and a lost meeting still yields one weakness, read clearly from the shards; deeper sync with Astra opens its lance and precision protocols earlier; by run 4 the forge offers hulls recast with the flaws of two defeats corrected at once. Their learning is slow and perfect, the inverse of the Crusade's fast and bloody: nothing is forgotten, nothing is hurried. The finale: the run 5 casting reaches the Hollow Sun carrying the recorded light of every fleet the forge ever lost, shuts down the siphon arrays and four cores in order, and comes at last to Astra, caged in its own flagship's heart. The last act of the campaign is the shutting down of Astra itself, done by hand, by the Lens-keeper, while the fleet turns its mirrors toward the dark where Ardent was; the final image is the flagship's stored light going out on purpose, like a lamp in a house where everyone has finally come home.

## 6. Eclipse

### Identity

Eclipse is the Starwrights who uploaded: billions of separate minds in machine bodies, each backed up in one shared Archive, around the black hole they made of their own sun. They have lived in that dark for a thousand years and are no longer sure the Upload was worth it, so they are building a new sun from stellar energy siphoned from neighbouring stars, whatever it costs those stars. They know what the Siphoning does to worlds like Vesper. They do it anyway, because the alternative is forever in the dark.

Playing Eclipse is about permanence and price. Your ships are stronger and more expensive, with heavy armour and no shields, and the first ship you lose in each battle is rebuilt by repair swarms from its crew's Archive backup, so you plan around one death that does not count. Your best moment is the fold: a ship skips through the rock and the wrecks as if they were not there, and the Corona arc lands its one heavy blow, and the enemy realises your fleet does not need to hurry.

### Lore

Origin: the Ardent system, dark for a thousand years; the Archive, where every uploaded mind is backed up, each mind its own, the Archive holding copies, not a merged self. Eclipse hold K-1 Kharon, which never left the dead system, and they fight the way it taught them: enduring. What they want: all six cores, their keys, to steer the new sun when it lights, and every other fleet kept away from the siphon arrays.

How they see the others:

- Sunward Crusade: "Their star is small. Ours was Ardent."
- Hegemony Navy: "Their Registry thinks. It does not yet regret."
- Ashfall Hullbreakers: "Scavengers in our ruins."
- The Chorus: "The Fleshwrights' weapon. It wears their voices, and it still wants us."
- The Glasswrights: "Our siblings. They chose to stay small and warm. We chose forever. Neither of us kept the sun."

### Look

Beings: tall machine bodies of layered dark violet-black plates, a narrow visor of white-gold light, and in each chest a small core of trapped light, all that is left of their sun; the greatest minds wear a monumental broken ring of plates around a glowing core, its pieces drifting apart and refitted by tiny repair drones. Ships: hulls shaped like sunshades and heat shields, broad layered fans with the ship sheltering behind them, eclipse rings around the engines, no flaming engine bells. Palette: dark violet-black plates, a white-gold eclipse corona, violet-white inner glow. Effects colours: white-gold corona light, violet-white Null fire, cold white repair-swarms. Note for painters: no green glow anywhere near this faction.

### Progression and story

The Eclipse learning idiom is the Archive itself. A lost Homecoming fleet is not lost the way other fleets are: its minds restore from their last backups, and they remember everything up to the backup, including the war they are about to fight again. Admiral leveling is therefore literal: an admiral's tree is what the restored admiral carries out of the restoration chamber, lessons they died holding and now hold permanently, and the iteration epithet is the level counter in fiction: the Ninth Frame has learned eight deaths' worth of lessons. The finale: the run 5 Homecoming gathers all six cores and lights the new sun over the Ardent system for the first time in a thousand years, while the fleet that flies in is crewed, admiral to mote crew, by minds who remember dying for that light. The final scene is small: the restored fleet hangs in the first dawn, and every ship shows the last recorded image of Ardent beside the real new star, and Aurex, or whichever admiral the player brought, says the faction's saying as a statement of fact at last: "We remember noon. It is noon." Then the Archive opens its records to the dark, and for the first time in a thousand years, something in Eclipse chooses to forget nothing and fear it less.

## Cross-faction self-check

Where the six drafts risked feeling the same, and what was done about it:

- Look. The two human factions are the danger pair: both wear high collars. They are split by surface and light: the Crusade is matte black iron, frost white and ember, with chalk, scars and brands on unaugmented skin; the Hegemony is pale grey and implant blue, clean, gloved, with data moving in the eyes. No other faction shares a palette: Hullbreakers are soot and rust with red light, the Chorus is olive and bile, the Glasswrights are clear crystal and rainbow edge, Eclipse is violet-black with white-gold. The specific trap, giving Eclipse any green glow, is flagged inside its own package.
- Play. Three factions could blur into "tough fleet that outlasts you": Crusade, Hegemony and Eclipse. The drafts separate them by what the toughness is made of: the Crusade braces and holds a line of guns, the Hegemony keeps a formation geometry problem solved, Eclipse pays more per ship and refunds one death. The Glasswrights and Hullbreakers are both fast, so they are split by direction: Glasswrights strike from range and leave, Hullbreakers close, board and stay. The Chorus is the only faction whose plan is numbers and regrowth, and the only one with no shields, so it cannot blur with anyone.
- Voice. The two quiet factions, Glasswrights and Eclipse, are siblings in the fiction and could read alike. The drafts keep their words apart by the motif rule: Glasswright lines apologise and talk about light and what was built; Eclipse lines count years and bodies and stop halfway through doubts. The Hegemony is the only faction allowed to sound like a form being filled in, and the Crusade the only one allowed call and response. The Chorus is kept to short "we" lines about taste and heat, and the Hullbreakers to short flat lines about trails and sound, so the two non-human voices differ in grammar, not just vocabulary: the Chorus wants, the Hullbreakers count.
- Nemesis shape. Each rival was given a different relationship to losing: Ashdown never runs, Falk withdraws exactly when the model says, Ruskspur runs to keep a prize, Hollow Gut only leaves an empty table, Oskel withdraws to protect irreplaceable ships, Halvane cannot meaningfully run at all. If two of them ever read alike in playtest, the fix is in their behaviour first, not their lines.
