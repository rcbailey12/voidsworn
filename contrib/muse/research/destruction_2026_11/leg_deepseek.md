1. Per game

Homeworld 3

Accumulating hull damage. Blackbird built a system called Splat Damage. It does not paste a decal on the surface. It creates dynamic impact points that alter the surface geometry and the material properties of the hull in the exact area hit, and it can transition from surface scars to open hull breaches as damage rises (Blackbird Interactive developer blog by Tim O'Connell, official Steam announcement). A second layer is a set of damage sockets placed around the hull. Each socket can spawn smoke, fire, sparks and breach effects, and the effects escalate through four named stages: light, heavy, critical, and the final stage the developers call "it was a pleasure serving with you, Captain" (same blog). At the far tactical camera, the read comes from those sockets: growing smoke plumes, fire points and sparks that are scaled to remain visible on small units. Close up, the read is the deformed hull surface, the burn marks and the persistent scars that carry from mission to mission. Splat Damage is resource intensive, so it is prioritised on capital ships, and fighters use a simpler ribbon and sprite trail instead of geometry damage (same blog).

Breaches and internal damage. Splat Damage creates the visual of a breach on the surface, and the damage sockets add breach effects, but the system does not appear to open a persistent, inspectable interior during combat. The internal modelling is reserved for the wreckage stage. This is a weak point for the specific problem in this pack: Homeworld 3 gives a superb surface and effect read, but not a hole you can look into while the ship is alive.

The kill and break-up. Blackbird built a procedural Ship Breaking tool in Houdini. It simulates how a hull collapses under an explosion, and it also models the internal rooms and mechanisms of the ship, then warps and crinkles them so they bulge outward, as if the explosion forced the ship's guts into space (Blackbird blog, and GamingTrend summary of the same blog). The result is persistent wreckage that salvagers can collect. At distance, the kill is a large staged explosion with debris that stays on the field. Close up, the wreck has modelled internal structure, which is exactly what stops it reading as an empty shell. This is the strongest reference in the pack for problem (b), but it depends on offline authored generation, so the technique does not transfer directly.

Sins of a Solar Empire 2

Accumulating hull damage. Sins 2 is systemic first. Ships have shields, armour, hull and individually targetable subsystems, and components can be destroyed to cripple engines or weapons without killing the ship (PC Gaming review, observation). The visual hull damage is weak. Players on the official forums asked for bullet holes, burn holes and sparks from damaged areas because "hardly anything changes on the ships" when the hull is hit (Sins of a Solar Empire II official site, Damage Model/Graphics/Sounds feedback). A community mod adds fire pillars, sparks, plasma leaks and embers, which confirms that the base game leaves a gap (mod.io, Improved VFX, observation). At the far camera, the read is mostly the health bar and shield effects. Close up, turrets track and subsystems can be seen, but the hull itself does not tell a strong damage story.

Breaches and internal damage. There is no persistent open breach into an interior. Ships can be crippled, and subsystems can be knocked out, but the visual language is fire, smoke and sparks on the surface, not a hole with structure behind it. This is a weak reference for problem (a).

The kill and break-up. The base game has been criticised for ships that "just pop out of existence upon death" and for the absence of a death animation (Matrix Games forum, observation). The Ruins of War update added unique persistent wreckage for every capital ship, titan and starbase, and the wreckage can be collected for resources (Stardock, Ruins of War announcement). At distance, the wreck field is the lasting read. Close up, the wreck models are unique and detailed, but the transition from alive to dead is not a staged break-up. The lesson is that persistent wreckage helps the aftermath, but it does not fix the moment of death.

Battlefleet Gothic: Armada 1

Accumulating hull damage. Armada 1 uses location-based damage. Ships have subsystems such as weapons, engines and shields, and damage to those locations removes the facilities and abilities tied to them (GameInformer preview; Capsule Computers hands-off, observation). Shields ripple and burst back into life, and hull hits produce sparks and fires. At the far camera, the read is broadside exchange, shield bubbles and subsystem loss shown through effects and UI. Close up, the gothic hull detail and turret activity carry the damage read, but the hull does not visibly deform.

Breaches and internal damage. Critical damage and fires exist, and ships can become drifting hulks, but there is no authored interior and no persistent hole into the ship's guts. The breach read is fire and sparks on the surface. Weak for problem (a).

The kill and break-up. The kill is a massive final explosion, and the destroyed ship becomes terrain on the map, a wreck that other ships can hide behind (Capsule Computers hands-off). At distance, the explosion and the persistent wreck are readable. Close up, the wreck is a detailed model but it is not opened up in a staged way. The death is a single event, not a sequence. This is exactly the pattern the pack identifies as problem 2.

Battlefleet Gothic: Armada 2

Accumulating hull damage. Armada 2 keeps the subsystem and critical damage model. Crew losses cause critical damage and permanent efficiency penalties, and ships can become drifting hulks when the crew is killed but the hull survives (Namu wiki summary and Steam community observation; these are gameplay observations, not developer documentation). Shields burst back into life with a visible pulse, and ships explode with a massive engulfing final explosion (GameWatcher review, observation). At distance, the read is shield bursts, broadsides and large explosions. Close up, the detailed models and weapon fire carry the scene, but hull deformation and persistent breach interiors are not part of the visual language.

Breaches and internal damage. Fires and critical damage exist, but the game does not open a persistent interior. The breach read remains surface fire and explosion effects. Weak for problem (a).

The kill and break-up. The base game leans on a large final explosion. Modding discussions describe editing explosion sprite sequences and exporting pre-broken ship variants so that a ship appears to break into pieces with flames on each piece (Armada Fleet Command forum, observation from modding). That is evidence that the shipped game's default death is a sprite and explosion event, not a runtime structural break-up. At distance, the big fireball reads. Close up, the illusion weakens because the ship is not visibly coming apart into solid pieces. This is the exact failure mode in the pack.

Star Wars: Squadrons

Accumulating hull damage. Starfighters show damage through sparks, fire, smoke and cracked cockpit glass as they take hits (Vidaextra gameplay observation). Capital ships use a subsystem model: shield generators, a targeting system and a power system can be destroyed, and destroying the power system reveals weak points on the hull that glow blue and take extra damage (Ars Technica guide; PSNProfiles trophy guide). Shields are a visible bubble, and flying under the bubble lets you hit the hull directly (Ars Technica guide). At distance, the shield bubble and the subsystem markers are the read. Close up, cockpit damage and local fires on the fighter sell the damage.

Breaches and internal damage. The game does not open a persistent interior. Destroyed subsystems explode and reveal weak points, but the hull is not breached into a room or a burnt cavity. This is a weak reference for problem (a).

The kill and break-up. Starfighters pop in a single explosion. Capital ships are killed through a staged multi-phase sequence: turrets, shield generators, power system, then hull damage, then the final destruction (IGN Fleet Battles guide). The flagship kill is a large scripted event with a final blast and often an escape run. At distance, the shield collapse and the large final explosion read clearly. Close up, the explosions are detailed, but the ship does not break into solid structural pieces. The strength is the staged timing, not the physical break-up.

EVE Online

Accumulating hull damage. EVE introduced a new visual damage system in 2015 that visualises shield and armour damage in real time using animated textures, and it later added dirt levels and kill marks to ships (4Gamer GDC 2024 talk by CCP's Andre B. Bragason; CCP devblog Dirt and Killmarks). In 2025 CCP added a Smoke and Fire system and a Smart-Light system, a scalable way to render effects across ships. Damaged ships show smoke and fire, and you can see that a ship is in hull damage without opening the overview (MMORPG.com report on the CCP devblog). At distance, smoke and fire plumes are the read. Close up, animated hull textures and local fires sell the damage.

Breaches and internal damage. EVE shows hull damage as texture and fire, not as an open interior. There is no persistent breach geometry into a ship's compartments. Weak for problem (a).

The kill and break-up. CCP replaced the old centre-out flare with a 3D, multi-part explosion system in 2016. The explosion spreads according to the damaged locations on the ship, and the effect was designed to look good both close up and from a camera up to 600 km away (4Gamer GDC 2024 talk). At distance, the multi-part explosion and the colour and light changes are readable. Close up, the explosion has depth and multiple pieces. EVE is a strong reference for distance-readable explosions, but it does not solve the solid-piece break-up problem because ships are destroyed by an effect, not by a visible structural separation.

Nebulous: Fleet Command

Accumulating hull damage. Nebulous uses a detailed positional damage model. Each ship's survival is dictated by its systems rather than a general health bar, and individual components can be targeted. Damage control teams move from compartment to compartment to repair damage (Hooded Horse press release). High-explosive shells create an explosion sphere inside the ship at a point along their path, and any components that touch that sphere are damaged (Hooded Horse wiki, Component Damage). At distance, the read is the ship status display and the loss of function, plus fires and smoke. Close up, the internal component damage is legible through the ship's behaviour and the damage control UI, not through a visible hole.

Breaches and internal damage. The simulation models internal damage, but the visual does not open the hull into a burnt interior. This is a strong systemic reference and a weak visual reference for problem (a).

The kill and break-up. A reactor detonation produces a gigantic explosion with a 1.5 km radius that shreds armour and damages nearby ships, and nearby radar is scrambled (Hooded Horse wiki, Damage Control; review observation). Wrecks persist but do not break up into debris, which players have asked for (Steam community feedback, observation). At distance, the explosion is dramatic. Close up, the wreck is a single object rather than a field of solid pieces. The lesson is that systemic internal damage is not the same as a visual breach, and a large explosion is not a staged break-up.

Highfleet

Accumulating hull damage. Highfleet uses a modular damage model. Engines can be shot off, guns can jam, ammunition magazines can detonate, and parts physically fall away from the ship (DTF first-look observation; GamePretty guide). Because the game is a 2D side view, the damage is unusually readable at a glance: the silhouette changes as modules are lost, and fires and smoke pour from the gaps. At distance, the silhouette change and the smoke column are the read. Close up, the pixel art and the flying debris carry the damage.

Breaches and internal damage. Destroyed modules leave gaps in the ship, and fires and smoke come from those gaps, but there is no interior to look into because the game is 2D. The breach read is a hole in the silhouette plus fire and smoke. This is a useful reference for cheap, readable damage, not for 3D interiors.

The kill and break-up. Ammunition magazine detonations chain through connected magazines and badly damage or destroy surrounding parts (GamePretty guide, observation). The ship comes apart into modules, and the explosion is accompanied by heavy smoke. At distance, the kill reads because the silhouette disintegrates and the smoke plume is large. Close up, the parts and fire are visible, but there is no 3D internal structure. Highfleet is the best reference in the pack for making a small unit's death read through silhouette change and motion rather than through brightness alone.

What transfers to this game

The constraints change the answer. There are no authored interiors, no seams and no separate wreck meshes. Everything must come from one surface mesh at runtime. The flash budget is fixed at three counted large flashes per second, so brightness is not a lever. The good news is that the current effects cost about 0.1 ms, so there is room for geometry and shader work as long as it is cheap and pooled.

The techniques that survive are the ones that create structure from the existing surface, not the ones that depend on offline authored content.

Shader-cut holes. A spatial shader on the hull can discard fragments inside a small set of object-space breach spheres. Each breach is a uniform with a centre, a radius and a noise seed. The fragment shader uses a noise-modulated distance test to create an irregular torn edge, and it darkens and roughens the rim. This makes a real hole in the hull mesh without editing the mesh or using CSG. The cost is negligible. The hull shader becomes slightly more expensive, and there is some overdraw where the interior behind the hole is drawn, but with a handful of breaches per ship this is well under 0.1 ms. Godot's shader discard is the cheapest way to get a hole in a single mesh (Godot shading language documentation, spatial shader fragment discard). Do not use runtime CSG for this. CSG boolean recalculation is too slow for gameplay and is not meant for runtime changes (Godot community performance guidance; CSG runtime boolean is too slow).

A burn sleeve behind the hole. The hole is only convincing if there is something behind it that reads as the ship's thickness and structure. Generate a short inward sleeve at the breach: a deformed tube or box, 300 to 800 triangles, with a dark charred material, a few rib silhouettes and a black cap at the back. Place it so its front edge meets the discarded hull edge. Add two or three emissive strips inside the sleeve, and a small GPUParticles3D emitter at the rim for smoke and embers. The sleeve is the key to problem (a). It gives the hole depth, thickness and a burnt interior without a modelled room. A lit room reads as a dollhouse. A dark sleeve with structure and small fires reads as guts.

Decals and vertex denting for accumulation. Godot's Decal node projects a texture onto the hull without mesh generation, and it is cheap when the decals are small and screen coverage is limited (Godot documentation, Using decals). Use decals for scorch rings, burn streaks and small impact pits. Use a vertex shader to push the hull inward slightly around each impact point, so the surface is not perfectly smooth. The vertex displacement is nearly free, and it adds the "crumpled metal" read that decals alone cannot give. Fade decals over time and cull them at distance so the count stays bounded (Godot documentation, Using decals).

Precomputed fracture patterns for death. Runtime Voronoi fracture is possible in Godot, but a death-frame hitch is unacceptable with a 40+ fps 1% low target. Precompute a fracture pattern per hull at load, or on first sight, and cache it. The pattern is a set of convex cell meshes generated from the hull's bounding volume and clipped against the hull surface. At death, split the hull along the cached pattern, cap the cut faces and spawn RigidBody3D pieces. Because the game is turn-based and death is a staged moment, you can spread the spawn over two or three frames behind the zoom punch. This keeps the expensive work off the death frame. The Godot-Destruction library uses plane slicing for basic holes and Voronoi generation for fracture, and it explicitly says precomputed destruction is far more efficient than fully dynamic destruction (Godot-Destruction GitHub, README). That is the right model here: procedural at load, instantiated at death.

Layered cut-face material. Every fracture piece must have a cap on the cut face. The cap should not be a flat grey polygon. Give it a layered cross-section: a dark outer armour band, a mid-tone structural band and a hot emissive core band that cools over a few seconds. Extrude the cut edge inward by a small amount and bevel it so the piece has visible thickness. This is the single most important fix for problem (b). A shell reads as a shell because the cut edge is paper-thin. A solid piece reads as solid because the edge has layers, thickness and heat.

Attached guts. Add two to four simple deformed meshes to each large piece: a bent pipe, a crushed box, a glowing reactor core. These do not need to be detailed. They need to break the silhouette and fill the interior volume when the piece is viewed from an angle. Use MultiMeshInstance3D for the small shards and for repeated gut pieces to keep draw calls low (Godot documentation, MultiMesh). Large pieces can be individual MeshInstance3D nodes with convex collision shapes, but pool them.

Minimum screen size for kill VFX. At the far camera, a small ship is only a few pixels. Scale the death effect root so that its projected size never drops below a threshold, for example 40 to 60 pixels. This is a cheap script that measures distance to the Camera3D and adjusts scale. It makes the effect readable without increasing its brightness, so it does not violate the flash budget.

Staged timing. The game already has paced event playback and a zoom punch. Use a Tween or AnimationPlayer to sequence the death: a short crack and flare, a separation of two or three large pieces, a drift phase, then secondary blasts on the pieces. The flash budget allows one counted large flash for the break. Secondary blasts should be emissive puffs and small lights that stay below the counted threshold. The sequence is free performance-wise and it directly fixes the "one fireball" complaint.

The techniques that die are authored pre-broken meshes, offline Houdini ship breaking, runtime CSG boolean, and full generated room interiors. They either violate the one-mesh constraint, cost too much at runtime, or read as set dressing.

The three named problems

(a) A breach that shows a burnt interior rather than a surface mark. The cheapest convincing method is the shader-cut hole plus the burn sleeve. The hull shader discards fragments inside a noisy object-space sphere, so the hole is in the hull itself, not on it. Behind the hole, spawn a short inward sleeve of dark, charred geometry with ribs, a black back cap and two or three small emissive fires. Add a rim of torn, curled metal by displacing the vertices near the hole outward and giving them a hot emissive tint that cools. Add smoke and embers from the rim. This fixes the player's complaint because the before and after are structurally different. Before, the hull is continuous with a scorch mark. After, there is a dark cavity with visible thickness and internal structure, and the smoke and fire come from inside the hole, not from a decal on the surface. The lit room approach fails because it shows a diorama. The sleeve succeeds because it shows the ship's burnt guts.

(b) A break-up whose pieces read as solid, not shell. The cheapest convincing method is the capped, layered cut face with a short inward extrusion, plus attached guts. Each fracture piece is a closed mesh with a cut cap. The cap uses a three-band material: armour, structure, hot core. The cut edge is extruded inward and beveled so it catches light. Two to four simple gut meshes are parented to the piece. This fixes the complaint because any viewing angle shows thickness, layers and internal clutter. The piece cannot read as an empty shell when its cut face is a solid cross-section and its interior has structure. The current pieces read as shell because they are open surfaces. Closing the cut face and adding heat and guts is cheap: a few hundred triangles per piece.

(c) A small-ship kill that reads from the far battle camera. The cheapest convincing method is a two-stage sequence with a minimum on-screen size. Stage one is a short crack and flare, using one counted flash, with the ship's silhouette breaking visibly into two or three large pieces. Stage two is a scatter and glow phase, with pieces separating on diverging vectors, emissive trails, and a persistent glowing core that fades over one to two seconds. Scale the effect so its projected size stays above a threshold. Add the existing zoom punch and a brief pause in the action. This fixes the complaint because the eye at distance reads silhouette change and motion before it reads small bright particles. A small pop is over before the eye finds it. A visible separation, a widening debris ring and a lingering glow give the eye time to register the kill. The flash budget is respected because only the initial flare counts, and the rest is emissive debris and smoke.

Ranked plan

1. Add shader-cut breach holes with the burn sleeve in fx_breach.gd. The player sees a real hole with dark, ribbed, burning structure behind it instead of a surface mark.

2. Add the layered cut-face material and cap every fracture piece in fx_death.gd. The player sees solid cross-sections with armour, structure and a hot core, not paper-thin shells.

3. Precompute fracture patterns per hull at load and cache them in fx_death.gd. The player sees a consistent break-up with no death-frame hitch, and the 1% low stays intact.

4. Add the minimum screen-size scaler and the two-stage small-ship kill sequence in fx_death.gd. The player sees small kills at the far camera as a visible break and scatter, not a pop.

5. Attach two to four simple gut meshes and cooling rim lights to large pieces in fx_death.gd. The player sees internal clutter and heat from any angle, which reinforces the solid read.

6. Add staged timing with Tween or AnimationPlayer for crack, separation, drift and secondary blasts in fx_death.gd, keeping one counted flash per death. The player sees a sequence instead of a single fireball.

7. Add persistent scorch decals and vertex denting for accumulating hull damage in fx_hull.gd. The player sees damage build up before the breach or death, so the final event has a visual history.

8. Add MultiMesh pooling for debris shards and GPUParticles3D pooling for smoke and embers in fx_death.gd. The player sees the same effects with lower draw-call and spawn cost, protecting the 40+ fps target.
