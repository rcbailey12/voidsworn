Destruction and Damage Research Leg
Per Game
Homeworld 3 (Blackbird Interactive, 2024, Unreal Engine)
Accumulating hull damage. At the far tactical camera, damaged ships show glowing orange scorch points where they have been hit, and the overall silhouette dims slightly as hull integrity drops. These are damage decals and emissive scorch shaders applied to the hull material, scaled to remain visible at distance (gameplay observation, Homeworld 3 campaign and skirmish footage). Up close, the scorch marks resolve into darker burnt areas with faint ember glow, and some panel geometry appears recessed or missing through what reads as mesh swapping to pre-broken hull variants on the larger capital ships (gameplay observation). Smaller fighters show almost no accumulating damage; they simply die quickly.
Breaches and internal damage. Homeworld 3 does not model true interiors. When a capital ship takes a critical subsystem hit, fire and particle venting pour from the wound point. Up close you see volumetric smoke and flame particles issuing from a darkened hole, but there is no authored interior geometry behind it. The hole reads as a dark socket with fire particles, not a room you could enter. This is a known weakness relative to the game's cinematic ambitions (gameplay observation, campaign missions where the camera dwells on damaged flagships).
The kill and break-up. At distance, a capital ship death in Homeworld 3 is staged: the ship glows along internal lines for one to two seconds, then splits into large chunks along what appear to be authored seams, with a core flash and expanding shock ring. Secondary explosions pop along the drifting pieces over several seconds. Up close, the chunks carry scorch detail and trailing fire particles, and the interior faces of the split are dark with some ember glow. The technique is splitting along authored seams with pre-modelled interior faces on the chunk geometry, plus staged timing: glow, split, secondary pops, lingering debris (gameplay observation, Homeworld 3 death sequences across all ship classes). Fighters die with a single particle burst and a small flash, no break-up.
Sins of a Solar Empire 2 (Ironclad Games, 2024, Iron Engine)
Accumulating hull damage. Sins 2 is viewed almost entirely at a far tactical zoom. Hull damage shows as a progressive darkening and reddening of the ship's material, plus small fire particle clusters that appear at random points as integrity drops. There is no close-camera inspection mode in normal play, so the technique is designed purely for distance reading: tinted damage states and spawned fire particles (gameplay observation, Sins 2 skirmish matches).
Breaches and internal damage. Sins 2 does not do breaches in any meaningful sense. Ships do not open up to reveal interiors. Damage is communicated through surface particles and material changes only. This is plainly a gap in the game's destruction vocabulary, though the game's extreme zoom-out makes it less noticeable (gameplay observation).
The kill and break-up. At the far camera, capital ship deaths produce a large fireball, a shock ring, and several glowing chunks that drift outward. There is a brief brightening before the explosion that acts as a telegraph. The chunks are simple convex shapes with emissive edges, not detailed fractured geometry. Small ships die with a single flash and a tiny particle puff. The technique is a single explosion event with spawned chunk meshes and a shock ring shader, no authored seams, no staging beyond the initial brighten (gameplay observation, Sins 2 combat at various zoom levels). This is functional but not cinematic.
Battlefleet Gothic: Armada 1 (Tindalos Interactive, 2016, Unreal Engine)
Accumulating hull damage. Ships in Armada 1 show damage through progressive fire spawning on the hull and a visible darkening of the material. At the tactical distance where most combat occurs, the growing number of fire points and smoke trails reads clearly. Up close, you can see that the fires are particle emitters placed at predefined damage points on the hull, and the material darkens in broad zones rather than at precise hit locations (gameplay observation, Armada 1 campaign and skirmish).
Breaches and internal damage. Armada 1 does not open ships to show interiors. Critical hits produce larger fire bursts and sometimes a muffled secondary explosion effect, but the hull stays closed. The breach concept is communicated by fire intensity, not by geometry. This is a limitation of the game's scope (gameplay observation).
The kill and break-up. Armada 1 stages capital ship deaths well for the camera. The ship lists, fires intensify for two to three seconds (a death spiral), then the hull splits into two or three large pieces with a core flash. Pieces drift and burn. At distance the sequence reads as a clear event: brighten, split, scatter. Up close the split faces are simple dark polygons with some fire particles, not detailed interiors. The technique is staged timing with a death spiral, then splitting into a small number of pre-authored large chunks, plus particle fire on the chunks (gameplay observation, Armada 1 destruction sequences). Small ships explode in one beat with a fireball and a few sparks.
Battlefleet Gothic: Armada 2 (Tindalos Interactive, 2019, Unreal Engine)
Accumulating hull damage. Armada 2 improves on the first game by adding more granular damage states. Ships show progressive panel damage: sections of the hull appear scorched or missing as integrity drops. Fire points multiply. At the far camera this reads as a ship going from clean to visibly burning. Up close, the panel damage appears to use damage decals layered over the base material, plus some mesh swaps where entire armour plates vanish to reveal darker under-geometry (gameplay observation, Armada 2 campaign, Ork and Imperial ship classes).
Breaches and internal damage. Armada 2 adds limited breach visuals. When a ship takes heavy damage to a section, a dark opening appears with fire and smoke pouring out. Up close, the opening has some depth, suggesting a shallow interior box with emissive strips, but it is not a full modelled compartment. The technique is a darkened inset with particle fire and smoke, plus a faint interior glow shader on the inset faces (gameplay observation, Armada 2 close-up destruction moments). This is a step beyond Armada 1 but still shallow.
The kill and break-up. Armada 2 stages deaths in three beats for capital ships: critical fires and listing, a brightening pulse, then a multi-piece break-up with secondary explosions on the drifting chunks over two to four seconds. The chunks carry fire trails and eventually fade. At the far camera the sequence is very readable because of the staging and the size of the flash. Up close, the chunk geometry is simple but the fire and smoke particles sell the mass. The technique is staged timing with three distinct phases, splitting into authored chunk variants, secondary explosion particles on a timer, and persistent wreck debris for a few seconds (gameplay observation, Armada 2 destruction of Imperial cruisers and Ork battleships). Small ships get a faster two-beat death: flash, then two or three pieces scatter.
Star Wars: Squadrons (Motive Studios, 2020, Frostbite)
Accumulating hull damage. Squadrons is primarily a cockpit game, so damage is read at very close range on enemy ships and through cockpit damage on your own. Enemy hulls show progressive panel loss: pieces of the outer surface blow off, revealing darker under-geometry. Sparks and small fires appear at damage points. At a medium distance (the range you usually see enemies in dogfights), you see the panel loss as chunks flying off and the ship darkening. The technique is mesh swapping to progressively stripped variants, plus spark particles at hit points and small looping fire emitters (gameplay observation, Squadrons fleet battles and dogfights).
Breaches and internal damage. Squadrons does not model full interiors, but the stripped panels reveal dark cavities with some faint geometry inside, giving the impression of shallow internal structure. On larger ships (flagships in fleet battles), you can see glowing internal components through breaches. The technique is pre-modelled internal faces visible through removed panels, with emissive materials for machinery glow (gameplay observation, Squadrons fleet battle missions).
The kill and break-up. Enemy fighters die with a brief fire trail and a single explosion into particles. Capital ships in fleet battles take prolonged damage and eventually break into large sections with multiple secondary explosions. At the distance you typically view them, the capital ship death is a sequence of growing fires, a brightening, and then a two or three piece split with trailing debris. The technique for small ships is a single particle explosion. For capitals it is staged timing with fire buildup, then authored chunk separation with secondary explosion particles (gameplay observation, Squadrons fleet battle conclusions).
EVE Online (CCP Games, 2003 to present, proprietary engine)
Accumulating hull damage. EVE shows damage primarily through particle effects at the hit location: shield hits produce a coloured ripple, armour hits produce sparks, and hull hits produce a larger spark burst with a brief hull flash. There is no progressive visual degradation of the hull model itself. At the far tactical camera, you see the particle bursts and the shield ripples. Up close, the effects are the same particles at higher resolution; there is no additional geometry detail. The technique is layered particle effects keyed to damage type (shield, armour, hull) with no persistent hull state change (gameplay observation, EVE Online combat across multiple ship classes).
Breaches and internal damage. EVE does not do breaches. Ships do not open up. There are no interiors. This is a plain gap that the game has never addressed in its long life, largely because the camera rarely dwells on a single ship (gameplay observation, EVE Online, confirmed by absence across all ship classes).
The kill and break-up. EVE kills produce a single explosion scaled to ship size: a flash, a shockwave ring, a cloud of debris particles, and a lingering wreck object. Capital ships get a larger explosion with more debris, but it is still one event, not a staged sequence. At distance, the explosion reads well because of its scale. Up close, the wreck is a static darkened version of the ship with some scorch marks, but there is no break-up into physics pieces. The technique is a scaled explosion event with a particle debris cloud and a persistent static wreck mesh swap (gameplay observation, EVE Online kills from frigates to titans). This is functional and readable but not cinematic.
Nebulous: Fleet Command (Eridanus Industries, 2022, Unity)
Accumulating hull damage. Nebulous is the most granular damage model in this list. Ships have individual subsystems and armour sections. As armour strips away, you see the underlying hull darken and spark. At the tactical camera, damaged ships trail more smoke and fire. Up close, individual armour plates disappear to reveal dark structural geometry underneath, and sparks emit from damaged sections. The technique is per-section mesh swapping (armour plates removed to show under-mesh) plus particle sparks and smoke at damaged sections (gameplay observation, Nebulous fleet engagements, close inspection of damaged vessels).
Breaches and internal damage. Nebulous shows breaches as dark openings in the hull where armour has been stripped, with occasional venting particles (gas, sparks). The openings reveal a dark interior with faint structural ribs, but it is shallow, more like a darkened alcove than a room. Some ships show faint red emergency lighting inside breaches. The technique is hull openings revealing a simple interior shell with emissive strips, plus venting particles (gameplay observation, Nebulous close-up damage inspection).
The kill and break-up. Nebulous stages ship deaths with a brief period of cascading explosions along the hull, then the ship breaks into several large pieces that drift and continue to vent gas and spark. At the tactical camera, the cascading flashes and the scatter of pieces read clearly. Up close, the break pieces show internal structural faces with some detail, and fires continue on the drifting chunks for several seconds. The technique is a timed sequence of secondary explosion particles along the hull, then splitting into a small number of large chunks with interior faces, plus lingering fire and vent particles on the chunks (gameplay observation, Nebulous destruction sequences). Small craft die with a single explosion and two or three pieces.
Highfleet (Konstantin Koshutin, 2021, custom engine)
Accumulating hull damage. Highfleet is an atypical entry: its ships are large, slow, and viewed at a medium distance during artillery exchanges. Hull damage appears as visible dents, holes, and fires on the ship's surface. At the combat distance, you see smoke columns growing and fires spreading. Up close, individual hits produce holes with dark interiors and small debris particles. The technique is decal-based holes with a dark fill, plus growing fire and smoke particle emitters at hit locations (gameplay observation, Highfleet artillery duels).
Breaches and internal damage. Highfleet's breaches are the dark holes mentioned above. They show a black or very dark interior with occasional spark particles. There is no modelled interior room. The technique is a dark decal or inset with spark particles, giving the impression of depth without geometry (gameplay observation, Highfleet close inspection after artillery hits).
The kill and break-up. Highfleet ships die by catching fire progressively, listing, and eventually breaking apart into a few large sections that fall (the game has gravity, being atmospheric). At the viewing distance, the sequence of growing fire, tilt, and break is clear. Up close, the break pieces are simple and the interiors are dark. The technique is staged timing with fire buildup and list, then splitting into a few large pieces with gravity-driven fall, plus persistent fire on the wreckage (gameplay observation, Highfleet ship destruction sequences). This game shows that staging and physical motion (falling, listing) can sell a death even with simple geometry.
What Transfers to This Game
Given the pack's constraints (Godot 4.7 Forward+, one AI-generated mesh per ship, no authored interiors or seams, runtime generation, 20 ships, the flash budget), here is what survives and what dies.
Survives: staged timing with a death spiral. Every game that reads well at distance uses a multi-beat death sequence. This costs almost nothing in Godot: a timer in fx_death.gd that sequences phases. Use a simple state machine: critical fire phase (spawn fire particles along the hull, 1 to 2 seconds), brighten pulse (animate an emissive uniform on the ship's material), break phase, secondary pops on chunks. No geometry work required.
Survives: particle venting and fire on break-up chunks. Nebulous and Armada 2 both sell chunks with lingering fire and gas vent. In Godot, attach GPUParticles3D nodes to each physics chunk at generation time. Use a small fire shader on the particle material. Cost: one particle system per chunk, capped at 60 particles each. With 20 ships and at most 4 to 6 chunks per death, this is well within budget.
Survives: interior box geometry generated at breach time. The current build already does this. The problem is not the technique but the execution. In Godot, generate a small box of geometry (floor, ceiling, two side walls) behind the breach opening using SurfaceTool or ImmediateMesh at breach time. Apply a dark material with faint emissive strips (light strips) and a scorch shader. Cost: one small mesh per active breach, perhaps 200 triangles. Negligible.
Survives: screen-space scaling of effects for distance reading. The current build already sizes flashes in screen pixels. Extend this: chunk glow, fire particle size, and shock ring scale should all be computed from camera distance so they hold at the far tactical zoom. Use the Camera3D node's project_position or simply compute distance in the shader.
Survives: persistent wrecks for a few seconds. EVE and Nebulous both leave debris. In Godot, keep the chunk physics bodies alive for 8 to 12 seconds after death before freeing them. Cost is just memory and a few Jolt bodies.
Dies: authored seam splitting. Homeworld 3 and Armada 2 split along hand-authored seams. This game has one AI mesh with no seams. You cannot do this. Instead, use runtime Voronoi or plane cuts through the mesh to generate chunks. Godot does not have a built-in mesh slicer, so you would write a plane-cut routine that produces 3 to 5 convex chunks from the single mesh. Cost: a one-time computation at death, perhaps 1 to 2 ms for a 5000-triangle mesh. Acceptable because deaths are staged moments, not per-frame.
Dies: mesh swapping to pre-broken variants. Squadrons and Sins use pre-authored damage states. With one AI mesh per ship and no variants, this is not available. All damage must be procedural.
Dies: per-section armour plate removal (Nebulous). Requires authored sub-meshes. Not possible with a single mesh.
The Three Named Problems
(a) A breach that shows a burnt interior rather than a surface mark. The cheapest convincing method: at breach time, cut a hole in the hull mesh (or hide the triangles in the breach radius), then spawn a small interior box (SurfaceTool, 6 faces, roughly 150 triangles) positioned just behind the opening. Give the interior faces a dark base colour, a scorch texture applied via triplanar projection in a ShaderMaterial, and two thin emissive strips (the light strips already in the build). Add a GPUParticles3D venting dark smoke and a faint orange glow from one edge. The key change from the current build is making the interior box physically recessed: pull it 0.3 to 0.5 metres behind the hull surface so the camera sees depth when it zooms in. Add a subtle rim of torn metal around the opening by extruding the cut edge outward 2 cm and applying a glowing-edge shader that cools over 10 seconds. This fixes the player's complaint because the breach will no longer read as a flat decal; the camera will see a dark recessed volume with burnt walls and a glowing edge, which is what "a hole into a burnt interior" means visually.
(b) A break-up whose pieces read as solid, not shell. The cheapest convincing method: when you generate break-up chunks via plane cuts, also generate a cap face for each cut plane. The cap face gets a distinct material: dark metal base, faint internal rib geometry (three or four thin box meshes laid across the cap), and a few small emissive points suggesting severed wiring or piping. The cap face is the interior of the ship made visible at the break. Without it, chunks are hollow shells and the player sees through them. In Godot, after the plane cut, identify the open edge loop on each chunk, triangulate it into a cap using a simple fan or ear-clipping fill, and assign it to a separate surface with the interior material. Cost: a few dozen extra triangles per chunk, negligible. Additionally, give each chunk a slight thickness by duplicating the outer surface offset inward by 3 cm and connecting the edges, so no chunk is a single-sided shell. This fixes the complaint directly: every break face shows a dark, detailed interior surface, and no viewing angle reveals hollowness.
(c) A small-ship kill that reads from the far battle camera. The cheapest convincing method: scale the kill effect to a minimum screen-space size regardless of ship size. At the far camera, a small ship's explosion should still occupy at least 40 by 40 pixels. Achieve this by computing the screen-space size of the effect from camera distance and scaling the particle system's emission radius, the shock ring's radius, and the flash's quad size accordingly. In Godot, in fx_death.gd, get the distance from the Camera3D to the dying ship, compute a scale factor so the effect hits the minimum pixel size, and apply it to the particle emitter's scale and the shock ring shader's radius uniform. Add a brief 0.3 second slow-motion on the kill frame (scale the playback timer to 0.3 for 300 ms) so the player's eye catches the event even at distance. Add a small camera shake (2 to 3 pixels) on kill. This costs nothing in draw calls and fixes the complaint because the small ship's death now punches visually at any zoom level instead of being a sub-pixel pop.
Ranked Plan
Step 1. Breach interior recess. In fx_breach.gd, offset the generated interior box 0.4 metres behind the hull surface, add the extruded torn-metal rim with the cooling glow shader, and apply the scorch triplanar shader to the interior faces. The player sees: zooming into a breach now reveals a dark burnt room with depth, not a flat mark.
Step 2. Break-up cap faces and thickness. In fx_death.gd, after plane-cutting the mesh into chunks, generate cap faces with rib detail and the interior material, and add the 3 cm inward offset shell to each chunk. The player sees: break-up pieces that look solid from every angle, with dark detailed interiors at every break line.
Step 3. Screen-space scaling for all kill effects. In fx_death.gd, compute camera distance and scale flash radius, shock ring, particle emission radius, and chunk glow to a minimum pixel footprint. The player sees: small ship kills now register clearly at the far tactical camera as distinct events.
Step 4. Three-beat death staging for capital ships. In fx_death.gd, add a state machine: phase one (1.5 seconds of intensifying fire particles and hull glow), phase two (0.4 second brighten pulse), phase three (break into chunks with secondary explosions on a 2-second timer). The player sees: big ship deaths become a sequence with anticipation and payoff instead of one fireball.
Step 5. Kill slow-motion and camera nudge. In fx_death.gd, on any kill, scale the event playback timer to 0.3 for 300 ms and trigger a 2-pixel camera shake plus the existing zoom punch. The player sees: every kill, even a small fighter, gets a brief moment of emphasis that the eye can catch.
Step 6. Chunk lingering and secondary vents. In fx_death.gd, keep chunk physics bodies alive for 10 seconds, attach a small GPUParticles3D fire emitter to each chunk, and trigger one or two secondary vent bursts at random times. The player sees: the battlefield retains visible wreckage that continues to burn and vent, selling the aftermath.
Step 7. Distance-adaptive fire and smoke on accumulating damage. In fx_hull.gd, scale the number and size of fire particle emitters on a damaged ship by both damage state and camera distance, so that at far zoom the fires are fewer but larger, and up close they are more numerous but smaller. The player sees: damaged ships read clearly at any zoom without overdraw at close range.
Step 8. Reduce-flashing compliance check. Wire all new flashes (the brighten pulse, secondary pops, kill flash) into the existing 3-flash-per-second counter and the reduce-flashing setting. The player sees: no seizure-risk regression, and the reduce-flashing setting still works cleanly with the new staged deaths.
