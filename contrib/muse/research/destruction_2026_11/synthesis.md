# Ship destruction research: chair synthesis (Nov 1, 2026)

Commission: Opus brief `contrib/opus/briefs/muse_2026-11-01_destruction_research.md`. Four independent legs ran from the same evidence pack and leg prompt: DeepSeek (DeepThink and Search on), Qwen (Qwen3.8-Max, both model gates verified; the access slider dropped an attachment mid-run and it was re-attached before sending), Argon (Gemini, Argon High verified in the picker and the prompt bar before and during generation), and a blind Rey leg (pack and prompt only, own web research). The legs are filed verbatim beside this synthesis: `leg_deepseek.md`, `leg_qwen.md`, `leg_argon.md`, `leg_rey.md`.

## The headline diagnosis (all four legs agree)

The current effects do not fail for lack of geometry or budget. They fail on two specific things:

1. **The interiors are lit like the exterior.** Decks, ceilings with light strips, and red-lit rooms read as a set inside a hollow hull. Every reference game that convinces does the opposite: the inside of a broken ship is dark, with embers and silhouetted structure. Darkness is the fix, not more rooms.
2. **The hull skin is single-sided.** With backface culling on, any opening lets the camera see through the far wall to the sky. Until every opening is sealed with something opaque and every inside surface renders as dark solid, no amount of interior detail stops the shell read.

A third shared point: brightness is spent. The flash budget (3 counted large flashes per second, none under reduce-flashing) is a hard rule, and none of the legs spend it. Everything below buys readability with darkness, thickness, time, and motion instead.

## What the reference games actually prove

- **Homeworld 3** (best sourced leg material: Blackbird's own developer writing on Splat Damage and the Houdini Ship Breaking tool). Damage grades from scars to breaches on the surface, and the modelled interiors are baked into the wreck offline. The transferable part is the grading and the wreck guts. The pipeline itself (offline authored breaking) does not transfer to runtime generation from one AI mesh.
- **EVE Online** (CCP dev blogs, via DeepSeek and the Rey leg). The 2025 fire system is the model for distance-readable damage: fires trail once a ship is in hull damage, and the count scales by class (one for a frigate, two to three for cruisers and battleships, ten for structures), and CCP turns it off at low settings to protect fleet fights. Qwen's leg claims EVE has no progressive hull damage at all; that contradicts two sourced legs and the CCP material, so treat Qwen's EVE section as superseded by the other two.
- **Sins of a Solar Empire 2** is the cautionary case. Its hulls barely change when hit (players asked for exactly the damage this game is trying to build), and ships pop out of existence at death. Its one strong idea is rules-side: big ships enter a crippled state before dying, so death is staged by the game state, not just by effects. Its Ruins of War update also shows persistent unique wrecks help the aftermath but do not fix the moment of death.
- **Battlefleet Gothic: Armada 1 and 2** prove the hulk: a whole, dark, drifting ship is a kill that reads at any range and costs one model state. Their gunfire deaths are the failure mode in the pack (one big fireball, no structural break-up).
- **Star Wars: Squadrons** contributes one technique above all: the death spiral. A killed fighter corkscrews with a smoke ribbon for half a second to a second and a half before it bursts. Motion and time, not flash size, make a small kill read.
- **Nebulous: Fleet Command** has the deepest internal damage simulation and almost no visual breach. Its kills are often quiet: lights out, a gas cloud, a hulk that stays. The lesson cuts both ways: internal simulation is not a visual breach, and a kill confirmed by darkness and behaviour reads better than a slightly bigger explosion.
- **Highfleet** is the silhouette lesson. Ships come apart module by module, and the damage readout at every range is the shape of the ship changing. A dark backing layer behind the outer blocks means a breach never shows sky through the middle.

## The three named problems, cheapest convincing method for each

**(a) A breach that shows a burnt interior.** Four parts, all cheap. (1) A hull shader rule: inside surfaces (back faces) shade as near-black burnt interior with ember noise, so any hole looks into darkness, never into a lit shell. (2) Cut a real hole: discard the hull skin inside the breach mouth in the fragment shader, with a blackened soot collar and a torn, heat-tinted rim that cools over seconds. (3) Behind the hole, a short recessed dark throat (a tube or box set a fraction of a metre inside the hull) with ember points, silhouetted ribs, and smoke coming from inside the hole. (4) Turn the ceiling light strips off inside breaches. The before and after become structurally different: continuous hull, then a dark cavity with thickness, heat, and depth.

**(b) A break-up whose pieces read as solid.** Cap every cut face with a dark, noise-displaced slag cap (armour band, structural band, hot core band that cools), set piece materials to render both sides with the dark interior rule on back faces, and let the existing ribs and deck edges protrude from the cap instead of floating inside an open cavity. On method, the legs split: DeepSeek would precompute fracture patterns per hull at load; Qwen would plane-cut at death (about 1 to 2 ms, acceptable in a staged moment); Argon would plane-slice with caps; the Rey leg would avoid CPU splitting entirely and clip the same mesh into two to four copies with a shared cap plug carrying the guts. Chair call: build the caps and the darkness rule first, since every method needs them. Then prototype the Rey clip-and-plug (least new code, no mesh surgery) and fall back to plane cuts only if plugs read thin in screenshots. That is a benchmark question for Opus, not a research question.

**(c) A small-ship kill that reads from the far camera.** Spend time and motion. A short death run first (about half a second of spin or lurch with a thick smoke and fire ribbon, per Squadrons and Homeworld 3), then the burst with a minimum on-screen size for the effect (DeepSeek and Qwen both land on roughly 40 to 60 pixels), directional shrapnel streamers for a wide spiky silhouette (Argon), and a tumbling chunk that lingers for several seconds trailing smoke with an ember glow that cools. One counted flash total. Qwen also proposed a brief slow-motion and camera shake on every kill; that is a feel call for Ryan and Opus, not adopted into the plan below.

## Ranked plan for godot/fx/ (8 steps)

1. **Interior darkness rule (fx_hull.gd).** Shade hull back faces as dark burnt interior with ember noise; use it on live ships and on death pieces. What Ryan sees: every hole, breach, and cut stops looking like an empty shell, everywhere, at once. This is the cheapest step and it lifts all the others.
2. **Seal the cuts (fx_death.gd).** Cap every break face with the layered slag cap, ribs protruding from the cap, cut rims carrying cooling heat. What Ryan sees: tumbling pieces with solid cross-sections and thick torn edges instead of paper-thin shells.
3. **Breaches become real holes (fx_breach.gd, fx_hull.gd).** Shader-discard the skin inside the breach mouth, soot collar, recessed dark throat with ember points and internal smoke, light strips off inside breaches. What Ryan sees: zooming into a breach shows a burnt cavity with depth, and a breach finally looks different before and after.
4. **Break-up generation (fx_death.gd).** Clip-and-plug prototype first (two to four clipped copies of the hull mesh, shared cap plugs with inset machinery faces, slow first separation); plane cuts as the fallback if the plugs read thin. Spread the work over two or three frames behind the zoom punch. What Ryan sees: ships come apart into a few large, solid pieces, with no death-frame hitch.
5. **Staged capital death (fx_death.gd).** About two seconds: power-down (running lights and engine glow die), venting along the future cut, a thermal crack line, a split into two capped halves, then one counted core flash inside one half while the other half drifts as a blackened hulk. What Ryan sees: a big ship dies as a sequence with a beginning, a break, and an aftermath, not one fireball.
6. **Small-ship kill staging (fx_death.gd).** Death spin with a smoke and fire ribbon, minimum on-screen effect size, shrapnel streamers, and a lingering tumbling chunk with a cooling ember. What Ryan sees: small kills register from the battle camera as an event with a trail and a wreck, not a pop.
7. **Damage that reads at distance (fx_hull.gd).** Damage sockets sampled from the hull surface at load (no authored points needed): smoke first, then fire, then venting as hull fraction falls, counts capped by ship class the way CCP caps EVE's fires; plus a cumulative soot and scar record in the hull shader so ships carry their damage for the rest of the battle. What Ryan sees: any ship's condition from across the field without a health bar, and battered ships look battered before they break.
8. **Hulk outcome and guardrails (fx_death.gd, fx_pool.gd).** Some kills stay whole: lights out, one gas vent, cold drift (the Armada and Nebulous lesson). Wire every new flash into the existing counter and the reduce-flashing setting, and pool the new debris and particle work the way the current pass pools. What Ryan sees: a second kind of death that leaves no doubt the ship is dead, and no regression on the flash budget or the frame rate.

## Disagreements and open points for Opus

- Fracture method (step 4) is the one real fork. The legs' cost claims are estimates, none measured in this engine on these meshes. Benchmark clip-and-plug against one plane-cut hull on the RTX 5080 and pick from screenshots plus the 1% low.
- Qwen's leg is observation-only throughout and its EVE section conflicts with the sourced legs; weight it lowest on facts, though its engineering suggestions (recess depth, cap triangulation, compliance wiring) agree with the others.
- Argon flags several of its own Homeworld 3 and Armada claims [UNVERIFIED] in its leg; the DeepSeek and Rey legs source the same ground from Blackbird's blog, so the synthesis relies on those.
- Nothing here needs a Ryan ruling except, optionally, Qwen's slow-motion-on-kill idea, which the chair left out of the plan.
