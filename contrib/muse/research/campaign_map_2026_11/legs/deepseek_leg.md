1. Headline

The next step is not another 3D rebuild. It is a presentation pass that makes the existing system view feel authored: one reusable sector sky, side-lit stop dioramas, lanes that read direction and knowledge, labels that appear in tiers as the camera moves, and a hold-to-reveal tactical overlay for danger, rewards, cores, and the Nemesis clock. The single research technique that matters most is zoom-linked information tiering, drawn from Stellaris and Homeworld: show type, ownership, and threat at a distance; show name, tags, and reward at mid zoom; show full detail only when close or on hover. That one technique lets the map stay cinematic at rest and informative on demand, which is exactly what R72 and the owner's cheap-panel complaint require.

2. Research notes per game

Sins of a Solar Empire 2

Camera. Sins uses one seamless camera for strategic and tactical play. The mouse wheel zooms from the whole map to the smallest ship, WASD pans, and the right mouse button rotates around the focused object. Q and E jump to maximum and minimum zoom. The store page states the design plainly: there is no separate strategic mode and battle mode, and the player instantly zooms between them. Sources: Stardock Basic Game Controls, https://stardock.atlassian.net/wiki/pages/viewpage.action?pageId=1905361001 ; Steam page, https://store.steampowered.com/app/1575940/Sins_of_a_Solar_Empire_II/ .

Scale tricks. Sins makes a system feel large by making it move. Planets orbit, moons orbit, and phase lane connections open and close as bodies move. A UI button projects the movement of scouted worlds and their phase lines for the next hour, with a slider between now and then. Source: Stardock video blog, https://www.stardock.net/article/522588/fog-of-war-in-gravity-wells-and-scanning-abilities-of-ships , and Steam dev journal comments, https://steamcommunity.com/app/1575940/eventcomments/4355620764349257892/ .

Icons over 3D. Planet icons carry ownership marks, fleet bars, and status beacons. A red beacon on the upper right of a planet icon marks an enemy phase jump inhibitor. Ships become icons at distance; community reports describe the model-to-icon switch as a zoom threshold, and players ask for that distance to be configurable. Source for the beacon: official change notes, https://stardock.atlassian.net/wiki/plugins/viewsource/viewpagesrc.action?pageId=2008088596 . The model-to-icon threshold is my impression from community discussion, not a formal manual statement.

Route lines. Phase lanes connect gravity wells. Lanes are not permanent scenery; they can break as planets move. The future-projection tool exists precisely because lanes change. Source: Stardock video blog above.

Fog and information hiding. Sins currently gives full intel on everything in a gravity well once the player jumps in, regardless of sensor or sight range. An official article debates whether this should change and notes that the game already shows how old intel is on a gravity well. Scouting mainly extends vision and detects incoming fleets. Source: official article, https://www.sinsofasolarempire2.com/article/522588/fog-of-war-in-gravity-wells-and-scanning-abilities-of-ships . The scouting detail is my impression from community sources.

Danger and rewards. Bounties are visible on the map: the bounty amount sits above the planet icon, and pirate raid danger is shown as a gauge. This is a secondary source, the Sins wiki, https://wikiwiki.jp/sins2/懸賞金 , so treat it as an impression rather than a manual quote.

Stellaris

Camera. Stellaris has a galaxy view and a separate system view. The galaxy view pans with WASD or middle mouse and zooms with the wheel; Home fits the whole galaxy. The system view is an up-close look at one star system where planets, stations, and fleets are visually located. Source: Stellaris wiki main interface, https://stellaris.fandom.com/wiki/Main_interface .

Scale tricks. Stellaris does not use real distances. It uses a hyperlane graph and zoom thresholds. Fleet icons appear above a camera height of 300, star names above 800, and galaxy fleet icons at 2300. This is data-driven and documented in the defines. Source: Stellaris wiki defines, https://stellaris.paradoxwikis.com/Defines .

Icons over 3D. System assets carry the owning empire's flag, a status bar, and an asset icon. The status bar is green for owned, blue for friendly, grey for neutral, and red for enemy; it also shows military power and hull, armor, and shield levels. Points of interest appear as exclamation points in hexagons, and the same hexagon appears on the galaxy view. Source: main interface wiki above.

Route lines. Hyperlane connections are shown as triangles pointing out of a system. Clicking an explored green triangle moves the view. Hovering gives intel level, survey status, and ownership. Lanes are hidden until detected, and hyperlane detection range is typically twice normal sensor range. Source: Stellaris wiki FTL travel, https://stellaris.paradoxwikis.com/FTL_travel .

Fog and information hiding. At game start the player sees system locations and types, but not hyperlanes, system names, or system features. Nebulae block all sensor coverage from other systems, so what is inside and beyond a nebula is hidden without a ship or station. Source: FTL travel wiki above and exploration wiki, https://stellaris.paradoxwikis.com/Exploration .

Danger and rewards. Fleets show a strength number, and the game uses relative power categories and a red battle icon when a fleet is engaged. This is functional but spreadsheet-like. Source: Stellaris wiki space warfare, https://stellaris.paradoxwikis.com/Space_warfare .

Homeworld 3

Camera. Homeworld 3 uses a free 3D camera with pan, rotate, and zoom, plus tactical pause. The Sensor Manager is a separate strategic view toggled with the spacebar. It shows a tactical overview broken into friendly or controlled units and hostile forces. Sources: official Homeworld Universe dev update, https://www.homeworlduniverse.com/war-games-feedback/ ; Epic Games Store guide, https://store.epicgames.com/en-US/news/homeworld-3-guide-15-tips-to-help-you-succeed-in-the-campaign .

Scale tricks. Homeworld uses NLIPS, Non-Linear Inverse Perspective Scrolling, which scales ship models up relative to camera distance so small ships remain visible and selectable when zoomed far out. Players can turn it off. The game also offers granular icon amalgamation presets. Source: official dev update above. This is the cleanest scale trick in the four games for a game with one fleet token.

Icons over 3D. In the Sensor Manager, friendly units, hostile forces, objectives, and engagements are marked with colored icons. Community guides describe red for engagement areas, yellow for current objectives, green for the mothership, blue for friendly, and triangles for resource points. Source: secondary guide, https://www.entertainment14.net/blog/post/111377215 . The color scheme is my impression from guides, not an official manual.

Route lines. Movement uses a movement disc; holding Ctrl converts a move order to attack move and the disc turns red. The Sensor Manager is the macro view for long-distance movement and objectives. Source: official dev update and Steam announcement, https://store.steampowered.com/news/posts/?feed=steam_community_announcements&enddate=1711562898 .

Fog and information hiding. Vision is controlled by sensor range. Things outside sensor range are basically unseen. The Sensor Manager reveals the tactical picture in range, not the whole map. Source: secondary guide, https://www.uhyx.cn/news/gl/202407/92133.html . Mark as impression.

Danger and rewards. The Sensor Manager advertises danger as red engagement zones and rewards as yellow objective markers and resource triangles. It is a momentary overlay, not a permanent spreadsheet. This is the right register for this game.

Endless Space 2

Camera. ES2 uses free pan and zoom on the galaxy map, with a separate system view. The scan view is a press-and-hold information layer whose content changes with zoom. Source: official manual contents list, https://manualzz.com/doc/26835546 , and secondary scan view wiki, https://bw.artemislena.eu/wiki/Scan_View .

Scale tricks. ES2 uses constellations, groups of star systems connected by star lanes, as a mid-scale unit. The constellation name appears when the player zooms out and is displayed once the constellation has been fully explored. The first player to reveal it is credited as its explorer. Source: official GDD 3, https://community.amplitude-studios.com/amplitude-studios/endless-space-2/forums/65-general/threads/18858-es2-gdd-3-galaxy-exploration .

Icons over 3D. System names and planet counts sit above systems. Empty circles are uncolonizable planets, white circles are colonizable, and colored circles belong to an empire. An influence circle and colored name mean the system is controlled. Source: secondary guide, https://www.gamepressure.com/endless-space-2/systems-and-ways-of-travelling/z29c4b .

Route lines. Star lanes are white lines. At the start, the player sees only the direction a lane goes, not where it leads or how far away it is. Dark connections are visible only when both ends are known. Source: official GDD 3 above.

Fog and information hiding. ES2 calls its fog "miasma." At the start, only lanes and systems close to the player are visible. Vision range permanently reveals systems and galactic objects. Probes extend vision. A constellation's name appears only after the player has explored it in its entirety. Sources: GDD 3 above and ES2 wiki galaxy page, https://endless-space-2.fandom.com/wiki/Galaxy .

Danger and rewards. ES2 advertises danger and reward weakly before commitment. Combat is auto resolved with a pre-battle setup screen, and players have complained that the pre-fight evaluation is not accurate enough. Source: community thread, https://community.amplitude-studios.com/amplitude-studios/endless-space-2/forums/65-general/threads/18858-es2-gdd-3-galaxy-exploration . This is my impression from the discussion, not a design statement. The lesson for this game is to avoid ES2's ambiguity: the route choice needs clearer danger and reward advertising than ES2 gives.

3. Steal and avoid

Steal

One-mode seamless zoom, from Sins. This game has one screen per sector and one fleet. A separate tactical mode would break the cinematic register. Sins proves that zoom can carry the player from a close stop to the whole sector without a mode switch. Tie: section 4 says route choice is the core decision and R72 says cinematic first. A seamless camera keeps the decision inside the same beautiful view.

Zoom-linked information tiers, from Stellaris and Homeworld. Stellaris uses hard zoom thresholds for fleet icons and star names; Homeworld uses icon amalgamation and a Sensor Manager. This game already has label priority rules, so the system exists. Extend it: far zoom shows stop type icon, faction crest, and danger diamond; mid zoom adds name and tags; close zoom adds reward and hover detail. Tie: section 4 lists seven pieces of at-a-glance information. Tiering is how those seven fit without a permanent sidebar.

NLIPS-style token scaling, from Homeworld. The fleet token must stay readable at system scale without becoming physically absurd. Homeworld scales models with camera distance and lets the player turn it off. Tie: section 4 has one player fleet and section 2 says the fleet token flies its jump. A token that shrinks to a pixel at sector view is a usability failure.

Hold-to-reveal tactical overlay, from Homeworld's Sensor Manager. The overlay should draw over the 3D, not replace it. It should show danger, rewards, cores, and Nemesis state as thin holographic marks, then disappear. Tie: R72 wants information on demand, and section 2 already uses holographic threat diamonds. This extends that language instead of adding panels.

Directional known-lane treatment, from ES2. ES2 shows lane direction before it shows the destination. This game can show the next lane as a thin "whisper" line with only the destination's type silhouette, then fill it in when the player arrives. Tie: section 4 is forward-only, so the player cannot go back. Showing direction and rough destination supports route choice without adding scouting mechanics.

Bounty-style reward marks, from Sins. Sins puts bounty numbers above planet icons. This game already has reward labels and safest and richest route lighting. Replace the text label with a small number of reward pips or a single ember glyph at far zoom, and keep the text for hover. Tie: section 4 says rewards must be readable before commitment.

Constellation naming on zoom out, from ES2. ES2 names a constellation only after it is fully explored. This game's sectors are already named, but the technique of revealing the sector name as a zoom-out reward is useful for sector transitions. Tie: section 4 says each run is three sectors, so the sector name can become a large, quiet label at far zoom, not a panel.

Avoid

Orbiting planets that shift lanes, from Sins. Sins makes orbits a mechanic. This game is forward-only and the route choice must be stable. Moving lanes would add a mechanic and undermine the core decision. Presentation-only version: the Hollow Sun's disc and the stop rings can rotate slowly, but lane topology does not change.

Stellaris-style hyperlane density and icon soup. Stellaris is built for many empires and many fleets. This game has one fleet and a small run. Dense lanes and permanent fleet icons would look like the spreadsheet the owner rejected.

Homeworld's completely free 3D camera. Homeworld lets the player rotate anywhere. This game needs composed arrival, mid-sector, and boss shots. Free camera would let the player break the composition and find empty angles. Use a constrained orbit with limited pitch and a zoom range, not full six-axis freedom.

A separate Sensor Manager that hides the 3D, from Homeworld. The owner wants the map to feel high quality and cinematic. Replacing the star system with a flat tactical view would throw away the art. Keep the overlay over the 3D.

Fog of war scouting mechanics, from Stellaris and ES2. The pack forbids new mechanics. Presentation-only version: all stops are visible as silhouettes, but their contents and rewards are hidden until the player is one lane away. Do not add probes or sensor ships.

ES2's vague pre-battle evaluation. ES2 often surprises the player with combat outcomes. This game's route choice depends on danger and reward being legible before commitment. Avoid any ambiguity that makes the threat diamond feel like decoration.

4. The design

System view

The sector view becomes a composed 3D scene, not a free camera. The Hollow Sun stays at the centre, always visible, with the battle world's two-part accretion disc, sky lensing, and photon ring reused from world_living.gd and the battle dead-star shader. The camera orbits the Sun at a fixed distance range: a high three-quarter view for sector overview, a lower three-quarter view for stop close-ups, and a boss framing that puts the Sun behind the flagship. No free pitch beyond a gentle tilt. This preserves the Sins-style cinematic arrival shot while keeping the map readable.

Each sector gets one baked sky from world_bake.gd, the same bake used in that sector's battles. The map sky and battle sky are the same asset, so the transition from map to battle is seamless. The sky is the single most important reusable asset in section 3. Quality Low uses the same bake at lower resolution, with fewer stars, no cloud layer, and no dust specks near the camera.

Planet stops use world_planet.gd side lighting: a lit face, a soft terminator, and a dark side. The current map planet stop is flatter than the battle planets; replacing its lighting with the battle planet shader fixes that. Belt stops use world_rocks.gd with the region's rock kind. Nebula pockets use the battle gas wisps. The graveyard stop uses the battle's real death pieces. Every stop gets a soft contact shadow and a thin orbital arc instead of a flat ring.

Lanes

Lanes stay forward-only. Each lane is a glowing bezier curve from stop to stop, with a slow directional pulse toward the boss. Lane color is a blend of the two stops' faction colors, desaturated enough that threat diamonds and reward pips still read. The safest and richest route lighting already built stays, but it becomes a subtle brightness change on the lane, not a separate neon overlay.

Known lanes show full color and a solid line. The next lane beyond the player's current stop shows as a thin, dotted whisper line with only the destination's stop-type silhouette. This is the presentation-only version of ES2's directional lane visibility. The player always knows which way the run goes, but not exactly what is at the far end until they commit. No scouting mechanic is added; arrival reveals the stop.

Stops

Each stop remains one clear 3D object, but it now sits in the same lighting model as the battle world. The stop ring becomes a thin arc of light that hugs the object, tinted by kind. The label system gets three tiers. Far tier: stop type icon, faction crest, danger diamond count, and reward pips. Mid tier: name, tags, and a short faction label. Near tier or hover: full detail card with reward text, danger explanation, and any event or core hint. Existing label priority rules remain: labels that would run under the run ribbon hide, crowded stacks keep name and tags, and place type shows on hover only.

Panels and text

The large opaque sidebars go away. At the bottom of the screen sits one slim run ribbon: the Nemesis clock as a thin segmented bar, and the cores in range as small ember glyphs with names. The ribbon is always visible but never covers the system view. Selecting a core glyph draws a faint arc toward the lane region where that core can be reached, without revealing the exact stop until the player is close. This is presentation-only; it does not change core range or routing.

Text uses one type scale and one family. The owner called panels and text cheap, so the fix is not more decoration. It is fewer sizes, higher contrast, wider letter spacing at small sizes, and no full-width dark boxes. Detail cards appear on hover or selection, over a dimmed system, not over a black panel. The existing fleet, shipyard, and battle pages keep their dimmed-system treatment, but their headers and body text adopt the same type scale. Quality Low uses the same layout with a static ribbon and no background blur.

Sector transitions

The three sectors are visible as three concentric lane rings around the Hollow Sun. In sector 1, the player sees the outer two rings only as faint orbital lines. As the player approaches the sector boss, the next sector's sky tint begins to show at the edge of the screen, and the Sun's lensed arcs bend toward it. This is the visual promise that sector 2 exists. On boss completion, the camera pulls back to show the three-ring structure and the next sector's sky bake crossfades in. The Hollow Sun stays at the centre through the whole transition, so the run always feels like it is happening around one object.

Sector 2 and 3 are foreshadowed by faction color in the lane glow and by the sky bake. The Hollow Sun's accretion disc carries the Nemesis state: it is calm at low clock, and the inner ring brightens and pulses when the clock is nearly full. This reuses the battle dead-star disc and makes the Nemesis clock part of the sky, not a separate panel.

Information layer

At rest, the map shows only the baseline: stop type, faction crest, danger diamonds, reward pips, the run ribbon, and the Hollow Sun. Holding the tactical overlay key (spacebar or controller equivalent) brings up a thin holographic layer over the 3D. It draws the safest and richest route lines brighter, marks each stop's danger band as a thin ring, and shows the cores in range as small arcs. It does not replace the 3D and it does not pause the game. This is the Homeworld Sensor Manager technique, but as an overlay rather than a mode.

Danger is advertised by diamond count and color, plus ring thickness at the stop. Reward is advertised by pips and lane brightness. The Nemesis clock is always in the ribbon, and the disc pulse makes it felt in the scene. The player can read the whole run at a glance without opening a single panel, which is the R72 register.

5. Mock layout per sector

Sector 1: Hullbreakers and Hegemony space

The Hollow Sun sits at the centre, large, with a cold blue-white photon ring and a thin accretion disc. Stops sit on two arcs on the near side of the Sun, so the player's first view has the Sun behind the first stop and a clear forward path. The lanes curve around the Sun rather than crossing it. The sky bake is cold blue-black with a faint dust band, matching the battle sky for sector 1. Rock kinds are stone and iron.

On arrival, the camera is high and slightly to one side. The player sees the fleet token on the first stop, the first lane split, and the sector boss's shipyard silhouette at the far edge. Only three forward stops are fully lit; the rest are silhouette icons. The Hegemony crest appears on the first military stop. Mid-sector, the route splits around a drydock and an elite stop. The drydock uses the battle shipyard gantry and work lights. The Nemesis ribbon is about a quarter full, and the Sun's disc is calm. Facing the boss, the camera drops lower, the flagship shipyard fills the foreground, and the Sun's lensed arc rises behind it. The boss lane glows in the faction's dull red.

Sector 2: Chorus and Glasswrights added

The Hollow Sun is slightly off-centre because the camera orbit has shifted. Stops sit on two interleaved arcs, so the player sees a nearer and a farther route without a flat left-to-right line. The sky bake warms to violet and magenta, with a brighter nebula band. Rock kinds are crystal and ice. Lane glow picks up cyan and violet from the new factions.

On arrival, the camera shows a gap between two stops where the next sector's red sky is faintly visible. This is the first foreshadow of sector 3. Mid-sector, a nebula pocket stop sits on a branch that is hidden behind the nearer lane until the player moves close; this is the presentation-only "unknown signal" treatment. The Nemesis ribbon is about half full, and the Sun's inner disc is brighter. Facing the boss, the flagship sits in front of a bright accretion arc, and the Glasswright stop on the flank has a hard, crystalline rim light.

Sector 3: Eclipse added

The Hollow Sun appears larger because the camera orbit is tighter and the run has come around the ring. Stops sit on three short arcs, and the boss is at the far edge, so the player sees the whole sector as a funnel. The sky bake is deep red and orange with a dark dust lane. Rock kinds are volcanic and ice. Lane glow is dark violet with Eclipse accents.

On arrival, all three sector rings are visible as faint lines around the Sun, and the Sun's disc fills more of the frame. The player sees the graveyard stop and the field repair tender on a narrow lane, which makes the scarcity of repair obvious. Mid-sector, the Nemesis clock is near full and the disc pulses slowly, so the sky itself feels urgent. Facing the boss, the flagship is silhouetted against the photon ring. The lane behind the player is visibly closed or burned, so the forward-only structure is felt, not just stated.

6. Build order

Sector sky bake and side-lit stops. Reuse world_bake.gd for one bake per sector, and world_planet.gd and world_rocks.gd for the planet and belt stops. Owner sees sector 1 with the battle sky, a side-lit planet stop, and region-correct rocks. Low fallback: same bake at half resolution, fewer stars, no cloud layer, no dust specks.

Constrained camera and arrival framing. Add orbit and zoom limits with a composed high view for arrival and a low view for boss. Owner sees a cinematic sector 1 arrival shot with the Sun behind the first stop. Low fallback: same framing, no depth-of-field, simpler anti-aliasing.

Lane redraw. Replace flat lines with directional bezier lanes, full color for known lanes, dotted whisper for the next lane. Owner sees lanes that point forward and a clear hint of the next destination without full information. Low fallback: no pulse animation, solid lines only, fewer lane segments.

Stop grounding and ring arcs. Add contact shadows and replace flat stop rings with thin orbital arcs. Owner sees stops sitting in the scene rather than floating above it. Low fallback: no contact shadow, fewer debris pieces around stops.

Label tiers and text system. Implement far, mid, and near label tiers with the existing priority rules. Owner sees clean labels at three zoom distances and no text running under the run ribbon. Low fallback: fewer labels at far zoom, no hover animation.

Hold-to-reveal tactical overlay. Add the overlay for danger, rewards, cores, and Nemesis state. Owner sees the clean map at rest and a readable tactical layer on hold. Low fallback: fewer holographic lines, no glow bloom.

Run ribbon and core strip. Remove large sidebars and add the slim bottom ribbon with the Nemesis clock and core glyphs. Owner sees the system view unobstructed and the run state always legible. Low fallback: static ribbon, no background blur, no core arcs.

Sector transition and foreshadow. Add the three-ring sector structure, the next-sector sky tint near the boss, and the pullback on boss completion. Owner sees sector 2's sky through a gap and a pullback that shows the three-sector run. Low fallback: crossfade between skies instead of a full pullback.

Final polish. Tie the Hollow Sun disc pulse to the Nemesis clock, tune faction lane colors, and check that the map and battle skies match. Owner sees a sector 3 boss shot where the sky, Sun, and UI feel like one image. Low fallback: simplified disc, no pulse, reduced particle count.

7. Not checked

The pack does not establish whether the map's Hollow Sun shader can share code with the battle dead-star shader without a new bake budget. It does not establish how the existing label priority rules behave under localisation, especially in languages with longer stop names. It does not establish whether the owner will accept a hold-to-reveal overlay rather than a permanent information layer. It does not give exact faction palettes, so the mock layouts assume the existing faction looks can be sampled and desaturated for lanes. It does not establish the map's performance budget at 20 ships' worth of scene; section 3 gives a battle budget, but the map has different costs. It does not establish whether the three-sector ring should be visible from sector 1 or only foreshadowed. It does not establish whether the run ribbon can coexist with the existing fleet, shipyard, and battle page headers without a layout change. Finally, it does not establish whether the owner wants the Nemesis clock to affect the sky, as proposed, or keep it in the UI only.
