# Issue #135 research leg: Rey (writer leg, blind)

Scope note: this leg works from the issue #135 evidence pack, the chair grounding notes, my own reads of the code at main cad86f04, and the public sources cited inline. Claims about shipped games carry a source or are labelled INFERRED. Time estimates are INFERRED throughout.

## Headline: the ranked leaps

1. Deaths and hits: replace the single raymarched fireball and the one-beat "split, flash, done" capital death with a staged, art-directed breakup sequence built on baked flipbook gas (Blender fluid sims rendered to sprite sheets) plus GPU particle sparks and venting jets, while keeping the existing geometric split and Jolt debris. Same skeleton, completely different surface and pacing.
2. Nebulas and storms: move the gas body from billboard puffs to box FogVolumes with a custom fog shader (3D noise density, the settled R82 four-stop ramp as albedo/emission), keeping a thinned layer of the current billboards only as a silhouette edge treatment. This is the one area where "use the engine's real volumetrics" is simply the answer.
3. Planets: keep the GPU bake and the 4096x2048 ruling, but replace the value-noise surface model with a feature model: domain-warped continents, ridged-multifractal mountain ranges masked to land, a coastline treatment, latitude-and-moisture biome tinting, and a real normal map derived from the height field. Resolution was never the problem; feature structure is.

## The Opus hypothesis, tested

Opus's hypothesis: our effects are mostly flat sprites and code-made textures tuned by numbers; the jumps come from using the engine's real volumetrics, baking high-quality assets offline, and one consistent art direction.

Verdict: it holds for area 2, half holds for area 3, and mostly fails as a diagnosis of area 1, while its last clause (one consistent art direction) holds everywhere and is arguably the biggest single lever in area 1.

- Area 1 (deaths): the diagnosis is wrong. Deaths are already real geometry: worker-thread clip-and-plug cuts (fx_death.gd precompute/_cut/_seal_piece), Jolt physics debris with momentum (DEBRIS_CAP 360, fx_death.gd:16), a heat-driven slag shader on wrecks that cool over 14 s (WRECK_COOL, fx_death.gd:18). The fireball is already a true volumetric, a raymarched shader (fx_shaders.gd:1626), not a flat sprite. What is actually broken, per the owner's own words, is staging (deaths read "sudden and jerky", "random explosions") and fidelity consistency (the one raymarched, photoreal-leaning element sits next to painterly ships, and only exists at the max tier). More volumetrics is not the fix; art-directed baked gas, a longer breakup chain, and tier consistency are. Offline baking (the hypothesis's second lever) does apply here, but as a replacement for the volumetric, not an addition to it.
- Area 2 (nebulas): the diagnosis is exactly right. The gas is MultiMesh billboard quads sampling one noise field. It has no volume and no internal light transport. The engine's real volumetrics (FogVolume plus custom fog shaders) are available in the same renderer we already ship, and a scene volumetric fog already exists in the battle environment (disabled only at low, battle.gd:355). This is the cleanest 50-75 percent jump in the whole job.
- Area 3 (planets): half right. The surfaces are code-made textures, yes, but they are baked at 4096x2048 on the GPU per battle, not hand-tuned sprites, and the fix is a better procedural model inside the same bake (feature structure plus derived normal data), not primarily offline assets. Offline work helps at the margins (authored detail/roughness maps per planet kind), but the leap is the surface model.
- Art direction: holds everywhere. Today the three areas sit at three different fidelities: photoreal raymarch (deaths, max tier only), soft billboard gas (nebula), flat-shaded procedural surface (planets). One shared detail-frequency and palette discipline is what makes the other two leaps read as one game.

## Area 1: ship deaths and weapon hits

### What the best shipped games actually do

Homeworld 3 (Blackbird Interactive) is the closest comparable: an RTS at a similar camera distance, and its team published their approach. In the developer post "Weapons and Unit Damage in Homeworld 3" (Tim O'Connell, Senior FX Artist, Steam news, Feb 2023), damage is built in three stages: weapon damage, ship damage, wreckage. Four damage types (Kinetic, High Explosive, Energy, Ion) each get their own visual treatment and their own impact marks. Two techniques matter for us:

- "Splat Damage": instead of decal stickers, impacts create dynamic impact points that alter the surface geometry and material properties of the hull where the shot landed, so a salvo visibly rips through armour. Persistent, located, cumulative surface damage, not a generic flash at the centre of the ship.
- A procedural "Ship Breaking" tool built in Houdini that simulates how a hull collapses under an internal explosion, models the internal rooms and mechanisms, and warps and crinkles them so they bulge outward. Wrecks are the broken geometry itself, and they persist on the battlefield (salvage). For small craft, they flag a real problem we also have in miniature: a camera-facing smoke sprite on fast fighters produced a "popcorn" effect of separate puffs instead of a stream, which is why our small-hull pooled ribbon reads as a smear at speed (INFERRED: same failure mode, same cause, discrete sprites on a fast mover).
Source: https://store.steampowered.com/news/app/1840080/view/3669906156890227103 (Steam news post for app 1840080).

EVE Online (CCP) works at the opposite scale problem: fleets of hundreds, camera from metres to hundreds of kilometres. Two documented practices transfer. First, CCP's capital wreck update (dev blog "The Art of Rubicon 1.1", eveonline.com) replaced wrecks that were "little more than darker versions of the original ships" with paper-thin empty shells by purpose-built wreck assets with real interior structure, because at inspection distance an empty shell reads as fake immediately. Second, the Viridian expansion art pass made missile impact effects distinct per damage type (thermal reads as heat, EM crackles with electricity), directional based on the angle of attack instead of randomly rotated, and spreading across the shield surface; the same pass added state-driven ship lighting, with lights dimming and flickering as a ship takes structure damage. Sources: https://www.eveonline.com/article/art-for-rubicon-1.1 and the Viridian in Focus dev blog (devtrackers.gg mirror of the eveonline.com post).

Sins of a Solar Empire 2: I found no developer breakdown of its death pipeline in the sources I could check, so this is INFERRED from its shipped presentation: at its extreme zoom-out, deaths read through a brief bright flash, an expanding debris field, and the loss of the ship's silhouette and icon, not through surface detail. The transferable lesson (INFERRED) is that at RTS distance the death is a timeline of read events (flash, break, scatter, fade), and surface detail is the least important part of it. That supports spending our budget on staging rather than on a more detailed fireball.

Battlefleet Gothic: Armada 2: likewise no primary breakdown sourced here; INFERRED from the shipped game: capital deaths are staged as a chain of secondary explosions walking along the hull before the final break, and hulks are left drifting. The chain, not any single explosion, is what makes a big ship's death feel big (INFERRED).

The Expanse (visual reference, not a game): the production's stated approach is science-first. Showrunner Naren Shankar (who holds a PhD in applied physics) describes leaning heavily on the science and wanting ships that "move like real ships" with battles that feel real, using Gravity as an early model (VFX Voice, "New Visual Vistas Propel The Expanse into Higher Orbit", Oct 2020, https://vfxvoice.com/new-visual-vistas-propel-the-expanse-into-higher-orbit/). What that produces on screen, and what the owner is asking for in R88, is a specific death grammar: no sustained flame billows; hits spark and vent; a dying ship sheds atmosphere and debris along its existing vector; wreckage keeps its momentum and tumbles slowly. Fire in that grammar is brief, local, and tied to something venting or burning inside a pressurised space, not a free-floating rolling fireball.

### The jump: staged, baked, art-directed breakup

Our current capital death is mechanically strong and visually short: lose power, vent along the tear, split into two sealed halves, flash once. The owner's words ("sudden and jerky", "random explosions") describe a pacing problem and a gas problem, not a geometry problem. The 50-75 percent jump is therefore:

1. A staged breakup timeline for capitals, roughly 2.5 to 4 seconds (INFERRED duration), replacing one flash with a legible chain that matches R88 word for word:
   - Beat 1, kill flash: one counted flash under the existing budget (FLASH_CAP 3, fx.gd:23), white-hot and small, at the killing section, not the ship centre.
   - Beat 2, venting: directional gas jets from the open sections, using baked flipbook plumes (below), plus GPU spark particles (incandescent metal, ballistic, no drag) along the tear.
   - Beat 3, structural failure: the existing worker-thread cut completes, but internal structure is now visible at the break (a small set of generic interior rib/spar meshes placed inside the seal caps, the poor-team version of Homeworld 3's modelled internals and EVE's structured wrecks), and the two halves separate slowly with relative rotation instead of popping apart.
   - Beat 4, secondary events: one or two delayed, smaller pops (below the counted-flash intensity threshold FLASH_I 4.0, fx.gd:24, so they are budget-free) as tanks or magazines go, each throwing a few more debris bodies on ballistic paths.
   - Beat 5, the long tail we already have: halves and debris drift, tumble, and cool through the slag shader's heat uniform over WRECK_COOL 14 s. Hot metal white to red to black is already implemented here; the chain finally gives it time to be seen.
   Small hulls get the same grammar compressed: flash, a short vent plume, break into debris, no lingering ribbon. The pooled smoke/fire ribbon is retired; at RTS distance and at speed it is the popcorn problem Homeworld documented.
2. Baked flipbook gas replaces the raymarched fireball as the visible gas in deaths and hits. In Blender (free), run Mantaflow smoke/fire simulations shaped for our three gas events (vent jet, breach plume, snuff-out flash ball), render each as a flipbook sprite sheet (for example 8x8 frames at 512 px per frame, INFERRED spec), and paint the colour ramp into the bake: white core, faction-neutral hot metal tones, fast falloff to transparent dark smoke. In Godot, play them on camera-facing quads or short-lived GPUParticles3D with the sheet as an animated texture, with a soft-particle style depth fade where the plume crosses the hull. What this buys over the raymarch: the gas matches the painterly ships because an artist (or a tuned sim plus a colour grade) decided its shapes; it costs a texture lookup instead of per-pixel raymarching; and it exists identically at every quality tier, ending the current oddity where the most realistic effect in the game only appears on max settings.
   The raymarch shader (fx_shaders.gd:1626) is removed from the death path. Whether it survives anywhere is owner question 1.
3. Hit language gets the EVE treatment within the R80 limits: impacts become directional (aligned to the incoming shot vector, which fx_weapons.gd already knows per shot) and typed (each weapon family gets a distinct transient: kinetic spark cone, energy arc flicker, plasma splash), layered on top of the existing wound marks. The R80 ladder, mark caps (12 per ship), and wound variants are untouched; this is the transient layer above them, which today is mostly muzzle sprites and streaks.

### How we build it here

- Flipbook production: Blender Mantaflow, one artist pass per gas event type, rendered against black with additive-friendly falloff; pack sheets with a small script; store as textures beside the fx code. Godot side: a shared flipbook material/shader with frame blending between adjacent frames (cheap, kills the slide-show read), a per-instance random rotation and flip, and a heat tint uniform so one sheet serves several factions/events. GPUParticles3D for sparks (points with a stretched billboard along velocity, additive), and for vent gas use a single quad per jet with the flipbook, scaled over its life.
- Timeline: extend fx_death.gd's death sequence into an explicit beat list per hull size class (capital, mid, small), driven off the existing precompute so the cut timing stays worker-thread based and never hitches the death frame. Interior detail: 3 to 5 generic interior chunks (rib frames, a spar, a tank sphere) instanced at the seal caps; they are also added to the debris variant pool (currently 4 variants) so breaks scatter recognisable structure.
- Weapons: in fx_weapons.gd, pass the shot direction into the impact effect spawn and select the transient by weapon family; keep muzzle behaviour, replace the generic impact sprite set.
- Tiers: max gets full beat chain, flipbooks at full frame count, sparks uncapped within reason; high gets the same chain with fewer sparks and half-rate flipbook playback; low gets flash, one plume, break, debris (no sparks beyond a token count, no interior chunks). The chain itself, which is the actual jump, survives at every tier, unlike today's max-only fireball.
- Flash discipline: beats are scheduled against the existing flash accounting in fx.gd (counted flashes only at beat 1 and optionally one beat-4 event if budget allows; everything else stays under FLASH_I or over the life limit so it does not count).

### Frame cost at 4K, 60 fps

Frame budget is 16.7 ms; the 5080 has large fill and compute headroom, and our scene is not vertex-bound. Costs, as drivers not benchmarks (INFERRED):

- Removing the raymarch removes the worst per-pixel cost in the death path: a raymarched box covering, say, 10 percent of a 4K frame at dozens of steps per pixel is millions of noise evaluations concentrated in the most visible moment of the battle (INFERRED magnitude; the shader's own comment prices it as a max-tier-only feature, which tells its own story).
- Flipbook quads cost one textured quad each with overdraw roughly equal to the plume's screen area once, not per step: two orders of magnitude cheaper than the raymarch for the same screen coverage (INFERRED). A capital death at peak might show 4 to 8 quads plus 200 to 400 spark particles; that is comfortably under 0.5 ms of GPU time on this class of card.
- The beat chain spreads work over seconds instead of one frame, and the precompute is already on a worker thread. Debris stays capped at 360 Jolt bodies, an existing, shipped cost.
Net: area 1 should end up cheaper than today at max tier, and prettier at every tier.

## Area 2: nebulas and ion storms

### What the best shipped games actually do

The honest headline: very few shipped space games put the player inside a true volumetric nebula at RTS scale, and the ones known for their nebula look mostly fake it at skybox distance. EVE Online's nebulas are famously rich, but they are environment backdrops (system skyboxes), not volumes ships fly through; CCP's documented volumetric work is local, for example the volumetric clouds called out in the Viridian expansion art pass (dev blog, 2023). That distinction matters: our problem is harder than EVE's, because our gas is a gameplay terrain the camera sits inside of, at 170 units, with ships that must stay crisp inside it (R82).

Homeworld 3 is again the closest comparable for in-volume gas: its maps are built around large 3D terrain features, including gas clouds ships manoeuvre through, rendered as volumetric media rather than billboards (INFERRED from the shipped game and its marketing of fully 3D battlefields; I did not find a public engineering breakdown of its nebula renderer, so the implementation claim is INFERRED, the visual result is observable). The observable lesson stands regardless of their exact tech: the gas has parallax against itself, ships dim progressively as they go deeper, and there is no visible quad edge or pop as the camera moves. Billboard fields cannot produce any of those three; a volume can.

Godot's own volumetric stack is documented and is the relevant "what the best tooling does" source here. Volumetric fog in Forward+ is a frustum-aligned voxel (froxel) buffer with a PBR single-scattering model (density, albedo, emission) that interacts with lights and FogVolume nodes (Godot docs, Internal rendering architecture, and the Environment class reference). FogVolumes come in box, ellipsoid, and world shapes; box and ellipsoid volumes are culled and write only into the froxels their bounds touch, while world-shape volumes write every froxel and are explicitly the expensive option (Godot engine PR 53353, which implemented FogVolumes and fog shaders, clayjohn). Custom fog shaders are compute shaders run once per froxel inside the volume's bounds, with WORLD_POSITION, UVW (for sampling a 3D density texture), SDF, and outputs DENSITY (which may be negative, for carving), ALBEDO, and EMISSION (Godot fog shader reference). Temporal reprojection smooths the froxel buffer at minimal cost, with a documented caveat: fast-moving volumes or lights ghost, so fog animation should be slow (Environment reference, volumetric_fog_temporal_reprojection notes). Every one of those facts maps onto a design decision below.

### The jump: FogVolume patches with a custom fog shader

Replace the billboard body of each gas patch with one box FogVolume per patch, running a custom fog shader:

- Density: sample a tileable 3D noise texture (baked once in Blender or generated in-engine into a Texture3D; 128 cubed is a reasonable starting point, INFERRED) at two scales through the shader's UVW/WORLD_POSITION, multiplied by the patch's existing mask so the gameplay footprint and silhouette stay exactly the ruled ones. Use the SDF output of the volume shape to feather density at the patch boundary so patches end in wisps, not in a box.
- Colour: the settled R82 design ports directly. ALBEDO carries the four-stop violet ramp (c_dark, c_shadow, c_lit, c_glow, today at world_living.gd:45-48) driven by local density, with the faction tint mixed only at the ramp ends at the same NEBULA_FACTION_END_WEIGHT 0.25 (world_living.gd:35). EMISSION carries a small fraction of the glow stop so dense cores read as faintly self-lit, the quality the current shader fakes with its energy term (world_living.gd:115).
- Light: volumetric fog scatters scene light natively, which gives us the internal light transport the billboard model cannot do at all. Keep the one optional patch light per patch. For storms, the bolt lights are currently forced to light_volumetric_fog_energy 0 (grounding notes); letting storm bolts couple into the volume at the max tier only would make a lightning strike light the storm from inside, which is the single most dramatic free effect in this area (and it is owner question 4, because it changes a deliberate current exclusion).
- Animation: drift the noise sample position very slowly (temporal reprojection ghosts on fast fog motion, per the docs), and animate storm cores by modulating emission, not by moving the volume.
- Keep a thinned billboard layer: retain the broad tying veils and perhaps one puff layer at reduced opacity as an edge and silhouette treatment on top of the volume. The froxel buffer is screen-aligned and resolution-limited; at 170 units a pure volume can read soft at patch edges. The billboards we already own are a cheap crispness insurance at exactly the place the volume is weakest, and the R82 rule (ships crisp inside gas) is easier to guarantee when the near-camera gas is partly a controlled 2D layer whose alpha we cap. Full removal is owner question 3; my pick is the hybrid.

Ships stay crisp for three reasons: gas density at ship height stays moderate (the volume gives depth through accumulation over distance, not through local thickness), the albedo ramp is dark at low density so a ship inside thin gas is dimmed, not veiled, and the retained billboard layer's ship-height band can keep the current alpha discipline.

Storms: same FogVolume path, second shader variant. Near-black albedo, emission-driven glow cores (the existing glow-core look becomes literal emission in the volume), bolt lights coupling at max tier, and the existing ribbon lightning and shield-tick flare kept as the surface language on top. The tick flare stays a shader uniform effect on ships, untouched.

### Frame cost at 4K, 60 fps

Volumetric fog cost is driven by froxel count (set by viewport resolution and the detail spread setting) and by how many froxels our volumes touch, not by patch count directly. Drivers, qualitatively (INFERRED magnitudes):

- The froxel buffer itself is a fixed scene cost we already pay at max/high (the battle environment already enables volumetric fog; battle.gd:355 only disables it at low). Adding localised box volumes adds compute only inside their bounds: a handful of terrain patches, each a few hexes across at 170 units distance, touch a modest fraction of the froxel grid.
- The custom shader's cost is two 3D texture fetches and a ramp per froxel inside volume bounds. That is cheap next to the raymarched death fireball this job removes elsewhere.
- Temporal reprojection should stay on (near-free, and our fog moves slowly by design).
Budget statement: this area is the one place I am comfortable spending real frame time, on the order of 1 to 2 ms at 4K on the 5080 for the fog pass in a gas-heavy battle (INFERRED), in exchange for the largest fidelity jump in the job. Low tier keeps today's billboard path wholesale (volumetric fog is already off there), which satisfies the R88 lower-setting requirement with zero new work.

2. Custom fog shader: two-scale density, SDF edge feather, patch mask multiply, R82 ramp as albedo, glow emission term, slow drift. Colour-match against the current shader's output in the same scene (the ramp constants port verbatim).

4. Tier wiring: max and high use volumes (high may reduce the noise to one scale), low keeps the billboard path; bolt-light fog coupling at max only, behind the owner ruling if it lands after this ships.
5. Profile a gas-heavy battle at 4K; tune volumetric detail spread and volume bounds before converting every patch type.

## Area 3: planets, especially rocky and terran

### What the best work actually does

The transferable knowledge here is technique literature more than any one game, because planet surfaces at backdrop distance are a solved problem with a known recipe, and our bake currently implements only the first step of it. The standard procedural terrain stack (Musgrave's fractal terrain work is the lineage; a recent survey pipeline, "Multi-Stage Procedural Terrain Generation Pipeline with Hydraulic Erosion", Zenodo record 19724772, states it plainly) is: fractal Brownian motion for base landmass, ridged multifractal noise for mountain ranges (1 minus abs(noise), sharpened, because plain fBm makes rounded hills, never ridges), domain warping of the noise coordinates to break up the blobby, obviously-fractal continent shapes, then an erosion pass (droplet hydraulic erosion) that carves valleys and deposits sediment, which is what makes terrain read as geology instead of noise. Layered on top: masks. Ranges are masked to where tectonics would put them, biomes are chosen by latitude, altitude, and a moisture field, and coastlines get their own treatment (shallows, beach band) because the eye goes to the coast first.

Shipped-game confirmation of the same recipe, at the level I can source: Elite Dangerous's planets are built from layered procedural noise with distinct surface materials by terrain type (widely documented by Frontier in their discovery/scanner era dev material; the specific claim I rely on is only that feature layering and material-by-slope is their approach, INFERRED in detail). Star Citizen's planet tech talks similarly describe biome distribution driven by humidity and temperature fields over a heightfield (INFERRED in detail, same caveat). The Expanse's Ilus and Mars shots make the backdrop version of the point: what reads from orbit is large feature structure (canyon systems, basin rings, polar caps), not texture grain (observable in the show; INFERRED as a production claim).

Our current bake (world_planet.gd, world_bake.gd) does step one only: FBM value noise up to 8 octaves for the surface, a 3D cell crater field, and a single relief channel. Value noise at 8 octaves is still value noise: continents are unions of blobs, there are no ridges because nothing in the function can make a ridge, there are no coasts because sea level is a threshold on a smooth field, and there are no biomes because colour is not driven by latitude or moisture. The sphere shader downstream is genuinely good (terminator, cloud shadows, ocean glint, night lights, limb haze, ring shadows), which is why planets "look a bit better" already; it is being fed a featureless surface. Doubling resolution again would do almost nothing, and the Job 10 ruling already fixed the size anyway.

### The jump: a feature model inside the same bake

Rewrite the surface function in the bake shader, same 4096x2048 output, same channel budget, new content:

1. Continents by domain-warped FBM: warp the 3D sample position by a low-frequency noise vector before evaluating the continent field. This one change kills the union-of-blobs look and produces coastlines with bays, peninsulas, and island chains. It is the highest-value single edit in this area.
2. Ranges by ridged multifractal, masked: a ridge field (1 minus abs(wvn), squared) evaluated at range scale, multiplied by a tectonic mask (a second low-frequency field, thresholded into belts) and by land mask, added into height. Ranges now run in belts across continents instead of uniform roughness everywhere.
3. Erosion look, cheap version: a full droplet erosion does not belong in a per-battle GPU bake shader. Approximate its two visible products inside the bake: valley carving by subtracting a flow-ish term derived from the ridge field's derivative (INFERRED technique: use the analytic slope of the height field to darken and depress drainage lines), and talus/sediment smoothing by blending height toward a blurred version proportional to slope. If screenshots say that is not enough, the fallback is an offline erosion pass per planet kind in Blender (droplet erosion on a heightfield is a known Blender/Python exercise, INFERRED effort), baked once and shipped as a texture set per kind, six kinds, one-time cost. My pick is shader-first (owner question 5).
4. A real normal map: derive normals from the final height field in the bake (Sobel over the equirect height, with the standard pole and seam handling: sample the height function in 3D at offset directions rather than offsetting UVs, so the seam and poles stay clean). Pack normals into the channels freed by reworking the relief channel: today relief is one channel of texture B; a tangent-space normal needs two to three. Channel plan (INFERRED, Codex to confirm against world_planet.gd): texture A keeps albedo rgb plus ocean a; texture B becomes normal xy in r/g, clouds stay in one of the remaining channels by moving city lights and lava into a packed third texture or into spare albedo-side capacity. The sphere shader then lights the surface with real normals, so ranges shade correctly at the terminator, which is where terran planets currently look flattest.
5. Biomes: colour the albedo by latitude bands warped by the moisture field (a third low-frequency noise), altitude (from height), and slope (from the normal): equatorial green-brown, arid belt tan, temperate green, tundra, polar ice; exposed rock colour on steep slopes regardless of biome; shallow-water tint in a band below sea level from the ocean mask's distance, giving coasts a shelf. Rocky/barren kinds reuse the same stack with the water and biome stages turned down and the crater field kept.
6. Detail detail: one tiling detail texture (rock grain) modulated by slope in the sphere shader, shared across kinds, breaks the last of the smooth-shaded look at close zoom. Free CC0 texture libraries (ambientCG, Poly Haven) have suitable rock detail under licences compatible with our rules (licence per asset to be checked at intake; stated as a route, not a cleared asset).

Clouds, city lights, lava, glint, limb haze, and ring shadows all stay: they read well and they are not the complaint.

### Frame cost at 4K, 60 fps

Runtime cost is nearly zero: the planet is one sphere (plus moons and rings) at backdrop distance; the new work is two extra texture fetches and normal-based lighting in a shader that already does several effects, over a small fraction of the frame's pixels. The cost moves to bake time: the surface function grows from one 8-octave FBM to roughly three noise fields plus a ridge field plus Sobel taps, so the one-time GPU bake gets slower, plausibly 2 to 4 times the current bake time (INFERRED). That is a battle-load cost, not a frame cost, and it is the right trade; if bake time becomes a loading complaint, the offline per-kind texture route in step 3 is also the bake-time fix, at the price of losing per-battle seed variety.

### Build order (Codex)

1. Domain warping on the continent field only; same seed set, before/after screenshots of three terran and two rocky planets at battle distance.
2. Ridged ranges with tectonic and land masks into height; terminator screenshots (ranges must shade as belts, not noise).
3. Height-derived normal map with 3D-offset sampling; sphere shader consumes it; relief channel retired or repurposed per the channel plan.
4. Biome and coast treatment in albedo (latitude, moisture, altitude, slope, shallows band); rocky kind pass with the same stack.
5. Slope detail texture in the sphere shader; moon pass at 1024x512 to confirm the model degrades gracefully.
6. Bake-time measurement; if over budget, move the erosion-look step (only) to the offline per-kind route.

## Shared art direction

Five rules, applying to all three areas and to the ships they sit beside.

1. One detail frequency. The ships are painterly: broad shapes, limited fine detail, detail concentrated at panel scale (at 15.4 px per unit at 4K, that means features under roughly 2 px do not exist). Every effect is authored to the same band: gas, fire, and terrain may have large-scale structure and mid-scale breakup, but no fine photographic grain. The raymarched fireball breaks this rule today (dense, high-frequency, realistic turbulence); the flipbook bakes must be graded down to the band (soft shapes, limited octaves, slightly posterised colour) or they will break it too.
2. Fire does not burn like that in space, and the owner is right to ask. In vacuum there is no convection and no ambient oxygen, so a free-floating rolling fireball with rising dark smoke is an atmospheric effect wearing a space costume. What actually happens, and what The Expanse's science-first VFX language shows (Shankar, VFX Voice, cited above), is: a breach vents gas and vapour in a jet that expands, cools, and goes out as it thins (flash and snuff, exactly as R88 words it); burning only persists where fuel and oxidiser keep meeting, which at a hull breach means briefly and directionally, along the vent; solid material glows by temperature (white to red to black, which our slag shader already does) and throws incandescent sparks that travel ballistically and cool in flight; and debris keeps its momentum and tumbles, full stop. So the rule is: flame is short, directional, and attached to a vent or a surface; glow belongs to hot solid material; nothing billows upward, because there is no up. Sustained fire is allowed in exactly one place: a pressurised interior seen through a breach, and even there it should gutter as the compartment vents.
3. Palette discipline. Effects draw from the scene's existing families: the nebula's violet ramp, faction glow colours at the ramp ends only (the R82 25 percent rule is the model), hot-metal white-orange-red for heat, and planet colours kept desaturated relative to ship paint. No effect introduces a new saturated hue family. Explosions are not orange by default; they are white-hot at the core with the colour coming from what is burning or venting.
4. Contrast and flash discipline. The counted-flash budget (3 per rolling second, fx.gd:23) is an art rule as much as a safety one: at most one bright event owns the frame at a time, everything else lives below the threshold as glow, sparks, and smoke. Deaths get their drama from duration and structure, not from brightness stacking.
5. Motion language. Everything ballistic or drifting, nothing snapping. Gas expands and thins; debris tumbles at constant rate; fog drifts slowly; planets are static backdrops with moving cloud shadows. Eased starts, long tails, no effect that appears at full size in one frame except the single counted kill flash.

## Owner questions (five, chair picks marked)

1. The raymarched fireball (fx_shaders.gd:1626): retire it completely, or keep it as a max-tier-only effect for one rare event (for example a station or reactor death)?
   Pick: retire it completely. Its realism is the mismatch the owner already flagged, and a kept exception will keep reading as a different game intruding. Cost of the alternative: one genuinely volumetric hero effect remains available for set-piece deaths, at the price of the fidelity break and of maintaining the shader for one call site.
2. Hits versus marks: keep the R80 mark system as the only persistent damage record and put all new hit work into transients (this leg's recommendation), or reopen R80 later for a Splat-style system where impacts alter hull geometry and material?
   Pick: transients now, no R80 reopen. The mark system shipped recently and reads fine; geometry-altering splats are a hull-pipeline project, not an effects project. Cost of the alternative: potentially the best hit feedback in the genre (Homeworld 3 evidence), at the price of reopening a settled ruling and touching every hull.
3. Nebula conversion: full FogVolume replacement of the billboards, or the hybrid (volume body plus a thinned billboard veil layer kept for edges and crispness)?
   Pick: hybrid. Froxel resolution at our camera distance is the known softness risk, and the veils are already built and paid for. Cost of the alternative: a cleaner, simpler system with one technique, at the price of softer patch edges and a harder crisp-ships guarantee.
4. Storm bolt lights: let them couple into the volumetric fog at the max tier (light_volumetric_fog_energy above 0), reversing the current deliberate exclusion?
   Pick: yes, max tier only. A bolt lighting its own storm from inside is the most dramatic single change available in area 2, and the tier gate protects weaker machines. Cost of the alternative: the fog stays calm and cheaper, and storm interiors stay flat-lit by their emission term alone.
5. Planet erosion: shader-only approximation inside the GPU bake (keeps per-battle seed variety, zero new assets), or an additional offline eroded texture set per planet kind (better valleys, fixed set of planets, one-time production cost, INFERRED days per kind)?
   Pick: shader-only first, offline only if the terran screenshots still read flat after steps 1 to 4 of the planet build order land. Cost of the alternative: visibly better drainage and valley structure, at the price of seed variety and a new asset pipeline for a backdrop element.
