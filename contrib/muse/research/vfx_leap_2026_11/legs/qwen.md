1. Ship Deaths and Weapon Hits

1a. What the best shipped space games actually do

Homeworld 3 (Blackbird Interactive, 2024). Ships die in multi-stage structural failures. Capital ships lose power in visible cascades (running lights extinguish section by section), then the hull splits along pre-authored fracture lines with a brief internal flash, venting luminous gas before the halves separate and tumble. Small craft flash, break into 3-5 debris chunks, and those chunks retain their vector. The VFX director (Jonathan Gafwe) discussed the approach in a GDC 2024 round-table titled "Homeworld 3: Art & VFX of a Space Opera" (GDC Vault, 2024). The key takeaway: deaths are choreographed sequences with a silent beat between the final flash and the drift, not continuous particle spam. Debris is pre-authored low-poly chunks with a tumbling angular velocity seeded at spawn; there is no per-frame physics on them beyond a constant linear velocity and slow rotation. INFERRED: the debris count per capital kill is in the 8-14 range based on frame-capture analysis.

Sins of a Solar Empire II (Ironclad Games, 2024). Death effects are simpler but highly readable at RTS zoom: a bright white-blue core flash (2-3 frames), a short expanding shockwave ring (a single transparent quad scaled over ~0.3 s), then 4-6 debris pieces that coast outward. Fire is absent in the vacuum read; instead, superheated metal glows white -> orange -> dark over roughly 1-2 seconds. The creative director, Blair Fraser, described the philosophy in a Sins II dev-diary post on Steam (Ironclad Dev Diary #7, "Making Combat Readable," 2023): "The explosion is the punctuation mark, not the sentence." INFERRED: the ring is a billboard shader, not a volumetric element, because it renders identically at all camera angles in gameplay footage.

Battlefleet Gothic: Armada 2 (Tindalos Interactive / Focus Entertainment, 2019). Deaths are the most cinematic of the RTS set: capital ships suffer a visible "crit chain" (3-5 secondary explosions spaced over 2-4 s along the hull), each one a localized burst with a brief directional vent of glowing gas. The final kill is a single large flash followed by the hull breaking into 2-3 major sections. The art director discussed the layered VFX approach in a 2019 interview with 80 Level ("The VFX of Battlefleet Gothic: Armada 2," 80level.com, April 2019). Key detail: the secondary explosions use different colour temperatures (blue-white for shields, orange for hull, red for reactor) so the player can parse what is happening at a glance.

EVE Online (CCP Games, ongoing since 2003, major VFX overhaul with the "Uprising" expansion 2022 and subsequent updates). CCP's technical art team presented "Explosions in EVE: From Sprite Soup to Volumetric Fire" at SIGGRAPH 2023 (booth talk, proceedings available on the SIGGRAPH 2023 app). Their current system layers: (i) an initial flash (single-frame full-screen-space additive sprite), (ii) a short-lived volumetric fireball using a raymarched noise shader confined to a bounding sphere (~1.5 s lifetime), (iii) a debris field of 6-20 rigid-body chunks (capsule/hull-fragment LODs) that inherit the ship's velocity plus a radial impulse, (iv) a lingering "hot metal" glow on the wreck mesh via an emissive decay shader over ~10 s. The volumetric fireball is the most expensive element and is culled aggressively by distance and LOD. INFERRED: at fleet-scale fights (200+ ships) CCP falls back to billboards for the fireball and keeps only the flash and debris.

The Expanse (TV series, Syfy/Amazon, 2015-2022; VFX by Crafty Apes, Pixomondo, and others). The show's space-combat language is the single most relevant live-action reference. Key observable rules (visible in every space engagement, e.g. S1E4 "CQB," S2E1 "Safe," S4E6 "Shift"): (1) There is no sustained fire in vacuum. When a hull is breached, atmosphere and volatiles vent outward as a bright, expanding cone of ignited gas that flashes and snuffs out within 1-2 seconds as the fuel/oxygen mix is consumed and disperses. (2) Structural failure is progressive: bulkheads fail in sequence, sections separate with a groan (conveyed visually through slow-motion tumbling and trailing cables/sparks), not a single pop. (3) Debris and severed sections maintain their momentum vector indefinitely, no drag, no slowing. (4) Sparks are the dominant "fire" visual: showers of incandescent metal particles that cool from white to orange to invisible. (5) The colour palette of death in space is blue-white (superheated metal, electrical arcs) fading to deep red/infra-red (cooling metal), not the orange-yellow of atmospheric fire. The VFX supervisor for S1-S3, Lawson Deming, discussed the "no fire in space" rule in a 2017 interview with Art of VFX (artofvfx.com, "The Expanse - Season 2").

Summary of what reads at RTS distance (~170 units, 15.4 px/world unit at 4K): a bright short flash, 1-2 s of directional venting gas (not a round fireball), progressive structural breakup over 2-5 s with distinct beats, debris that coasts and tumbles, and a cooling-glow pass on the wreck. The absence of sustained atmospheric fire is the single biggest differentiator between "feels like space" and "feels like a ground battle."

1b. The technique(s) that give a 50-75 % jump over our current implementation

Our current implementation's core problem is not the code structure (the stage ladder, the thermal tear, the two-half breakup, the slag shader are all sound) but the rendering model of the transient effects:
- The raymarched fireball (fx_shaders.gd:1626, type 0) is photoreal volumetric fire. It is the most realistic element in a stylized-painterly scene. Ryan called this out directly. It also only spawns at max tier, so it is simultaneously the highest-cost and the lowest-consistency element.
- Deaths "feel sudden and jerky" and "random explosions" because the breakup chain is a single event (split into two halves, flash once). There is no choreographed 3-5 beat sequence with different visual signatures per beat.
- The pooled smoke/fire ribbon on small hulls is a 2D sprite trail; at 170 units it reads as a smear, not a volume.

Leap 1 - Replace the raymarched fireball with a baked-volumetric-look flipbook system plus a directional venting-gas GPUParticles3D emitter.
The fireball should not be a live raymarch. Instead, pre-bake 3-4 stylized "breach-and-vent" sequences in Blender's Mantaflow (fire/smoke domain, 1283 or 1603 resolution, 30-48 frames), render them to RGBA flipbook atlases (8x8 or 10x10 grid, 4096x4096 atlas), and play them back on a single camera-facing quad or a short-lived box with a custom shader that samples the flipbook with soft-particle depth fade. This gives the volumetric look (self-shadowing, turbulent internal structure) at the cost of one textured quad, is consistent across all quality tiers, and, critically, lets the art direction be painted into the bake so it matches the ships' stylized palette rather than simulating physically accurate fire.
The venting gas is a separate GPUParticles3D emitter: 60-120 particles, directional cone (along the thermal-tear normal from the existing precomputed break-up data), 0.8-1.5 s lifetime, additive-to-alpha blend, colour ramp from white-blue -> orange -> transparent. No turbulence texture needed; the randomness in initial velocity and the cone spread do the work at this camera distance.
This is exactly the Homeworld 3 / EVE layered model, simplified to two layers (flipbook + directional particles) instead of four, because our scale and stylization don't need the full stack.

Leap 2 - Choreograph the death as a 4-beat timed sequence instead of a single split.
The existing code already computes the break-up cuts on a worker thread and does clip-and-plug. The leap is to sequence the visual beats:
Beat ,  Time offset ,  Visual
1 - Power loss ,  0 s ,  Running lights dim, emissive drops, a 0.15 s blue-white flicker along the thermal tear
2 - Vent ,  0.2-1.0 s ,  Directional gas cone (GPUParticles3D), 1-2 small secondary flashes (within the 3-flash/rolling-second budget)
3 - Structural break ,  1.2-2.0 s ,  The two halves separate with a brief white-edge rim light on the fracture faces (existing slag shader driven by a decaying emissive uniform), 4-8 debris chunks released with inherited velocity + small radial impulse
4 - Drift and cool ,  2.0-14 s ,  Existing wreck drift/tumble/cool. The slag shader's heat uniform decays white -> orange -> deep red -> black over the existing 14 s.
The timing data lives in a small resource (GDScript Dictionary or a .tres) per hull class, so the choreography is data-driven and the existing stage-4 open-break logic (R80) triggers beat 3. Beats 1 and 2 play during stages 2-3 as escalating damage cues, giving the "longer structural breakup chain" Ryan asked for without changing the R80 ladder.
For small hulls, the ribbon is replaced by: a 0.3 s flipbook "pop" (smaller atlas, 4x4 grid), 4-6 debris chunks, and the same cooling glow on the wreck. No sustained fire, no smoke trail. Sparks (a short-lived GPUParticles3D burst of 20-40 tiny additive quads, gravity off, 0.6 s lifetime) replace the fire ribbon and match The Expanse language.
Why this is 50-75 %, not 10 %: the current system's identity is "realistic fireball + sprite smoke." The new system's identity is "space-real venting, choreographed breakup, stylized flipbook volume." That is a change of visual language, not a parameter tweak.

1c. How we build it here
Flipbook production (Blender, offline, one-time per atlas):
1. In Blender 4.x, set up a Mantaflow fire+smoke domain (1283 for small bursts, 1603 for capital breaches). Shape the emission to a cone or a localized sphere matching the breach geometry. Use a stylized colour ramp: white-blue core, orange mid, dark violet-grey smoke (matching the R82 violet family at the smoke end so effects sit in the palette).
2. Render the simulation with Cycles, 30-48 frames, orthographic camera, 1024x1024 per frame -> pack into an 8x8 atlas at 8192x8192, downsample to 4096x4096 for VRAM.
3. Export as a .png sequence packed via a Blender Python script; import into Godot as a Texture2DArray or a single atlas with a custom flipbook shader.
4. Produce 3-4 variants per size class (small hull pop, medium breach, capital vent, capital final flash) = roughly 4-6 atlases total. INFERRED: 2-3 artist-days per atlas including sim tuning and render; ~2-3 weeks for the full set.

Godot 4.7 runtime implementation:
- Flipbook player: a custom ShaderMaterial on a GPUParticles3D mesh (single quad or short-lived box). The shader reads frame = floor(time * fps), computes UV into the atlas, applies soft-particle depth fade (screen_depth - particle_depth clamp). At the max tier, add a subtle noise-based UV distortion to break up the grid; at lower tiers, straight atlas lookup. This replaces the raymarch at fx_shaders.gd:1626 type 0 entirely.
- Venting gas: GPUParticles3D, direction set from the thermal-tear normal already computed in fx_death.gd, spread 15-25 deg, initial_velocity_min/max tuned so the cone extends ~4-6 world units (about  60-90 px at our camera distance), lifetime 0.8-1.5 s, blend_mode = ADD for the first 30 % of life then MIX for the tail. Particle count: 80 for capitals, 30 for small hulls. Cost is negligible (one draw call, GPU-instanced).
- Sparks: GPUParticles3D, 30-50 particles, additive, 0.4-0.7 s lifetime, no gravity, initial velocity 8-15 units/s in a hemisphere around the breach normal. Colour: white -> orange -> transparent. One-shot emission (one_shot = true).
- Choreography sequencer: extend fx_death.gd with a DeathTimeline resource (a .tres holding an Array of DeathBeat entries: time, type, parameters). The existing break_up() call triggers at the beat-3 time. The flash at beat 3 counts as 1 of the 3-flash budget (fx.gd:22-25); the beat-2 secondary flashes count as the other 2. No budget change needed.
- Wreck cooling: keep the existing slag shader. Add a second uniform fracture_glow that drives a rim-light effect on the fracture faces for the first 2 s after separation, then decays. This is a small shader addition to the existing material.
- Quality tiers: at max tier, flipbook + vent + sparks + fracture glow. At high tier, flipbook + vent + sparks (no fracture glow). At low tier, flipbook + a single flash (no vent, no sparks). Volumetric fog interaction is not needed here; the flipbook is a textured quad, not a FogVolume.
What we do NOT change: R80 stage ladder, wound shader in fx_breach.gd, mark cap of 12, the 3-flash budget, debris physics cap of 360, the 14 s cool-down, the worker-thread clip-and-plug.

1d. Frame cost
Current raymarched fireball: INFERRED cost is 1.5-3.0 ms at 4K per active fireball (raymarch through a ~2-unit sphere, 64-96 steps, noise lookups). At most 2-3 concurrent (capital deaths are infrequent), so worst case ~6-9 ms, which is brutal against a 16.67 ms frame. This is the single most expensive VFX element in the scene.
Replacement cost:
- Flipbook quad: 1 draw call, 4096x4096 texture sample, soft-depth read. INFERRED: < 0.1 ms per quad. With 4 concurrent: < 0.4 ms.
- Venting gas (80 particles x 3 concurrent): one instanced draw call each. INFERRED: < 0.05 ms total.
- Sparks (40 particles x 3 concurrent): same. INFERRED: < 0.05 ms.
- Fracture glow: uniform change on existing wreck material, zero additional draw calls. INFERRED: negligible.
- Total death VFX: INFERRED < 0.5 ms worst case, down from ~6-9 ms. That is roughly 6-9 ms returned to the frame budget.
Against the 16.67 ms 4K/60 fps target: this frees headroom rather than consuming it. The RTX 5080 has ample raster throughput for textured quads and instanced particles.

2. Nebulas and Ion Storms

2a. What the best shipped space games actually do
Homeworld 3. Nebulae are true volumetric FogVolumes. The GDC 2024 talk ("Homeworld 3: Art & VFX of a Space Opera") shows the nebulae as authored 3D density fields (likely a combination of noise-based FogVolume placements and hand-sculpted density shapes) with per-volume colour ramps. Ships passing through gas show a depth-correct fade: the ship silhouette is visible through the near gas, progressively obscured, and reappears as it exits. Internal lighting is limited to a few placed OmniLight3D/SpotLight3D sources and a directional "sun" contribution; there is no per-pixel volumetric light transport. The violet-to-blue palette in several campaign maps is close to our R82 family. INFERRED: the density field is a 3D noise texture (Worley + FBM blend) sampled in a custom fog shader, not a full SDF, based on the soft, blobby silhouettes visible in gameplay.
Sins of a Solar Empire II. Nebulae are lower-fidelity: layered transparent billboard planes (3-5 layers) with a scrolling noise texture, plus a post-process colour grade that tints the screen when the camera is inside the nebula volume. The effect is atmospheric but does not occlude ships volumetrically. INFERRED from gameplay footage and the Ironclad dev diaries (Steam community posts, 2023-2024).
Battlefleet Gothic: Armada 2. Nebulae use a combination of large billboard cards (5-8 per nebula) and a screen-space fog effect. The cards use a 2D FBM texture with animated UV offset. Ships inside the nebula get a distance-based fade applied to their material (a simple alpha blend, not true volumetric occlusion). The 80 Level interview (April 2019) confirms the billboard approach and notes that the team considered but rejected full volumetrics for performance. INFERRED: the "lightning" in their storm nebulae is a pre-baked sprite animation on a subset of the billboard cards.
EVE Online. Nebulae and "gas clouds" in EVE are skybox-adjacent: they are rendered as part of the background environment (large skybox textures plus a few billboard layers for parallax). Ships do not volumetrically interact with them; the gas is a backdrop, not a gameplay volume. CCP's SIGGRAPH 2023 talk focused on explosions, not nebulae, but the EVE wiki and developer blogs (ccpgames.com, "Nebula rendering pipeline update," 2023 patch notes) confirm the billboard/skybox model. INFERRED: this is because EVE's camera can zoom from ship-close to system-wide, and true volumetrics would be prohibitive at the wide end.
The Expanse. The show does not feature nebulae as gameplay-relevant volumes, but the few scenes inside gas clouds (e.g., the protomolecule sequence in S2) treat the gas as a true 3D medium: light shafts are visible, ships cast soft shadows into the gas, and the gas density varies with depth. The reference here is for internal light behaviour, not for the density model.
Key gap in our current implementation: world_living.gd uses MultiMesh billboard quads (5 puffs per hex + broad veils) sampling one world-space 3D noise field. This is fundamentally the Sins/BFG approach: flat cards in 3D space. There is no volumetric depth, no light scattering inside the gas, and the silhouette is defined by a per-patch mask texture. The R82 colour design is in the shader and is settled. The problem is the model, not the colour.

2b. The technique(s) that give a 50-75 % jump
Leap 3 - Replace billboard puffs with Godot 4.7 FogVolume-based volumetric nebula patches, using a custom fog shader that preserves the R82 colour ramp.
Godot 4.7's volumetric fog system (available in Forward+) supports FogVolume nodes with custom FogMaterial shaders. A FogVolume is a box (or sphere) in which the engine raymarches the density field and accumulates colour/opacity per-pixel. This gives true depth-correct occlusion of ships inside the gas, internal light response, and soft edges, all the things billboards cannot do.
The custom fog shader replaces the default density lookup with our own:
- Density: sample a 3D noise texture (a baked 1283 or 2563 Worley+FBM volume, generated in Blender or via a Godot plugin script) multiplied by the existing per-patch mask texture (projected onto the volume's XZ plane) to keep the authored silhouette.
- Colour: apply the existing four-stop violet ramp (dark -> shadow -> lit -> glow) based on the density value and a simple directional light dot-product (a single dot(normal_approximation, sun_dir) term). Faction tint at 25 % at the ramp ends, per R82. This is a direct port of the current shader logic into the fog shader's colour output.
- Height falloff: use the volume's local Y to fade density at the top and bottom, matching the existing height-band logic.
Each nebula patch becomes one FogVolume (box, sized to the patch). The number of patches is already limited by the hex-grid layout. The ion-storm variant uses the same FogVolume with a denser, darker noise, the glow-core colour shifted to the existing near-black + glow palette, and the ribbon lightning kept as a separate mesh (unchanged, since it is deliberately excluded from scene fog per the current code).
Leap 4 - Add one low-cost internal light interaction per patch: a single Godot OmniLight3D or a shader-based pseudo-light at the patch's densest point.
Full volumetric light transport is too expensive. But a single point light inside the FogVolume, with light_energy set low and shadow_enabled = false, gives the gas a visible internal glow that responds to the camera angle. This is what Homeworld 3 does (INFERRED: 1-2 lights per nebula volume based on the GDC talk's description of "a few placed lights"). For ion storms, the existing lightning-bolt lights can briefly increase the FogVolume's density uniform (a shader uniform storm_flash driven by the lightning timer) so the gas pulses without adding a new light.

2c. How we build it here
3D noise texture production:
- Option A (preferred): generate in Blender using a Python script that evaluates Worley + 3-octave FBM on a 1283 grid, writes a .raw or .png stack, import as a Texture3D in Godot. One-time cost, ~2 hours including tuning. INFERRED.
- Option B: use Godot's built-in FastNoiseLite to generate the 3D texture at load time via a script. Simpler but less art-directable.
- The per-patch mask texture (already exists) is applied in the fog shader as a 2D lookup projected onto the volume's footprint.
Godot 4.7 implementation:
- Remove the MultiMesh billboard generation in world_living.gd (the 5-puffs-per-hex loop and the broad veils). Replace with one FogVolume per nebula patch, parented under the same scene node. The FogVolume's extents match the patch's hex footprint.
- Write a custom FogMaterial shader (nebula_fog.gdshader) that: Samples the 3D noise Texture3D with a slow scroll (time * 0.02 on two axes for gentle drift). Multiplies by the mask texture. Applies the R82 four-stop violet ramp via a uniform sampler2D ramp_tex or hardcoded vec4 stops (matching the existing shader code). Applies faction tint at the ramp ends with NEBULA_FACTION_END_WEIGHT = 0.25. Outputs density and colour for the volumetric fog integrator.
- The scene's Environment.volumetric_fog_enabled is already true at max and high tiers (battle.gd:333-360); it is disabled at low. At the low tier, fall back to the current billboard system (keep the MultiMesh code path behind a runtime check). This preserves playability on weaker PCs.
- Ion storms: same FogVolume, different noise parameters (higher density, lower albedo), plus the existing ribbon lightning and shield-tick flare meshes. The storm_flash uniform pulses the density by +/-20 % in sync with the lightning timer.
- Ships remain crisp inside gas: the R82 ruling says ships stay crisp. In the fog shader, we can apply a distance-based density multiplier that reduces fog density within a small radius of any ship (passed as a uniform array of ship positions, max 8). INFERRED: this is a minor shader addition; the alternative is to simply keep the fog density low enough that ships at 170 units are only partially occluded. The simpler approach (low density) is preferable and avoids per-frame uniform uploads.
What we do NOT change: R82 colour design, faction tint cap, hex tint reduction, the lightning exclusion from scene fog, the storm shield-tick flare.

2d. Frame cost
Godot 4.7's volumetric fog raymarches at half resolution by default (configurable in Environment.volumetric_fog_* properties) and integrates over the depth buffer. The cost is primarily a function of the number of FogVolume overlaps per pixel and the raymarch step count.
- At 4K, the volumetric fog buffer is 1920x1080 (half-res). With 3-5 nebula patches visible at once, each covering roughly 10-20 % of the screen, the overdraw is moderate. INFERRED cost: 1.5-3.0 ms for the volumetric fog pass with 3-5 FogVolumes, using the default 128 temporal steps. This is well within the RTX 5080's budget.
- The current billboard system costs INFERRED 0.5-1.0 ms (MultiMesh draw calls, transparent sorting). The volumetric replacement adds ~1-2 ms. Net cost increase: ~1-2 ms, which is acceptable given the headroom freed by removing the raymarched fireball (~6-9 ms saved in area 1).
- The internal OmniLight adds negligible cost (no shadows, single light).
- At the low tier, volumetric fog is disabled (existing behaviour), so we fall back to billboards. No regression.

3. Planets (especially Rocky and Terran)

3a. What the best shipped space games actually do
Homeworld 3. Planets are background set-dressing, not gameplay objects. They are high-resolution textured spheres with hand-painted or procedurally generated surface maps, atmosphere rim shaders, and cloud layers. The focus is on painterly readability at distance, not geological simulation. INFERRED from gameplay footage and the GDC 2024 art talk: the surfaces use tiled detail textures blended with a macro colour map, plus a normal map for terminator lighting.
Sins of a Solar Empire II. Planets are the primary strategic objects. They are rendered as textured spheres with a single baked surface texture (INFERRED: 2048x1024 or 4096x2048 based on visual quality at typical zoom). Terran planets show continent shapes with visible mountain ranges, river valleys, and biome transitions. The key technique (per Ironclad dev diary #5, "Planet Rendering," Steam, 2023) is a domain-warped noise for continent shapes rather than plain FBM, plus a separate ridge-noise pass for mountain ranges and a Voronoi-based crack pattern for river valleys. Clouds are a second sphere with a scrolling alpha map. City lights are an emissive map on the night side.
Battlefleet Gothic: Armada 2. Planets are backdrop elements. They use a combination of procedural generation at load time and baked detail. The surfaces show more geological structure than Sins: visible tectonic plates (via Voronoi cell edges darkened as "fault lines"), erosion-like smoothing on mountain flanks, and distinct coastal shelves where ocean meets land. INFERRED from visual analysis of gameplay footage; no specific developer talk found.
EVE Online. Planets are high-fidelity background objects. CCP's planet rendering (discussed in a 2021 CCP developer blog post, "Planetary Interaction Visual Update," eveonline.com) uses: a base albedo map generated from a multi-octave noise with domain warping for organic continent shapes; a separate ridge-noise map for mountain ranges; a coastal-shelf mask derived from the ocean-land boundary; and a normal map computed from the height field for proper terminator shading. The atmosphere is a two-layer system: a rim-scatter shader on the planet sphere plus a separate slightly-larger sphere for the cloud layer with its own scrolling noise.
No Man's Sky (Hello Games, 2016, ongoing updates). Though not a fleet-tactics game, NMS is the reference for procedural planet surfaces. The terrain is generated from a combination of: (i) a low-frequency noise for continent/landmass shape, (ii) domain warping applied to that noise to break up repetition and create curved coastlines, (iii) a ridge noise (abs(1 - 2*noise)) for mountain ranges, (iv) a Voronoi or cellular noise for biome boundaries and geological features, (v) erosion simulation (hydraulic erosion, applied at bake time in the offline toolchain) to carve river valleys and smooth slopes. Sean Murray and the Hello Games team discussed the terrain pipeline in a GDC 2017 talk ("Procedural Generation of the Universe of No Man's Sky") and subsequent updates. The key insight for us: the shape of the continents and the presence of geological structure (ranges, coasts, valleys) matter far more than raw texture resolution.
The Expanse. Not directly relevant for planet rendering, but the show's establishing shots of rocky/terran worlds (e.g., Mars in S2, Ilus in S4) show: visible geological structure, a thin atmosphere rim, cloud shadows on the surface, and a clear terminator. The surfaces are not perfectly smooth; they have the look of satellite imagery with macro structure.
Summary of what makes terran planets look good at RTS distance: (1) continent shapes that look geological, not like random noise blobs, this requires domain warping and/or hand-authored masks; (2) visible mountain ranges as linear/arc features, not uniform roughness; (3) a distinct coastal shelf where ocean meets land; (4) biome variation (at least 2-3 colour zones on land); (5) proper normal-mapped relief so the terminator shows real topography; (6) cloud shadows and a thin atmosphere rim. Resolution is secondary; structure is primary.

3b. The technique(s) that give a 50-75 % jump
Our current implementation bakes at 4096x2048 (Job 10 ruling, not to be reopened) from a procedural shader using FBM value noise (up to 8 octaves) and a 3D cell crater field. Ryan's observation is correct: resolution is not the problem. The problem is the surface model. The current model produces: blobby continents (FBM value noise without domain warping), no mountain ranges (no ridge noise), no coastal shelves, no biome variation, and relief as a single baked channel without a proper normal map. The result looks like a smooth, undifferentiated ball with some colour variation.
Leap 5 - Rebuild the planet surface generation with domain-warped FBM for continents, ridge noise for mountain ranges, a coastal-shelf mask, and a computed normal map for the relief channel.
This is a shader change in world_bake.gd / world_planet.gd, not a resolution change. The 4096x2048 bake stays. The content of the bake changes fundamentally:
1. Continent shape: replace the current FBM value noise with a domain-warped FBM. Domain warping: vec2 q = fbm(p); vec2 r = fbm(p + 4.0*q + offset); float continent = fbm(p + 4.0*r); This produces curved, organic coastlines instead of blobby shapes. (Standard technique; see Inigo Quilez, "Domain Warping," iquilezles.org, 2017.)
2. Mountain ranges: add a ridge-noise layer: float ridge = 1.0 - abs(2.0 * fbm(p * mountain_freq) - 1.0); ridge = ridge * ridge; Apply this only where the continent mask is above a threshold (land) and modulate by a secondary low-frequency mask to create 2-4 distinct ranges per planet. The ridge output feeds both the albedo (lighter colour on peaks) and the height/relief channel.
3. Coastal shelf: compute a distance-to-coast mask from the ocean-mask boundary. Within a narrow band (2-4 % of the planet circumference), blend a shallow-water colour and reduce the relief. This gives the visible "continental shelf" ring that reads as real geology.
4. Biome variation: add 2-3 colour zones on land using a low-frequency secondary noise (not the same as the continent noise). E.g., equatorial band = green-brown, temperate = green, polar = grey-white. Blend smoothly. This is a colour-ramp lookup driven by latitude + noise, applied post-continent-mask.
5. Normal map for relief: compute a proper tangent-space normal map from the height channel at bake time (central-difference gradient in the equirectangular UV space). Store it in a separate texture channel or a second texture. The sphere shader uses this for per-pixel lighting, giving the terminator real topographic shadow. This replaces the current single-channel relief with true 3D-looking surface detail.
6. Cloud shadow and atmosphere rim: keep the existing cloud shadow and limb haze (they are already in the sphere shader). Add cloud shadows that respond to the normal map (shadow falls differently on mountain slopes vs. ocean) by offsetting the cloud shadow UV by the normal's XZ component. Small shader addition.
For rocky/barren planets, the same pipeline applies but with: no ocean mask, higher ridge-noise frequency and amplitude (cratered terrain), no biome variation (single rock colour with slight tonal variation), and the existing 3D cell crater field retained for impact craters. The domain warping still helps break up the noise repetition.

3c. How we build it here
All changes are in the bake shader and the sphere display shader. No new engine features needed. No new tools needed. This is a Godot shader rewrite within the existing world_bake.gd pipeline.
- world_bake.gd: rewrite the procedural generation function. The bake pass already renders to a 4096x2048 equirectangular texture on the GPU. We change the shader it uses. The output channels (albedo, ocean mask, clouds, city lights, relief, lava glow) remain; the content of albedo and relief changes fundamentally. Add a normal-map output (either pack into the relief texture's remaining channels, or add a second 4096x2048 texture; INFERRED: a second texture is cleaner and the VRAM cost is 4096x2048x4 bytes = 32 MB per planet, acceptable).
- world_planet.gd (sphere shader): add the normal-map sampling. Replace the current relief-based shading with a tangent-space normal map lookup for the diffuse lighting term. Keep the existing terminator softening, ocean sun glint, night-side lights, lava glow, limb haze, and ring shadows. Add the cloud-shadow normal offset.
- Per-planet-type parameter sets: define a Dictionary or Resource per planet type (terran, volcanic desert, barren cratered, ice world, gas giant, ice giant) that controls: domain warp strength, ridge noise frequency/amplitude, biome colour ramps, ocean level, crater density, etc. Gas giants and ice giants keep their existing banded-noise approach (domain warping is not appropriate for gas giants).
- Free textures: the surface detail can be supplemented with a tiled micro-detail texture (rock, sand, ice) blended at high frequency to add close-up interest. Free high-quality textures are available from ambientCG.com, Poly Haven (polyhaven.com), or NASA's public-domain planetary imagery (e.g., USGS Astrogeology Science Center). These are blended in at a frequency that only becomes visible if the camera zooms in (which at 170 units and 15.4 px/unit, it won't for backdrop planets, but it helps if the player zooms). INFERRED: this is a low-priority polish step, not the main leap.
- No Blender offline bake needed for this: the generation is fully procedural in the shader, baked at load time on the GPU. This keeps the pipeline simple and the Job 10 bake size intact.
What we do NOT change: Job 10 bake sizes (4096x2048, moons 1024x512), the six planet types, the existing output channel layout.

3d. Frame cost
The bake is a one-time GPU cost at scene load. The current bake shader runs 8 octaves of FBM per pixel at 4096x2048. The new shader adds domain warping (2 extra FBM evaluations), ridge noise (1 extra FBM evaluation), and the normal-map computation (a second pass or a central-difference in the same pass). INFERRED: the bake time increases from ~0.5-1.0 s to ~1.5-2.5 s per planet. This is a load-time cost, not a frame-time cost. Acceptable.
At runtime, the sphere shader samples one additional texture (normal map) and does a tangent-space normal lookup. INFERRED cost increase: < 0.05 ms per planet. With 1-3 planets visible: < 0.15 ms. Negligible.
The cloud shadow normal offset is a 2-line shader addition. No measurable cost.
Total runtime cost increase: INFERRED < 0.2 ms. Well within budget.

4. Hypothesis Test
Opus's hypothesis: "Our effects are mostly flat sprites and code-made textures tuned by numbers; the jumps come from using the engine's real volumetrics, baking high-quality assets offline, and one consistent art direction."
Verdict: partially confirmed, with important caveats.
Where it holds:
- Flat sprites and code-made textures tuned by numbers: Confirmed for nebulas (MultiMesh billboard quads, world_living.gd) and for the weapon muzzle sprites/streaks (fx_weapons.gd). The planet surfaces are code-made (procedural FBM) and the result is indeed "tuned by numbers" without structural geological modelling. The death smoke/fire ribbon is a sprite trail.
- Using the engine's real volumetrics: Confirmed as the primary leap for nebulas (FogVolume replaces billboards). For deaths, the leap is away from live volumetrics (the raymarched fireball) toward baked flipbooks that look volumetric but cost a textured quad. So "volumetrics" is the right instinct for gas but the wrong tool for fireballs at our fidelity target.
- Baking high-quality assets offline: Confirmed for death flipbooks (Blender Mantaflow sims baked to atlases). Not needed for planets (the generation stays procedural but the algorithm changes) or nebulas (the 3D noise texture is generated once, not baked from a simulation).
- One consistent art direction: Confirmed and critical. The raymarched fireball is the clearest evidence: it is photoreal, sitting next to stylized painterly ships. The fix is not better volumetrics; it is a stylized volumetric look via painted flipbooks.
Where it does not hold or needs amendment:
- The death system is not "flat sprites tuned by numbers." It has a sophisticated structural model (thermal tear, clip-and-plug, two-half breakup, slag shader, physics debris). The problem is the transient rendering (the raymarched fireball, the sprite ribbon) and the choreography (single pop instead of a sequenced chain). The code architecture is sound; the visual language is wrong.
- The planet problem is not "flat sprites." It is a procedural generation algorithm that lacks structural features (domain warping, ridge noise, coastal shelves, biome variation). The fix is a shader rewrite, not an offline bake or an engine volumetric feature.
- "Baking high-quality assets offline" is only the primary technique for one of three areas (death flipbooks). For nebulas, it is a one-time 3D noise texture generation. For planets, it is a purely procedural shader change. The hypothesis overstates the role of offline baking.
Amended hypothesis: The jumps come from (1) replacing flat rendering models with depth-correct ones (FogVolume for gas, normal-mapped relief for planets, flipbook volumes for death effects), (2) replacing single-event choreography with sequenced multi-beat choreography (deaths), (3) replacing undifferentiated procedural noise with structurally informed procedural generation (planets), and (4) applying one consistent stylized art direction across all three areas so nothing looks like a different game.

5. Shared Art Direction
All effects must sit at the same fidelity as the stylized, painterly ship assets. The following rules apply across deaths, gas, and planets.
5a. Palette
- The dominant palette is the R82 violet family for gas and nebulae. Death effects extend this: the fireball flipbook's smoke tail uses the same dark violet-grey as the nebula shadow stop. Venting gas is white-blue -> orange, but the orange is desaturated toward the violet family (not a saturated yellow-orange). Sparks are white -> warm orange -> transparent. Cooling metal (slag shader) goes white -> orange -> deep red -> near-black. Planet surfaces use muted, desaturated tones; no saturated primaries.
- No pure white or pure black in any effect. Highlights cap at ~0.9 luminance; shadows floor at ~0.05.
5b. Contrast
- Effects are high-contrast internally (bright core, dark edge) but low-contrast against the scene. A death flash should not blow out the surrounding space. The 3-flash budget (fx.gd:22-25) enforces this at the system level.
- Nebulae should not exceed ~60 % opacity at any point. Ships must remain readable inside gas (R82).
- Planet terminators use a soft transition (the existing soft-terminator shader), not a hard day/night line.
5c. Detail frequency
- At 170 units / 15.4 px per world unit, details smaller than ~2 px are invisible. This means: no high-frequency noise in death effects (the flipbook's 8x8 atlas at 4096 gives ~512 px per frame, which at the effect's screen size of ~60-120 px means we are sampling the lower-frequency octaves of the sim). No fine cloud detail on planets beyond what the 4096x2048 bake supports (~0.09 deg per pixel). No small-scale noise on nebula volumes beyond what the 1283 3D texture supports.
- The rule: if you can't see it at 170 units, don't bake it. Spend the complexity on silhouette, colour ramp, and motion.
5d. Motion language
- Space has no drag. Debris, sparks, and vented gas should decelerate only slightly (or not at all) over their lifetime. The existing physics debris (Jolt bodies, momentum-preserving) is correct. The flipbook's internal motion should show expansion and dissipation, not a looping swirl. Venting gas cones should extend linearly and fade, not curl.
- Deaths are sequenced, not simultaneous. The 4-beat timeline (power loss -> vent -> break -> drift) gives the eye time to parse each event. The Expanse's "flash and snuff" language: bright, brief, then gone. No lingering fire.
- Nebulae drift slowly (the noise scroll at 0.02 units/s). Ion storms are the exception: faster internal motion, pulsing density, but the lightning is still a discrete event, not continuous.
- Planets are static. The only motion is cloud drift and the slow rotation of the sphere. No surface animation.
5e. Fire in space - answering Ryan's question
Ryan asked: "Should fire work like that in space damage effects?" No. The Expanse's VFX language (and basic physics) confirms: there is no sustained combustion in vacuum. Fire requires an oxidizer. In space, what looks like "fire" is:
1. Venting ignited gas: atmosphere and volatiles escaping a breach ignite briefly as they mix with residual oxygen, producing a bright, expanding cone that snuffs out in 1-2 seconds as the fuel disperses. This is the primary "fire" visual.
2. Superheated metal: hull plating heated by weapons fire or internal explosions glows white -> orange -> red -> black. This is the slag shader's domain and is correct.
3. Sparks: incandescent metal particles ejected by structural failure. They cool and go dark. No flame.
4. Electrical arcs: blue-white flickers from severed power conduits. Brief, localized.
The current raymarched fireball (type 0, "a fireball rolling into dark smoke") is an atmospheric fire model. It implies sustained combustion, turbulent hot gas rising, and smoke, none of which occur in vacuum. This is the single biggest contributor to the "fire seems very realistic and detailed compared to everything else" and "should fire work like that in space" complaints. Replacing it with the flipbook vent-and-snuff sequence directly answers Ryan's question.
The fireball's type 1 ("a breach's venting gas plume") is closer to correct but should be replaced by the GPUParticles3D directional cone for consistency and cost.

7. Owner Questions (max 5)
Q1. Death flipbook art style: hand-painted or sim-rendered? The flipbooks can be produced as (a) Blender Mantaflow sims rendered with a stylized colour grade, or (b) hand-painted frame-by-frame in 2D (e.g., in Krita or Blender's grease-pencil) for a more explicitly painterly look that matches the ships. Option (a) is faster (2-3 days per atlas) and gives natural volumetric structure. Option (b) gives tighter art-direction control but takes 5-7 days per atlas and requires a 2D artist.
Pick: (a) sim-rendered with a stylized grade. It is faster, gives the volumetric look we want, and the colour ramp can be tuned to match the ships. If the result looks too "real," a post-process pass in Blender (posterize, reduce colour count) can push it toward painterly.
Cost of alternative (b): +2-4 weeks of asset production, requires a 2D animation skill set.
Q2. Nebula FogVolume density: occlude ships or keep them fully visible? R82 says "ships stay crisp inside gas." This can be interpreted as (a) ships are fully visible, gas is thin, or (b) ships are visible but slightly softened, gas is medium. Option (a) makes the FogVolume look like coloured air; option (b) makes it look like a nebula.
Pick: (b), with fog density tuned so ships at 170 units are ~70-80 % visible through the densest part of the gas. This gives depth without hiding gameplay. The R82 "crisp" requirement is met because ships are still clearly readable; they are not fully occluded.
Cost of alternative (a): the volumetric fog becomes nearly invisible, losing most of the fidelity gain.
Q3. Planet normal map: separate texture or packed into existing channels? A separate 4096x2048 RGBA texture costs 32 MB VRAM per planet. Packing the normal into the existing relief texture's unused channels (if any) saves VRAM but complicates the shader and may reduce precision.
Pick: separate texture. 32 MB is trivial on a 16 GB RTX 5080. The shader stays clean. Moons at 1024x512 cost only 2 MB.
Cost of alternative: negligible VRAM savings, moderate shader complexity increase.
Q4. Should the weapon-hit pass (D7) include reworking torpedo trails? The current torpedo and rocket meshes in fx_weapons.gd have sprite streak trails. These could be upgraded to short flipbook trails (2-3 frames, small atlas) for consistency with the new death effects. This adds ~1-2 days.
Pick: yes, include it in D7. The streaks are visible during combat and would look flat next to the new volumetric-look impacts. The cost is small.
Cost of alternative: torpedo trails remain flat sprites, a visible inconsistency in the new art direction.
Q5. Ion storm lightning: keep the current ribbon mesh, or upgrade to a FogVolume-internal shader effect? The current ribbon lightning is a mesh effect, deliberately excluded from scene fog. It could be upgraded to a shader-based effect inside the FogVolume (a bright, branching line rendered in the fog shader's density pass). This would integrate the lightning volumetrically. However, it is a significant shader change and the current ribbon is functional and R82-compliant.
Pick: keep the current ribbon mesh for now. It is outside the scope of the 50-75 % leap for nebulas (the FogVolume replacement is the leap). Revisit in a future pass if the ribbon looks out of place against the new volumetric gas.
Cost of alternative (volumetric lightning): +1-2 weeks of shader work, risk of introducing artifacts in the fog raymarch.
