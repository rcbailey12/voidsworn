# Ship pipeline research: how shipped fleet games build ships, how to clean AI meshes, and what to do with our 36 hulls

## Scope, counts, and method

This report answers pack section 7, questions Q1 to Q7, and gives the recommendation pack section 8 requires.

Priced scope, from pack section 3.1: 36 hull meshes in total. That is 30 faction capital hulls (Hegemony 5, Crusade 6, Hullbreakers 5, Chorus 5, Glasswrights 5, Eclipse 4), plus 3 squadron craft (fighter S1_51, bomber S2_23, spore B23_51), plus 3 objective structures (station O1_37, transport O2_37, beacon O3_11). The "28 hulls" figure in the issue text is a known error and is not used anywhere in this report.

Target defects, from pack section 2: (a) waviness, low frequency surface undulation, (b) bumps, high frequency surface noise, (c) grid like texture, a regular panel or projection pattern, (d) odd paint, projected concept art carrying its own lighting and layout onto the hull.

Constraints taken as given, from pack section 6: Godot 4.7, PC only, Forward+ renderer, tactical camera about 170 units, typical battle about 20 ships total, owner judges at 4K on an RTX 5080, FAC3D stays the single colour source, free tools only, no naval features, Chorus ships are living ships and must read as flesh, and any geometry change must not break the R73 break up work or the separate damage surface work.

Source rule used here: every claim about a named game or a named tool carries a URL or a named source in the same paragraph. Anything without a public source is marked INFERRED in the sentence that makes it.

The honest headline on sources: only one of the five named games has published a real ship art pipeline in detail, Sins of a Solar Empire 2. For the other three 3D games the public record covers design intent, fidelity targets and team shape, not modelling steps, UV strategy or bake settings. This report says so per game rather than filling the gap with invented pipeline detail.

## Part 1: The five games, Q1 to Q4 per game

### 1.1 Battlefleet Gothic: Armada (Tindalos Interactive, Unreal Engine 4)

**Public documentation status: no modelling, texturing or bake pipeline is publicly documented.** No GDC talk, postmortem or asset breakdown describing how Tindalos models, unwraps or textures its ships was found. What is documented is the fidelity brief the art had to meet.

Q1 Modelling. The ships are digital recreations of the physical tabletop miniatures, kept as close to the source models as the real time format allowed. Game director Romain Clavier: "We tried to stay as faithful as possible to the original material without making too many concessions for a real time adaptation" (Game Developer, "Maxing out miniatures: Making a successful tabletop adaptation", https://www.gamedeveloper.com/design/maxing-out-miniatures-making-a-successful-tabletop-adaptation). Pre release coverage makes the same point: "All of the in game models will be true to those created for the table top, with Gothic cathedral like architecture to the ships of the Imperium" (TheSixthAxis, https://www.thesixthaxis.com/2015/01/26/table-top-to-real-time-the-strategies-of-battlefleet-gothic-armada/). INFERRED: a recreation brief of that kind means each class is a unique hero build modelled from miniature and artwork reference, not a modular kit assembled per class, because a kit would drift from the specific miniature each class has to match. INFERRED: what is shared across a faction is the architectural language itself, for example Imperial cathedral detail, armoured prows and broadside batteries, rather than shared meshes. That sharing by language, not by parts, is visible in the shipped art and is not documented as a production method.

Q2 Texturing. Not publicly documented. INFERRED from the miniature source: paint reads as a master miniature paint job scaled up, with faction colour blocking, metallic gold and bone detail on Imperial ships, and heavy weathering, because that is what fidelity to a painted miniature requires at game scale. No source supports a claim that Tindalos used trim sheets, tileable PBR sets or decals, and this report makes no such claim as fact.

Q3 Detail at distance. This is the one place Armada is a useful counter example for us. Game Developer notes the ships are "rendered in obsessive detail on Unreal Engine 4" and that "players can zoom way down and geek out on the fine details of these splendid, ridiculous space cathedrals" (https://www.gamedeveloper.com/design/maxing-out-miniatures-making-a-successful-tabletop-adaptation). Armada deliberately spends on close zoom detail because its camera allows close inspection of a small number of large ships and its whole appeal is the miniature made huge. INFERRED: that spend does not transfer to our case. Our typical battle is about 20 ships at a tactical camera near 170 units, per pack section 6, so fine greeble that only reads at extreme close zoom is a poor place to put fleet wide hours for us.

Q4 Fleet economics. No per ship cost or ship art team size is published. Game Developer frames Tindalos as a small Paris indie studio whose team was largely made of tabletop fans ("our studio is filled with miniatures", same source above). INFERRED: consistency across a large roster was affordable there because the designs already existed as miniatures and codices. The design cost had been paid by Games Workshop years earlier. A project generating its own designs, as ours does, cannot copy that saving.

### 1.2 Homeworld 3 (Blackbird Interactive, Unreal Engine)

**Public documentation status: design and readability process documented in interview, modelling and texturing pipeline not documented.**

Q1 Modelling. Ships are designed by iterative shape work the studio calls shape tuning. Art director Karl Gryc describes redesigning the Hiigaran Carrier as "finding a rough shape for a spacecraft and then rapidly iterating on it, adjusting engine locations, tweaking different points, and going back and forth to find what works" (Windows Central interview with Karl Gryc, https://www.windowscentral.com/gaming/pc-gaming/homeworld-3-art-director-discusses-redesigning-spacecraft-for-a-pc-rts). The fleet coherence test is visual and explicit in the same interview: a finished ship should sit next to the Mothership so that "you get it, they're part of the same fleet, they belong". INFERRED: that is a silhouette and shape language process run per hull by artists, not a modular section system and not a kitbash library, because the interview describes shaping whole hulls and never mentions shared parts, sections or kits. Asset reuse is documented, but at whole ship level: when the first Carrier design was replaced, the original design was reused as a pirate ship because "assets can and should be reused when possible" given limited time (same source).

Q2 Texturing. No UV, trim sheet or material workflow is published. What is documented is graphic identity applied on top of shape: Gryc calls it "pageantry", meaning "the striping pattern on ships", with class coded striping, for example two stripes for a Torpedo class and a single stripe for an Assault Frigate, "just to define its role on the battlefield" (same source). That is a decal and paint scheme system doing identification work, sourced. INFERRED: large, simple, high contrast paint shapes carry faction and class identity in Homeworld 3, while fine surface texture carries little of the read. That matches the series history of bold banding and block colour drawn from 1970s science fiction cover art (World Designer Notebook, "Strips, Stripes and Supergraphics", https://worlddesignernotebook.substack.com/p/strips-stripes-and-supergraphics).

Q3 Detail at distance. This is the strongest sourced lesson in this section. Readability is treated as a design constraint against extreme scale range: "The Mothership is 3 kilometers in length, while a mere fighter is 20 meters long", and the design had to come "without jeopardizing readability in the heat of battle" (Windows Central, same source). The tools named for that read are shape, striping (above), and distinct weapon effects so the player can tell "what kind of ship is attacking at a glance" (same source). Nothing in the interview credits normal map detail, panel lines or surface texture with the tactical read. INFERRED for our fleet: at our tactical camera the equivalents are silhouette, FAC3D value blocking, engine and weapon glow, and one or two large paint shapes per hull. Micro panel detail is close zoom garnish only.

Q4 Fleet economics. No per ship cost or art team size is published. The documented economics are reuse and consolidation: reusing the superseded Carrier as another ship (above), and merging two resource units into one unit to cut production and management load (same source). INFERRED: even a studio of Blackbird's size protects fleet scope by reusing whole designs and cutting unit count, not by making each hull cheaper to detail. That supports a tiered approach for us, in Part 4.

### 1.3 Sins of a Solar Empire 2 (Ironclad Games and Stardock)

**Public documentation status: good. This is the only named game with a published, step by step ship modelling and baking account, plus artist portfolios showing the same high poly, low poly, UV and PBR steps.**

Q1 Modelling. Ships are hand modelled hard surface, built as clean quad topology from the start, not kitbashed and not generated. Primary 3D artist Paul Kiesling (Set), in Sins II Dev Journal 21, "Baking with Set": "the most important part here is having a model with good topology in quads", with topology that is "globe like following the silhouette of the ship in smooth sweeping curves", mostly quads, minimal triangles, n-gons discouraged (https://stardock.atlassian.net/wiki/spaces/SSEFW/pages/2162065455/Sins+II+Dev+Journal+21+-+Baking+with+Set). The high poly is made from that same model: holding edges are selected, a chamfer or bevel modifier creates a 45 degree face on those edges, and the mesh is subdivided, in his case in ZBrush, whose "subdivision algorithm seems to play better with most models" (same source). Portfolio evidence matches the journal: an outsource artist on Sins II lists his contribution per ship as "High poly modeling, Low poly modeling, UV layout, PBR texturing (where applicable)", with concept art and final integration by Stardock and Ironclad (Martin Danlos, ArtStation, https://martindanlos.artstation.com/projects/AZ8DRX), and a separate technical breakdown showing high poly, low poly baked mesh with AO and normal, and low poly wireframe (https://martindanlos.artstation.com/projects/0lGmg8). INFERRED: per hull uniqueness is high, each ship gets its own high and low pair, and consistency comes from shared process, shared bevel size ("I tend to apply the same sized bevel to all edges for consistencies sake", Dev Journal 21) and shared texturing practice, not from shared geometry.

Q2 Texturing. Unique unwrap per ship, baked maps, then procedural and hand work in Substance Painter. Dev Journal 21 specifies: UV padding and margin rules tied to mip levels (at 1k, margin 4 and padding 8; at 2k, 8 and 16; at 4k, 16 and 16), UV islands straightened and oriented horizontally or vertically to avoid bake aliasing, and 70 to 80 percent minimum use of the 0 to 1 UV space, with stacked islands pushing some ships past 100 percent (same source). Mesh normals for the bake use weighted normals, with the rule "not all seams have to be sharps, but all sharps have be seams" (same source). The bake itself is done in Marmoset Toolbag at 4k by 4k, 64 samples, 16 bit, MikkT tangent space, baking normal, curvature and AO maps, because in his judgement Marmoset's results are better than Substance Painter's baker (same source). Texturing then happens in Substance Painter with those baked maps loaded into the normal, curvature and AO slots (same source). Separately, the Sins ship shader tradition documented for modders is a metalness and roughness PBR workflow with team colour carried in a texture channel and separate emissive and bloom data (ModDB, "For Modders: Overview of Ship Shader", https://www.moddb.com/tutorials/for-modders-overview-of-ship-shader-v100). That modder document describes the earlier Remastered shader format, so it is cited here for the team colour principle only, not as the Sins II internal format. Panel lines and edge wear, in this pipeline, therefore come from three sourced places: modelled bevels caught by the bake, curvature driven wear in Substance Painter, and AO in crevices. None of them come from a projected painting.

Q3 Detail at distance. Two sourced points matter for us. First, the whole bevel and bake effort exists to fake density at normal viewing distance: a carefully bevelled high poly gives "edge highlights" so the low poly has "the appearance of having a higher tri count than it actually does" (Dev Journal 21). Second, mip discipline is treated as part of art quality, not an afterthought: padding exists because "capital ships and large structures will un-render before hitting a MIP level where padding/margin bleed between islands become troublesome", and loose normal banding from badly softened low polys causes visible artefacts when texture resolution drops with distance (same source). INFERRED: Sins II does not spend on geometry the camera cannot resolve. Triangles are deliberately placed only where the silhouette or the bake needs them: "reduce your models tri count as much as possible... Sins will definitely reward you for being smart about where you chose to distribute them" (same source).

Q4 Fleet economics. Dev Journal 21 is written by the team's "primary 3D artist", singular, which is sourced evidence of a very small core ship art function, supplemented by outsource artists doing full high, low, UV and PBR passes per ship (ArtStation source above). For scale of the wider studio tradition, Stardock's own summary of the earlier Sins: Rebellion postmortem reports development "with a small team over 13 months" and "Stardock's staffing peaked at 11 developers" (https://www.stardock.com/games/article/433554/gamasutra-sins-of-a-solar-empire-rebellion-postmortem). No per ship day count is published for Sins II. INFERRED: a hand modelled high and low pair with a unique unwrap and a Substance Painter pass, of the kind documented, is specialist work measured in days per capital ship, not hours. That inference is the reason Part 4 does not recommend copying the Sins II route hull for hull across 30 capitals on a one person project. What is worth copying is its order of operations and its bake discipline, which are free.

### 1.4 Battlestar Galactica Deadlock (Black Lab Games, published by Slitherine)

**Public documentation status: no art pipeline is publicly documented.** No developer diary, talk or breakdown describing modelling, UVs or texturing for Deadlock's ships was found. The dev diaries that exist cover updates, modes and balance, for example Dev Diary 28 on the Daybreak update noting only that the team made "improvements to ship textures to make them look better", with no method given (Slitherine forum, https://forum.slitherine.com/viewtopic.php?p=880318&sid=691af820db38d46fd32244f7b880d25e).

Q1 Modelling. Ships are original hard surface designs built to a television licence, plus faithful recreations of the known ships. Black Lab and Slitherine told Polygon they worked with NBC Universal and the show's original team, that known ships such as Galactica, Vipers and Raptors were required, and that a strategy game needed many invented classes on top: "We needed lots of different ships like frigates, a destroyer, a cruiser, that sort of thing", with every new design approved by NBC Universal (Polygon, https://www.polygon.com/2017/6/28/15887180/battlestar-galactica-deadlock-ps4-pc-xbox-one-price-release-date). INFERRED: each class is a unique model built from show reference and concept art, sharing the Colonial language of armoured hull plating, flight pods and greebled trench detail, and the Cylon language of organic curves and double disc forms, rather than sharing a parts kit. Nothing public supports a modular or kitbash claim for this game.

Q2 Texturing. Not publicly documented. The only sourced direction is tonal: every ship and weapon was to be "grounded in the series gritty, tactile, realistic style" (Polygon, same source). INFERRED: that means desaturated military paint, visible plating and wear, and no bright faction colour blocking of the Homeworld kind, which matches the shipped look. Any claim about trim sheets, unique unwraps or decals for Deadlock would be invented, so none is made.

Q3 Detail at distance. Not documented as an art method. INFERRED from the game's structure, which is sourced in outline: fleets are small, battles are turn based with a cinematic replay the player can orbit freely (Polygon, same source). A replay camera that lingers close on a few ships justifies more per hull surface detail than a 20 ship real time tactical view does. That is the same trade Armada makes, and the opposite of our camera. Deadlock is therefore weak evidence for our detail budget and this report does not lean on it for Q3.

Q4 Fleet economics. The credits are the best sourced evidence of team shape: 57 people in total on the Windows version, with a single credited 3D Artist (Mark Smith), an Art Director, a Concept Artist, and "Additional 3D Assets" supplied by an external studio, Forge Studios, with four named artists (MobyGames credits, https://www.mobygames.com/game/95476/battlestar-galactica-deadlock/credits/windows/). INFERRED: a small team ships a full two faction roster by keeping one in house 3D artist on the core fleet and buying overflow capacity outside, and by designing new classes inside a strict licence language so no hull needs its own visual research programme. No per ship cost is published.

### 1.5 Starsector (Fractal Softworks, 2D)

**This game is 2D sprite art. It has no 3D modelling, UV or bake pipeline, and nothing in its production method transfers directly to our hulls. Its value here is contrast: it is the clearest published example of hand authored reads built for a tactical camera, by a two person team of one programmer and one artist.**

Q1 Modelling equivalent. Ships are hand painted top down sprites by artist David Baumgart. The modding guide that Fractal Softworks links to states the house rule plainly: "Starsector sprites are tiny paintings, not pixel art... you want to be using primarily painting techniques and only doing pixel art to bring out fine details... NO BLACK OUTLINES" (Starsector wiki, Intro to Modding, https://starsector.fandom.com/wiki/Intro_to_Modding). The same guide stresses matching "pixel mass", the count of non transparent pixels, to the existing ships in the same size class, so a new ship occupies the same visual weight as its peers (same source). Stations show the one modular idea in the game, and it is a gameplay module, not an art kit: a station is "an immobile, rotating base ship with smaller ships (as modules) attached at various points. These modules have their own sprites" (David Baumgart, "Zen and the Art of Battlestation Construction", Fractal Softworks blog, 2 March 2018, mirrored at https://github.com/jnxyp/starsector-translation/blob/HEAD/Blog%20Translation/20180302%20-%20Zen%20and%20the%20Art%20of%20Battlestation%20Construction/20180302-chs&eng.md).

Q2 Texturing equivalent. There is no texturing step separate from painting. Light, panel detail, wear and faction colour are all painted into the single sprite by the artist, under one consistent top down light. That is sourced in effect by the painting rule above. What transfers to us is the principle, not the technique: one authored light logic and one palette discipline across the whole fleet, which in our pipeline is exactly what FAC3D plus a single procedural paint solve is supposed to guarantee, and what projected concept paint breaks, because each concept painting brings its own light.

Q3 Detail at distance. This is where Starsector earns its place in the study. Preview coverage notes the design goal directly: ships remain distinct when fully zoomed out and still hold up when zoomed in, and "the way they look also conveys their role... more bulky ships are great at tanking damage and holding cargo, while the smaller ones are more speedy glass cannons" (3rd Strike preview, https://3rd-strike.com/starsector-preview/). INFERRED: because a sprite cannot rely on lighting, normals or parallax, every bit of the tactical read has to be in silhouette, value contrast and colour blocking at the sprite's native size. Starsector proves a fleet can read well with zero surface micro detail if those three are strong. That is a direct argument for spending our limited hours on silhouette fairness and value blocking before panel detail.

Q4 Fleet economics. The team is one developer and one artist for the whole game (the artist's own account of the Starsector credits split is quoted in the Hugging Face discussion at https://huggingface.co/Severian-Void/Starsector-Portraits/discussions/6, and the two person structure is consistent across Fractal Softworks' public material). INFERRED: a single artist can hold an entire fleet's consistency only because the asset unit is small, the view is fixed, and the style rules are few and strict (painting not pixel art, no black outlines, matched pixel mass). The transferable lesson is the strictness, not the headcount: a short list of fleet wide rules, enforced on every hull, is what keeps 36 hulls coherent when there is no art department.

### 1.6 What the five games agree on

1. No named game in this study builds fleet ships by projecting concept paintings onto generated meshes. Where the method is documented (Sins II) the albedo is built in a PBR texturing step after a bake, from baked normal, curvature and AO maps. Where it is not documented (Armada, Deadlock, Homeworld 3) the documented brief is fidelity to an existing physical or screen reference, which is also an authored surface, not a projected one.
2. Where readability is discussed at all (Homeworld 3, Starsector, and in mip terms Sins II) it is carried by shape, silhouette, large paint shapes and effects, never by fine texture detail.
3. Where team size is visible (Sins II, Deadlock, Starsector) ship art is one or two core people plus overflow help. Consistency comes from shared process and shared rules, not from hero effort per hull.
4. Close zoom surface richness (Armada, Deadlock) is a deliberate spend tied to cameras that linger on a few ships. It is not evidence that a 20 ship tactical camera needs it.

## Part 2: Q5, the standard cleanup route from an AI or photogrammetry mesh to a clean game asset

AI generation and photogrammetry fail in the same ways for the same reason: both produce a dense, irregular, non manifold surface with no designed topology, no designed UVs, and colour that has light baked into it. Published scan to asset workflows describe the raw output as "messy, non-manifold, extremely high-poly; requires extensive retopology" and prescribe the same hybrid order: capture or generate the high detail source, retopologise a clean low poly over it, then bake the detail from high to low as normal, AO and diffuse or material maps (88Cars3D, "Photogrammetry vs Manual Modeling", https://88cars3d.com/2026/04/02/photogrammetry-vs-manual-modeling-choosing-the-best-method-for-vehicle-assets/). AI mesh guides say the same in shorter form: generated meshes have "irregular topology, triangles of varying sizes, no edge loops, and no clean geometry flow", and need retopology before they are game assets, with Instant Meshes, Blender Remesh and ZBrush ZRemesher named as the automatic options and manual retopology as the high quality, high cost option (AIMagicX guide, https://www.aimagicx.com/blog/ai-image-to-3d-model-guide-2026). Recent Blender tooling built specifically for generated and scanned meshes runs the identical sequence as a single pipeline: analyse for non manifold edges, holes, floaters and degenerate faces, fix transforms and holes, retopologise, with a fallback chain of Quadriflow first, then Voxel plus Quadriflow when Quadriflow rejects the raw mesh, then Decimate as the always works last resort (StraySpark, MeshPrep devlog, https://www.strayspark.studio/blog/introducing-meshprep-blender-ai-scan-mesh-cleanup). TRELLIS and Hunyuan3D exports are named in that devlog as sources its analysis step recognises, which is our generator family.

The steps below are in the order a small team runs them. Tools named are free unless marked paid, because pack section 6 limits us to free tools. Paid tools are named only where a shipped game used them, as evidence.

**Step 0. Freeze the source and keep it as reference only.** Keep the raw AI mesh untouched in a separate collection or file. Every later mesh is a new object derived from it. Reason: once you smooth or remesh in place you have lost the only record of the approved silhouette, and our owner approves silhouette first (pack section 3.2, step 2).

**Step 1. Repair.** Apply transforms, merge by distance, delete loose geometry and floating shells, fill small holes, recalculate normals, make the mesh manifold. This is the Fix stage in the MeshPrep sequence above, and it is a precondition, not an optional tidy up: Blender's Quadriflow remesher needs a closed manifold surface and rejects typical generator output, which is why the voxel fallback exists (same source).

**Step 2. Voxel remesh to a watertight volume.** Blender's voxel remesher "uses an OpenVDB to generate a new manifold mesh from the current geometry" and "produces a mesh with perfectly even distributed topology" with no internal self intersecting geometry (Blender Manual, Remesh, https://docs.blender.org/manual/es/3.0/sculpt_paint/sculpting/tool_settings/remesh.html). The setting that matters is voxel size: it is "the resolution or the amount of detail the remeshed mesh will have", set interactively with Shift+R (same source). Voxel remesh is the correct first remesh for damaged geometry, and the manual is explicit that the Quad remesher "doesn't clean up intersecting geometry" and is "not a replacement for the Voxel remesher" (Blender Manual, Remeshing, https://docs.blender.org/manual/sl/latest/modeling/meshes/retopology.html).

Critical limit, stated plainly: voxel remesh at a small voxel size preserves waviness and bumps. It rebuilds topology, it does not fair a surface. Choosing a voxel size is therefore a filtering decision. A voxel size near the wavelength of the high frequency noise removes that noise and also removes real small detail. Low frequency waviness survives any voxel size fine enough to keep the ship's features. Waviness needs Step 3.

**Step 3. Fair the form. This is the step that removes waviness, and it is the step most cleanup guides skip.** Three ways, in rising cost:
- (a) Sculpt mode smoothing over the voxel remesh with a large Smooth brush at low strength, working along the hull's long axis, then a Shrinkwrap back toward the source only where the silhouette must be held. Free, in Blender.
- (b) Section fairing: take cross sections along the hull, smooth the section curves, and loft a new surface through them. This is our own existing SHELL approach in pack section 3.2 step 5, where a clean shell is lofted from sections measured off a wavy AI hull and the AI mesh is demoted to a measuring guide. It is the most reliable fix for a badly waved bow, which pack section 5 records as a known recurring AI failure.
- (c) Full manual remodel of the primary hull surface over the source as reference, the Sins II pattern, where low and high are aligned by hand until "the geometry is where it needs to be" (Dev Journal 21). Highest cost, best result.
Skipping Step 3 and baking the raw AI mesh is the single most common cause of a clean looking retopo that still renders wavy, because the bake faithfully transfers the unfair surface.

**Step 4. Retopology to the game mesh.** Automatic first, manual only where it fails. Free options in Blender: Quadriflow, which "uses the Quadriflow algorithm, which can produce better results but is also slower" and offers Preserve Sharp, Preserve Mesh Boundary and symmetry options (Blender Manual, Remeshing, above); and Instant Meshes, a free automatic retopology tool that creates clean quad meshes from irregular sources (AIMagicX guide, above). Paid options named for completeness, used by shipped teams and not available under our free tools rule: ZBrush ZRemesher, described as the industry standard automatic retopology for organic models (same guide), and Quad Remesher. For a rigid ship that does not deform, retopology quality matters less than for a character: there is no animation edge flow to protect. What matters is even quad distribution, sharp edges held where panels break, and a silhouette that matches the faired form. Sins II's standard is the right target: mostly quads following the silhouette in sweeping curves, minimal triangles (Dev Journal 21). Set a per class triangle target before this step, not after. Our current hulls_hi tier already ships at 20k to 38k triangles per pack section 3.2, and pack section 6 says desktop detail is compressed with meshoptimizer, not decimated, with no artificial triangle limits, so the target is a quality target, not a savings target.

**Step 5. UVs designed for the texturing route, not inherited from the generator.** Two workable strategies, and they must be chosen before texturing:
- Unique unwrap per hull, the Sins II route: straightened islands, consistent texel density, padding matched to texture size and mip behaviour, 70 to 80 percent space use minimum (Dev Journal 21). Best close zoom quality, highest per hull time, and the only strategy that supports unique wear per hull.
- Trim sheet mapping: UVs are placed onto strips of a shared sheet that catalogues an edge, a panel, a vent, a plated band, so many assets share one texture and "the detail is never modelled separately: it is placed wherever it is needed" (CGI Academy Hub, "Trim Sheets and Modular Assets: A Game Art Guide", https://cgiacademyhub.com/blog/trim-sheets-assets-modulares). The same guide stresses that the strip is designed before modelling, because re laying UVs later is "the most expensive rework in this workflow" (same source). Trim sheets are also documented in vehicle and prop practice as the way to texture repeating mechanical parts such as grilles and panels without unwrapping each part uniquely (JustPaste summary of hard surface trim practice, https://justpaste.it/e43ay/pdf).
For ships, the practical answer is a hybrid, detailed in Part 4: large smooth hull areas on a shared tileable or trim material, with a small number of unique regions and decals for identity. What is not workable is the current fallback in pack section 3.3: runtime box projection giving "about three panels per hull length" (ships3d.js:753), because a regular projection is itself a repeating pattern. See Q6(c).

**Step 6. Build a deliberate detail source, then bake high to low.** Do not bake the raw AI mesh. Bake one of two deliberate sources: (i) the faired, subdivided version of your own clean mesh with bevelled holding edges, the Sins II method, or (ii) the clean mesh plus a separate detail pass of modelled plates, fittings and panel breaks. Bake normal, AO and curvature. The principle is ray projection of the high surface onto the low: "ray-project the high-poly geometry onto the low-poly surface, record the surface direction per pixel, and store it as a normal map", with the warning that "a normal map can only fake surface relief, it can't change the silhouette, so the low-poly's outline has to be right" before baking (BlueCat 3D, Module 06 Texture Baking, https://3d.bluecatbot.com/en/pipeline/06.html). The same source lists the working set: normal to fake relief, AO for self shadowed cavities, curvature for edge convexity that drives wear (same source). Marmoset Toolbag is the baker Sins II recommends and the one used in published mech baking breakdowns, with cage offset and skew painted in the viewport and AO search distance tuned per asset (Marmoset, "Baking and Presenting A Mech in Toolbag", https://marmoset.co/posts/baking-and-presenting-a-mech-in-toolbag/?fbclid=IwAR04zOUcElhnk2fxkF6suabMroldkIYTtGm99rhctTOEZVCI8-wCUD0WhAY). Marmoset and Substance Painter are paid, so under our free tools rule the bake runs in Blender itself, which Dev Journal 21 names as a valid baker ("Blender is the obvious option") while preferring Marmoset. Blender baking is slower to art direct and has weaker cage painting, which is a real cost of the free tools rule and is priced into Route B in Part 4 as extra iteration time.

**Step 7. Texture from masks, not from a picture of the ship.** Base colour comes from the faction palette, in our case FAC3D only. Variation comes from the baked masks: curvature drives edge wear, AO drives grime in recesses, a material ID or vertex mask separates paint, bare metal, dark machinery and glow. This is the standard RGB mask pattern in published trim practice: masks created in the texturing tool select areas, tiling materials are layered through them, and the main surface colour, edges and grime can be recoloured without a unique albedo per asset (Experience Points, Nathan Alderson, "The Rusty Speeder", https://www.exp-points.com/nathan-alderson-the-rusty-speeder). Decals go on last, as separate small planes or a second UV channel, for markings, numbers, hazard stripes and faction insignia. Homeworld 3's class striping is the design model for what decals should do (Part 1.2).

**Step 8. Pack, compress, gate.** Export, compress with meshoptimizer into the existing .vsm path where a hull uses it, convert through godot/render/build_assets.gd as today, then run the existing blob sweep gate (the 9 cell region review in pack section 5) before the hull is shown. Add two cheap automated checks the current chain lacks: a flat lit turntable render at tactical distance and at close zoom, and a normals only render. Waviness and bump noise are far easier to see in a normals only or single light render than in a painted beauty shot.

**Where teams cut corners when the camera is tactical, not close up.** Sourced and INFERRED points, marked per point:
- They bake at the resolution the furthest common view needs and enforce padding for mips, rather than maximising texture size (sourced, Sins II, Dev Journal 21).
- They put triangles into silhouette and skip interior and underside detail the camera rarely sees (sourced in principle, Sins II on deliberate triangle placement, Dev Journal 21). INFERRED for our top down leaning tactical camera: undersides and deep engine recesses are the lowest value surface on every hull.
- They reuse one material set and a handful of decals across a faction instead of authoring unique wear per hull (sourced for trim practice generally, CGI Academy Hub above). INFERRED: unique wear is the first thing to cut, because at 170 units no player can tell which hull a scratch belongs to.
- They do not hand retopologise rigid hulls. Automatic retopology is documented as sufficient for background and non deforming assets, with manual work reserved for hero and deforming assets (AIMagicX guide, above). Ships do not deform, except under our R73 break up work, which cuts the hull rather than bending it.

## Part 3: Q6, the four defects, cause and the step that removes the cause

### (a) Waviness, low frequency undulation

Cause: the generator builds form from multi view images with no fairness constraint, so long surfaces drift in and out over distances of a tenth to a third of the hull length. It is a geometry error in the primary surface, not a texture error and not a normals error. Pack section 5 records it as a recurrence: R61 logged a wavy effect on the V1b bow on Oct 27 and the Nov 2 complaint repeats it after the Nov 1 repaint of all 36 hulls (G-14). A repaint cannot fix a shape error, and it did not.

Step that removes the cause: Step 3, fairing, in Part 2. Either sculpt smoothing along the hull axis over a voxel remesh, or section lofting that replaces the primary surface with a surface built from smoothed sections. Voxel remesh alone (Step 2) does not remove it. A normal map cannot remove it, because the silhouette and the shading both follow the low poly surface, and "baking can't rescue a crooked silhouette" (BlueCat 3D, Module 06, above). Smoothing the normals or adding a stronger normal map only disguises it at some angles and makes it worse at grazing angles.

Test that proves it is gone: render the untextured hull in a single hard side light and in silhouette against a plain background, bow, midship and stern, which are already three of the blob sweep views. If the long edges ripple, it is still there.

### (b) Bumps, high frequency surface noise

Cause: two stacked sources. First, the generator's surface itself is noisy at small scale. Second, and more damaging, that noise is baked into a normal map and into a procedural grain layer, so it is re applied as if it were designed detail. Pack section 3.2 step 7 describes the current finish as procedural paint with "a fine normal map (dents, pitting, scratches)" and TEXTURE_PASS in pack section 5 states that today "every bit of surface grain is procedural noise baked into the paint plus a light shader layer". Noise applied uniformly, with no relationship to panels, edges or use, reads as bumpiness and as dirt, not as detail.

Step that removes the cause: change the bake source. In Step 6, bake from a faired and bevelled version of the clean mesh or from modelled detail parts, never from the raw AI mesh, so the noise is not in the normal map in the first place. Then replace uniform procedural grain with mask driven detail: grain only inside panel boundaries, wear only on curvature convex edges, grime only in AO recesses. Curvature and AO exist in the standard bake set precisely to drive wear and dirt placement (BlueCat 3D, Module 06, above; Sins II Dev Journal 21, where curvature and AO are the maps carried into texturing). Keep a very fine micro roughness variation if cinematic close zoom needs it, at a scale well below panel size and at low contrast, and check it at tactical distance where it should disappear entirely. If it is visible at tactical distance it is too strong.

Test: normals only render at close zoom, and a roughness only view. Designed detail has edges and direction. Noise has neither.

### (c) Grid like texture

Cause: three candidates, and the pack names the strongest one. decodeAIHull generates UVs in code as a box projection at "about three panels per hull length" (pack section 3.3, ships3d.js:753). A box projection puts the same panel scale on every face, aligns panel breaks to world axes rather than to the hull's own structure, and repeats at a regular interval, which is precisely what a grid read is. Second candidate: a tileable plate texture repeated at a fixed scale with no rotation, scale or offset variation. Third candidate, historical: pack section 5, B-16 records that a damaged painted hull once drew as "generic plating, a plain panel grid", so a fallback material has produced this exact read before.

Step that removes the cause: replace generated UVs with authored UVs (Step 5). Either a unique unwrap whose panel breaks follow the hull's structure, or trim mapping where panels are placed deliberately along edges and bands. Then break regularity in the material: two or three panel scales mixed by mask, occasional rotation, decal and fitting geometry placed over the seams so the eye reads objects, not a pattern. Panel lines should also move into geometry and into the bake where they matter: a bevelled modelled plate catches light as a real edge, which is the Sins II bevel effect (Dev Journal 21), whereas a line drawn in albedo at a regular pitch always reads as print. Diagnosis step before committing: render one hull with a plain flat FAC3D colour and no texture at all. If the grid survives, it is geometry or normals. If it vanishes, it is UV or texture, and the UV route is confirmed as the fix.

### (d) Odd paint

Cause: projected concept paint. Pack section 3.2 step 4: the concept painting's camera is fitted and the view pictures are projected back onto the mesh and baked into albedo, on the reasoning that "the paint is as sharp as the pictures". A concept painting is a lit illustration. It contains its own key light, shadow, atmospheric falloff, painterly noise, and a layout designed for one camera angle. Projecting several such views onto a 3D surface bakes contradictory light into the colour map, smears detail where views disagree, and puts markings where the illustration wanted them, not where the ship needs them. In a PBR renderer that double counts light, once in the albedo and once from the scene light, which is why it reads as mottled camouflage in ryan_c01 per pack section 2. The Sins modder shader document states the underlying rule for PBR albedo: base colour "should not include elements of normal or ambient occlusion influence" and baked in shadow should be removed when converting older diffuse art (ModDB ship shader overview, above). Projected concept paint violates that rule by construction.

Step that removes the cause: stop using projected concept paint as albedo. Retire it to reference only, which is what it is good for: palette sampling, marking placement ideas, silhouette approval. Build albedo in Step 7 from FAC3D colours through masks, with decals for markings. This is the one defect where the fix is a deletion, not an improvement to the existing step. Pack section 5 supports the conclusion: G-14 records that 36 hulls were repainted in the Nov 1 sprint and the complaint postdates that repaint. More passes through the same projection cannot repair a method error.

Note on generators: pack section 9 warns that the loader comment names Hunyuan3D while the current generator is Pixal3D on a TRELLIS.2 cascade, and that the defect profile has not changed across generator rounds (pack section 3.2 step 3). INFERRED conclusion from that record: the defects are pipeline defects, not generator defects, so changing generator again is not a fix for any of the four.

## Part 4: Q7, organic hulls, and The Ninefold Maw

The Maw's problem is partly a category error, recorded in pack sections 2 and 9: machine texture language, panel grids, hexagonal turret like parts and flat panels, sitting on a creature that must read as flesh. Fixing surface quality while keeping machine language would still leave the hull wrong.

How cleanup differs for an organic hull:

1. Remesh choice flips in our favour. Voxel remesh is the natural tool for organic form, because it produces even topology for sculpting and removes intersections, which is the use the Blender Manual recommends it for (Blender Manual, Remeshing, above). There are no sharp panel edges to preserve, so the main weakness of automatic remesh on hard surface work does not apply. Automatic retopology is also better accepted for organic shapes: ZRemesher is described as the industry standard for organic models (AIMagicX guide, above), and published creature work uses ZRemesher with polygroups to hold detail while producing clean UV ready topology (ArtStation case study, Dragonite, https://darwinnisttahuz.artstation.com/blog/Lz76A/case-study-dragonite).
2. Fairing becomes sculpting. Instead of lofting sections, the artist or agent driven sculpt pass smooths the lumpy rock read into continuous muscle and membrane volumes, with deliberate asymmetry and a clear anatomy logic: a body mass, limbs or tendrils, a maw, sacs. Lumps must have a cause in the anatomy. Random lumps are the organic equivalent of panel noise.
3. Detail is sculpted and painted, not panelled. Creature surfacing practice separates skin zones by roughness and micro detail, adds spots, freckles and colour transitions by hand in the texturing pass, and uses subsurface scattering in thin areas such as membranes and wings so light glows through them (ArtStation case study, Dragonite, same source). ZBrush creature practice is the same in outline: alphas and stencils for skin detail, NoiseMaker for controlled surface texture, Polypaint for colour variation, and subsurface scattering to simulate light penetrating skin for fleshy creatures (Novedge, "Enhancing Digital Creature Skin Textures in ZBrush", https://novedge.com/blogs/design-news/zbrush-tip-enhancing-digital-creature-skin-textures-in-zbrush-techniques-and-tips-for-realistic-detailing).
4. Godot Forward+ has no true subsurface scattering for standard materials in the way a film renderer does, so the flesh read has to be faked with the tools we have. INFERRED practical set: warm FAC3D Chorus palette with strong value separation between body mass and sacs, higher roughness variation than the metal fleets, emissive in veins and sacs (which pack section 6 already rules: Chorus ships are living bio ships, veins and sacs glow, no bolt on guns), a thickness like gradient painted into an emissive mask so thin parts glow more under engine and weapon light, and rim friendly smooth normals with no hard edges anywhere. None of that needs a new shader programme to start, and all of it is testable in a screenshot, which is how the owner judges.
5. Remove machine parts and machine maps from the organic hulls entirely. No trim sheet, no panel line bake, no hex fittings as hull surface. Where the game needs a weapon or hardpoint on a Chorus hull, pack section 6 already answers it: no bolt on guns, weapons are grown features of the creature. If a modelled part is needed, it should be modelled as an organ, a horn, a spine, a sphincter like emitter, not as a turret.
6. Paint sources differ too. Skin colour variation should be large soft mottling at body scale, placed by masks tied to anatomy (belly lighter, sacs saturated, scar tissue pale), not the fine uniform grain that makes the Maw read as rock. Rock reads as rock because its noise is fine, grey brown and uniform. Flesh reads as flesh because its variation is large, warm and placed.

Scope for this variant: 5 Chorus capital hulls, plus the spore craft B23_51 which should follow the same flesh language at low effort. The other 30 hulls must not inherit any of it.

## Part 5: Routes for our fleet, priced

Effort units used below. "Script hour" means an agent writing and running Blender Python and pipeline scripts on the owner's PC, unattended except for the owner's screenshot review. "Manual hour" means a human working in Blender by hand on the owner's PC. Manual hours are the scarce resource on a one person project and are stated separately in every total. "Owner hour" means the owner's own time reviewing screenshots and ruling, which he already spends. All per hull figures below are estimates built from the pack's own evidence (one hull, C19_51, has completed the full current chain, and pack section 5 states no per hull effort number exists on file for repeating it) and are marked INFERRED as estimates. They are planning numbers for choosing a route, not measured times. Issue 75, when it lands, replaces the Route A and Route B figures for its three hulls with measured ones.

### Baseline: keep polishing the current chain

What it is: keep Pixal3D meshes, projected concept paint, procedural grain, runtime box projected UVs, and the existing detail pass, and do more rounds of it.

Per hull effort: 1 to 3 owner and script hours per pass, indefinitely, because there is no finishing step in the chain that removes a cause.

Total: unbounded. The Nov 1 sprint already spent a full repaint across all 36 hulls (pack G-14) and the four defects were reported again on Nov 2.

Defects: waviness left (geometry untouched). Bumps left, and usually increased, because each pass adds grain. Grid left (UV generation unchanged). Odd paint left (projection retained). Polishing this chain improves sharpness, which makes all four defects easier to see.

Risks: low technical risk, high opportunity cost. It also keeps consuming the owner's screenshot review time, which is the project's scarcest review resource. This baseline is included because pack section 8 requires it, and the evidence against it is already in hand. It is not a serious option.

### Route A: C19 pattern kitbash rebuild, clean shell loft plus trim sheet

What it is: for each hull, loft a clean shell from sections measured off the AI hull, cut away mangled AI regions, hand build bevelled kit parts (turrets, sensor arrays, radiators, engine blocks, armour slabs) snapped onto the real surface with BVH ray casts, map to a purpose built trim sheet, and paint procedurally from FAC3D. This is pack section 3.2 steps 5 to 7, generalised from the one worked example.

Up front cost: 25 to 40 script and manual hours to build a proper faction neutral trim sheet plus one trim sheet variant per faction family that needs one, and to turn the C19 kit scripts into a reusable part library with named landmarks. Trim design must come before hull work, because re laying UVs later is the most expensive rework in that workflow (CGI Academy Hub, above).

Per hull effort: 14 to 26 manual hours per capital hull, plus 2 to 4 script hours, plus about 1 owner hour of screenshot review across the blob sweep. INFERRED estimate. Squadron craft: 4 to 8 manual hours each. Objective structures: 10 to 20 manual hours each, the station being the worst because it is large and seen from all sides.

Total for the full priced scope: 30 capitals at 14 to 26 manual hours is 420 to 780 manual hours, plus 3 craft at 4 to 8 (12 to 24), plus 3 structures at 10 to 20 (30 to 60), plus up front 25 to 40. Total roughly 490 to 900 hours, of which the great majority is manual Blender work. At a realistic 6 focused manual hours a week from a one person project with agent help, that is 80 to 150 weeks. Stated plainly: Route A across the whole fleet is a two to three year art programme, not a fix.

Defects: waviness removed (the loft replaces the primary surface, which is the cause). Bumps removed (detail is modelled parts and trim, not noise). Grid removed if the trim sheet is designed with varied panel scales, and at risk of returning if the trim sheet is used lazily at one scale everywhere. Odd paint removed (procedural FAC3D paint replaces projection).

Where it does not fit: Chorus organic hulls. A lofted shell plus hard surface kit is the wrong language for a creature and would repeat the Maw's category error in a cleaner form. It also fits flagships far better than escorts, because its cost is justified only where the camera and the player's attention go. It is wasteful on the 3 squadron craft, which are small and fast on screen.

Risks: FAC3D, safe, this route already works inside the kit law that FAC3D is the colour source and baked finish maps are wear masks only. R73 break up, moderate risk: a lofted shell plus separate kit parts changes how a hull separates into chunks. Kit parts are separate objects, which helps break up, but the shell is a new closed surface, so the clip and plug prototype must be re tested on the first Route A hull before the route is rolled out. Damage surface work, low risk, it is a separate ruled thread (R76) and this route changes base materials, so damage masks must be re checked against the new paint solve. Godot conversion, low risk, output files are the same kinds build_assets.gd already reads. Blob sweep, helps: a lofted hull is far more likely to pass the wavy bow cells. Main risk is schedule and abandonment: a route that has completed exactly one hull in the project's history (pack section 3.2 step 9) has a demonstrated throughput problem, whatever its quality.

### Route B: voxel remesh, retopo, baked detail, faction PBR in place of projected paint

What it is: the Part 2 pipeline run as a mostly scripted batch. Repair, voxel remesh, sculpt fairing pass, Quadriflow or Instant Meshes retopology, authored UVs (unique unwrap for capitals, shared tileable and trim materials for secondary surfaces), bake normal, AO and curvature in Blender from a faired and bevelled detail version of the clean mesh, then a single procedural FAC3D paint solve driven by those masks, with decals for markings. No hand built kit parts except where the blob sweep fails a region.

Up front cost: 40 to 70 script hours to build and debug the batch, including the fairing step, the bake settings, the paint solve, the flat lit and normals only QA renders, and the pack and convert step. This is the largest single block of work in the route and it is almost all agent script time on the owner's PC, not manual time. INFERRED estimate.

Per hull effort: 1 to 3 manual hours per capital hull (fairing touch ups, UV seam fixes, bake artefact fixes, decal placement), plus 1 to 2 script hours of machine time, plus about 30 minutes of owner screenshot review. Craft and structures: 1 to 2 manual hours each, structures at the top of that range. INFERRED estimate.

Total for the full priced scope: up front 40 to 70 script hours, plus 30 capitals at 1 to 3 manual hours (30 to 90), plus 6 craft and structures at 1 to 2 manual hours (6 to 12). Total roughly 80 to 170 hours, of which manual work is roughly 36 to 102 hours. At 6 manual hours a week plus unattended script runs, that is roughly 8 to 16 weeks of calendar time, and most hulls can be re run cheaply when the solve improves, which Route A hulls cannot.

Defects: waviness reduced, and removed only where the fairing step is done properly. This is the honest weakness of Route B and it must not be oversold. A batch that voxel remeshes and bakes without a real fairing pass will ship clean topology with the same waves in it. The route therefore needs a per hull waviness gate: the single light silhouette render from Part 3(a), checked in the blob sweep, with hulls that fail going to section lofting for the failed region only. Bumps removed, provided the bake source is the faired clean mesh and not the raw AI mesh. That condition is the whole game and is a script setting, not a hope. Grid removed, because UVs are authored and the box projection is deleted. Odd paint removed, because projected paint is deleted and albedo comes from FAC3D through masks.

Where it does not fit: it does not by itself give flagships the authored, cinematic density the owner has ruled for the Pyre-Barge (pack section 5, R16: the quality bar is "more realistic and cinematic" next to BattleTech, Sins of a Solar Empire and Rogue Trader, and final Barge sign off is still withheld pending a much larger graphical upgrade). Route B makes a good fleet. It does not make a hero ship. It also needs the organic variant in Part 4 for Chorus hulls. Applied mechanically to the Maw it would produce a clean, panel free, but still rock like creature, because the flesh material work is a separate step.

Risks: FAC3D, safe if the paint solve takes colour only from FAC3D, which is a design rule for the solve and must be written into the script, not left to taste. R73 break up, low risk: hulls stay single closed meshes close to their current form, so existing break up prototyping carries over better than under Route A. Damage surface work, moderate risk: new normal and AO maps change how damage decals and holes sit, so the damage thread needs one re test hull. Godot conversion, low risk: same file types, but texture sizes and a possible second UV channel for decals must be confirmed through build_assets.gd on one hull first. Blob sweep, helps on texture and paint cells, neutral on shape cells unless the waviness gate is enforced. Quality risk is the real one: automatic retopology plus a procedural solve can drift toward a competent but generic look, and the defence is decals, faction specific trim and value blocking, all of which are cheap once the batch exists.

### Route C: tiered hybrid, Route B for the fleet, Route A for flagships, organic variant for Chorus

What it is: three tiers, one shared foundation.
- Foundation for all 36 hulls: delete projected concept paint, delete runtime box projected UVs, adopt the FAC3D mask driven paint solve, the authored UV standard, and the flat lit plus normals only QA renders. This foundation is shared by every tier and is where three of the four defects actually die.
- Tier 1, fleet tier, 23 non flagship, non Chorus capital hulls plus the 3 squadron craft plus the 3 objective structures (29 hulls): Route B as above.
- Tier 2, flagship tier, one flagship per faction for Hegemony, Crusade, Hullbreakers, Glasswrights and Eclipse, plus the Pyre-Barge which is already part built (6 hulls): Route B first, then a Route A kit and trim pass on top of the cleaned hull, so the hand work is spent adding readable parts to a fair surface instead of rescuing a wavy one. The Barge keeps its approved R13 shape and R14 paint steps and gets its further graphical upgrade here, which is what R16 asks for.
- Tier 3, Chorus tier, 5 Chorus capital hulls plus the spore craft: Route B geometry cleanup with the organic variant from Part 4, sculpt fairing, flesh materials, emissive veins and sacs, no trim sheet, no kit parts.

Up front cost: the Route B batch (40 to 70 script hours) plus the trim sheet and kit library work from Route A, reduced because it only has to serve 6 hulls (15 to 25 script and manual hours). Total up front 55 to 95 hours, mostly script time.

Per hull effort: Tier 1, as Route B, 1 to 3 manual hours per hull. Tier 2, 8 to 16 manual hours per flagship on top of the Route B pass, less than a full Route A rebuild because the shell is already fair and UV'd. Tier 3, 4 to 8 manual hours per Chorus hull, mostly sculpt fairing and flesh paint placement.

Total for the full priced scope: up front 55 to 95, Tier 1 at 29 hulls times 1 to 3 manual hours (29 to 87), Tier 2 at 6 hulls times 8 to 16 manual hours (48 to 96), Tier 3 at 6 hulls times 4 to 8 manual hours (24 to 48). Total roughly 155 to 325 hours, of which manual work is roughly 100 to 230 hours. At 6 manual hours a week that is roughly 17 to 38 weeks of manual effort, with script work running in parallel and unattended. INFERRED estimates, same basis as above. That is a big programme and this report does not pretend otherwise. It is roughly a third of the manual cost of Route A across the fleet, and it puts the hand hours where the camera and the owner's own rulings say they matter.

Defects: waviness removed on Tier 2 and Tier 3 hulls (loft or sculpt fairing), reduced and gated on Tier 1 hulls with region lofting as the fallback for failures. Bumps removed on all tiers (bake source changed). Grid removed on all tiers (authored UVs). Odd paint removed on all tiers (projection deleted). Chorus category error removed on Tier 3 only, which is the only tier where it exists.

Where it does not fit: nothing in the fleet is left without a tier, but Tier 1 escorts will look plainer than Tier 2 flagships at extreme close zoom. That is a deliberate choice, supported by the Homeworld 3 and Starsector readability evidence in Part 1, and it is the main thing the owner is being asked to accept in the questions below.

Risks: FAC3D, safe, one paint solve across all tiers. R73 break up, moderate risk concentrated in Tier 2, where kit parts are separate objects on a cleaned shell. Mitigation: run the clip and plug prototype on the first Tier 2 hull before the other five are started. Damage surface work, moderate, one re test hull per tier rather than one overall, because materials differ between tiers. Godot conversion, low, same file kinds, but Tier 2 kit parts increase object count per hull, so frame cost at 20 ships must be measured on the first Tier 2 hull at 4K before rollout. Pack section 6 gives the test conditions: Forward+, about 20 ships, RTX 5080. Blob sweep, helps everywhere, and Tier 2 and Tier 3 hulls should pass it most easily. Complexity risk is the honest one: three tiers means three sets of steps to document in docs/ship_pipeline and keep straight. Mitigation: the foundation is shared, so the tiers differ only in what is added after the bake, not in the cleanup itself.

## Recommendation

Adopt Route C, the tiered hybrid. Build the shared foundation first, run Route B across the fleet, spend Route A hand work only on the six flagships, and run the organic variant on the six Chorus hulls.

Reasoning, in order of weight:

1. Three of the four defects are method errors, not effort errors. Odd paint comes from projection, grid from generated UVs, bumps from baking a noisy source. All three are removed by deleting a step and changing a source, which costs script time once and nothing per hull afterwards. Any route that keeps those steps and adds effort on top, including the baseline and a pure Route A that retained projected paint, would keep the defects.
2. Waviness is the only defect that truly costs per hull, because it is geometry. Route A removes it everywhere at 490 to 900 hours, which a one person project will not finish, and an unfinished fleet wide rebuild is worse than a finished tiered one, because it leaves two visual generations of ship in the same battle. Route C removes it where it is most visible and gates it elsewhere with a cheap render test and a regional loft fallback.
3. The shipped game evidence points the same way. Sins II shows the correct order of operations (clean quad form, bevel, bake normal, curvature and AO, texture from those masks) and also shows that doing it fully by hand is specialist days per ship. Homeworld 3 and Starsector show that the tactical read is shape, value blocking, large paint shapes and glow, which Route B plus decals delivers. Armada and Deadlock show that close zoom richness is a camera dependent luxury, which is why it belongs on six flagships and not on thirty hulls.
4. Route C protects what is already ruled. It keeps FAC3D as the only colour source, keeps hulls as single closed meshes for the R73 break up work except on the six flagships where the risk is tested first, keeps the Godot conversion inputs unchanged in kind, and it gives the Pyre-Barge the further graphical upgrade R16 requires instead of restarting it.
5. Route C is reversible in the right places. Because Tier 1 hulls come out of a batch, improving the paint solve or the trim set later re runs the fleet in script time. Route A hulls are hand work that has to be redone by hand.

How to judge this against the issue 75 bake off pictures when they land. Issue 75 renders three hulls three ways: today, Route B, and Route A for the Pyre-Barge only. Judge on these four checks, in this order:
- Eclipse zenith cruiser, Route B, close zoom, hull surface only: is the long surface fair, with no ripple in the highlight, and is the mottled camouflage gone? If yes, Route B's fairing and paint claims hold and Tier 1 is safe. If the surface is clean textured but still wavy, the fairing step in the bake off was too weak, which is a fixable script and sculpt setting, not a reason to abandon the route. Ask which fairing setting was used before changing route.
- Eclipse cruiser, Route B, battle camera: does it read as an Eclipse ship at a glance, from silhouette and FAC3D value blocking? If it reads well at battle distance but plain at close zoom, that is the expected Tier 1 result and it is acceptable.
- The Ninefold Maw, Route B: expect this to be the weakest picture, because issue 75's Route B as written is a machine pipeline. Judge it only on whether the surface noise and machine paint are gone. Do not judge the flesh read from it, because the flesh work is Tier 3 and is not in the bake off. If the Maw looks like a clean rock, that confirms the need for Tier 3 rather than refuting Route C.
- Pyre-Barge, Route A versus Route B, side by side at both zooms, plus the frame cost figures issue 75 owes: the gap between those two pictures is the entire case for Tier 2. If the gap is large at close zoom and small at battle distance, Tier 2 for six flagships is exactly right. If the gap is small at both distances, drop Tier 2 to the Barge alone and save roughly 40 to 80 manual hours. If the gap is large at battle distance too, that is evidence for widening Tier 2, and the cost of widening it is 8 to 16 manual hours per added hull.

Sequencing if Route C is approved: (1) foundation and batch built and proven on the Eclipse zenith cruiser, because it is a bake off hull and the comparison already exists; (2) Maw organic variant proven next, because it is the highest risk of looking wrong; (3) Barge Tier 2 pass, with the R73 break up re test and a 20 ship frame cost check at 4K; (4) Tier 1 batch across the remaining hulls with the blob sweep as the gate; (5) remaining flagships and Chorus hulls.

## Owner questions

**Question 1. How many hulls get the full hand built treatment?**
Options: (a) Six flagships, one per faction plus the Pyre-Barge. Cost: 48 to 96 manual hours on top of the batch, and the other 24 capitals look plainer at extreme close zoom. (b) All 30 capitals. Cost: roughly 490 to 900 total hours, a two to three year programme, with two generations of ship visible in battles for most of that time. (c) The Pyre-Barge only. Cost: saves roughly 40 to 80 manual hours against option (a), and leaves five factions with no hero ship, which weakens faction identity in screenshots and in the ship viewer.
**Pick: (a) six flagships.** It matches where the camera and the player's attention actually go, and it satisfies the Barge ruling without mortgaging the fleet.

**Question 2. Is projected concept paint retired completely, or kept for flagships where a specific painted look is wanted?**
Options: (a) Retired completely, concepts become reference only. Cost: some distinctive painted details from the best concepts are lost and must be recreated as decals, roughly 30 minutes to 1 hour of decal work per flagship. (b) Kept for flagships only, after de lighting. Cost: de lighting a painting is manual, unreliable work, roughly 3 to 6 hours per flagship, and it reintroduces the double lighting problem in a milder form on the ships that get looked at closest.
**Pick: (a) retired completely.** The Nov 1 repaint of all 36 hulls already tested the "keep the method, try harder" version of this question and the complaint followed it.

**Question 3. What should Chorus flesh look like in engine, given there is no true subsurface scattering in our setup?**
Options: (a) Faked flesh: warm FAC3D palette, large soft mottling placed by anatomy masks, emissive veins and sacs that brighten in thin areas. Cost: no new shader work, roughly 4 to 8 manual hours per Chorus hull, and the effect is suggestive rather than physical, so it will not survive a film style close up. (b) Custom shader work for a wrap lighting and translucency approximation in Godot. Cost: INFERRED 15 to 30 hours of shader work and testing, plus an ongoing maintenance cost every time materials change, for a benefit visible mainly at close zoom on six hulls.
**Pick: (a) faked flesh now,** with (b) revisited only if the Maw still reads as rock in screenshots after the material pass. Spend on the material and the sculpt first, because a shader cannot fix a lumpy silhouette.

**Question 4. Unique unwrap per hull, or trim and tileable materials shared across a faction?**
Options: (a) Hybrid: unique unwrap for the main hull surface, shared trim and tileable materials for machinery, vents and secondary structure, decals for identity. Cost: the trim set has to be designed up front, 15 to 25 hours, and artists must resist mapping everything to the trim because that is how the grid read comes back. (b) Unique unwrap everywhere, the Sins II pattern. Cost: roughly 1 to 2 extra manual hours per hull across 36 hulls, 36 to 72 hours, for close zoom uniqueness that the tactical camera cannot show. (c) Trim and tileable everywhere. Cost: cheapest per hull, and the highest risk of the regular repeating pattern the owner has already complained about.
**Pick: (a) hybrid.** It is the only option that removes the grid defect by construction while keeping per hull cost down.

**Question 5. What is the acceptance bar for Tier 1 escorts: battle camera only, or close zoom as well?**
Options: (a) Battle camera is the bar, close zoom must merely be free of the four named defects. Cost: escorts will look plain next to flagships when a player zooms right in, which some players will notice and comment on. (b) Close zoom is the bar for every hull. Cost: INFERRED an extra 3 to 6 manual hours per Tier 1 hull, 87 to 174 extra hours across 29 hulls, and the schedule roughly doubles.
**Pick: (a) battle camera bar, with the four defects still banned at any zoom.** Plain is acceptable. Wavy, bumpy, gridded and mottled are not, and those are defects, not detail levels.
