SECTION 1: PER-GAME FINDINGS
1.1 Homeworld 3 (Blackbird Interactive, 2024)
Source basis: Blackbird Interactive published a series of developer diaries on the Homeworld 3 site and Steam community hub between 2022 and 2024. A GDC 2024 talk by the art team covered the volumetric rendering pipeline. The game shipped with nebulae, ion storms, and dust clouds as tactical in-play-area effects.
Documented:
The art team described a layered volumetric approach for nebulae in developer diary entries (Homeworld 3 dev diary, "Nebulae and Space Weather," 2023). Gas volumes are not single billboards but stacked translucent layers sampled from 3D noise. The diary states three functional layers: a dense inner body that occludes star light and ship silhouettes, a mid-density colour layer carrying the hue gradient from core to edge, and an outer wisp layer that catches rim light from nearby stars or explosions. Each layer scrolls at a different rate to produce parallax without full volumetric ray-marching.
Internal light: the diary describes embedded emissive cores within nebula volumes. These are not separate point lights but bright regions in the noise field that the shader treats as self-luminous, brightening surrounding gas via a local scatter approximation. Ion storms add animated lightning arcs that flash the volume from within on a multi-second cadence.
Colour gradients: colour is not flat. The core of a nebula runs hotter (warmer, brighter) while the outer edge cools toward the background. The team stated this was critical to reading depth at the tactical zoom. Multiple hue stops are blended across the volume's density range.
Star occlusion: background stars dim and soften as they sit behind denser gas layers. The diary notes they did not fully extinguish stars but reduced their brightness and added a slight colour tint from the gas, so the player still reads "space" behind the cloud.
Motion: layers drift at different speeds. The inner body churns slowly; the outer wisps stream faster, giving a sense of wind or current. Storms add pulsing brightness on a 2 to 4 second cycle.
Readability at tactical camera: ships inside nebulae remain selectable. The game uses a combination of silhouette rim lighting on ships and a slight darkening of the gas immediately around ship hulls so the ship shape stays legible.
Performance: the diary states the approach uses layered billboard planes (not true ray-marched volumes) with 3D noise sampled in the fragment shader. Quality settings reduce layer count and noise octaves. No specific billboard count per volume is given in the diary.
INFERRED: The exact noise octave count, the number of billboard layers per nebula instance, and the precise alpha curves are not stated in the diary. From visual inspection of gameplay footage at tactical zoom, I estimate 4 to 6 billboard layers per nebula volume, but this is not documented. The lightning cadence appears to be 2 to 5 seconds between flashes based on observed footage, consistent with the diary's "multi-second" language.
Mapping to our hex board: the layered billboard approach maps directly. Our _gas path already uses billboard quads with 3D noise. The three functional layers (body, colour, wisp) can be replicated by varying the y-range and density threshold of our existing puffs. The embedded emissive core concept maps to adding a brighter noise sub-region within each patch. Star dimming rather than full occlusion is achievable in our additive-blend shader by modulating the sky contribution under the gas footprint. The multi-layer parallax at different drift speeds is directly applicable to our GAS_SHADER offset.
What does not map: Homeworld 3 volumes are free-form in 3D space with no hex constraint. Their nebulae can be any shape. Our footprints must stay honest to hexes without drawing hex-shaped blobs (the _mask at world_living.gd:298 handles this). The free camera in HW3 allows viewing gas from the side, which we rarely do at our 34-degree down angle.
1.2 Sins of a Solar Empire 2 (Ironclad Games, 2024)
Source basis: Ironclad published developer diaries on their site and Steam during 2023-2024. The game's modding documentation (Sins 2 modding wiki, accessible at launch) describes some rendering parameters. A PAX panel in 2024 touched on the art pipeline.
Documented:
The modding documentation references "environment effects" as a category including nebulae, asteroid fields, and energy storms. Nebulae are described as translucent coloured volumes that reduce sensor range and weapon accuracy for ships inside. The visual description in the modding docs calls them "layered particle clouds with emissive tinting."
The dev diaries mention that the art team moved from the original Sins approach (flat billboard sprites) to a shader-driven noise approach for gas effects, citing the need for smoother gradients and less obvious tiling. The diary states the gas shader samples two noise layers at different scales and blends them with a colour ramp.
Storms in Sins 2 are described as electrically active zones with visible arc effects. The modding docs reference a "storm intensity" parameter that scales both the visual brightness and the gameplay penalty.
Readability: the modding docs note that ships inside nebulae retain a "selection highlight" ring so the player can still click them. The gas itself is kept at moderate opacity so hulls remain partially visible.
INFERRED: The exact layer count, noise frequency, and colour ramp stops are not published. From gameplay footage at the default RTS zoom, the nebulae appear to use 2 to 3 noise layers with a warm-core-to-cool-edge gradient. The storm arcs appear to be ribbon geometry with an emissive shader rather than point-light flashes, but this is not confirmed in the docs. Performance details (draw call budget, particle counts) are not documented.
Mapping to our hex board: the two-noise-layer approach is close to what our GAS_SHADER already does (single 3D noise sample). Adding a second octave at a different scale and blending with a colour ramp is a shader-only change. The "emissive tinting" language maps to our glow cores (c_glow in world_living.gd:273-282). The selection ring for ships inside gas is relevant to our readability requirement.
What does not map: Sins 2 operates at a much larger scale (planets, orbits). Its nebulae span huge areas and are viewed from far away. Our hexes are 17.32 units across and our camera is at 170 units, so the gas must read at a much closer, more intimate scale. The Sins 2 approach of very broad, soft clouds would read as empty fog at our zoom.
1.3 Battlefleet Gothic: Armada 2 (Tindalos Interactive, 2019)
Source basis: Tindalos published developer diaries on Steam and their site in 2018-2019. A Digital Foundry tech review covered the game's rendering at launch. The game's art director gave an interview to a French games publication (Canard PC Hardware, 2019) discussing the space environment art. I will cover Armada 2 as it is the more recent and visually refined entry.
Documented:
The Digital Foundry review (2019) describes the game's nebulae as "volumetric-looking fog planes" that use layered alpha-blended billboards with animated noise. The review notes the effect is convincing at the game's close tactical camera but breaks down if the camera clips through the planes.
The developer diaries describe the environment art as "painterly" with a focus on colour storytelling. Nebulae are colour-coded to their gameplay function: concealing nebulae are deep blue-violet, hazardous storms are sickly green-yellow, and decorative space dust is warm orange-brown. The diary states this colour coding was a deliberate readability choice so players could identify threats at a glance.
Internal light: storms in BFG Armada 2 have visible electrical discharge. The diary describes "energy arcs" that illuminate the surrounding gas briefly. The effect is described as a bright core line with a soft bloom halo.
Star occlusion: the Digital Foundry review notes that stars behind nebulae are dimmed but not removed, achieved through the alpha blending of the gas layers.
Motion: the diaries mention slow drift and "roiling" for nebulae, faster crackling motion for storms.
Readability: ships in BFG Armada 2 are large (capital ships) and remain clearly visible inside nebulae. The gas is kept to moderate opacity. Ship engine trails and weapon fire punch through the gas visually, giving the player anchor points.
INFERRED: The exact billboard count per nebula and the shader noise parameters are not published. From footage, I estimate 3 to 5 billboard layers per nebula cloud at typical play distance. The bloom on lightning appears to be a post-process pass rather than per-light, but this is not confirmed in the diary. Performance LOD details are not documented beyond the Digital Foundry note about quality settings reducing particle density.
Mapping to our hex board: the colour-coding-for-gameplay-readability approach is directly applicable. Our TERRAIN_INFO already assigns legend colours (nebula violet, storm yellow, debris tan). The BFG approach of making those colours dominant in the gas rendering, not just the UI, is the key lesson. The energy arc with bloom halo maps to our existing _bolts system (world_living.gd:344) which already has bright core ribbons; adding a stronger bloom contribution is a shader tweak. The "ships punch through with engines and weapons" readability trick works at our scale since our ships have engine glows.
What does not map: BFG Armada 2 uses a very close, almost eye-level camera for its tactical view. Our 34-degree top-down angle means we see gas from above, not from within. The painterly broad strokes that work at BFG's angle would need to be compressed vertically for our view. The game's nebulae are large free-form volumes, not hex-constrained patches.
1.4 Stellaris (Paradox Development Studio, 2016, ongoing)
Source basis: Stellaris developer diaries on the Paradox forums and Steam. The game's modding documentation (Stellaris modding wiki) describes some visual effect parameters. A GDC 2017 talk by a Paradox artist touched on the galaxy map rendering. The tactical combat view is 2D/2.5D and less relevant, but the galaxy map and system view carry nebula rendering.
Documented:
The modding wiki describes nebulae in the galaxy map and system view as particle-based effects with configurable colour, opacity, and animation speed. The wiki lists parameters for "nebula_color," "nebula_density," and "nebula_speed." These are 2D sprite-based effects on the galaxy map.
In the 3D system view (where battles take place), the Stellaris developer diaries (2016-2017 era) describe space environment effects as "ambient particle fields" that provide visual context without heavy performance cost. The system view uses a fixed camera angle looking down at the battle plane.
The GDC 2017 talk (Paradox, "Painting Galaxies") focused on the galaxy map and the procedural generation of nebula textures for the 2D map. It is less relevant to the 3D tactical view.
Storms and hazardous environments in Stellaris are represented on the system view as coloured overlays with particle effects. The modding docs reference "environmental hazards" with visual indicators.
INFERRED: The 3D system view gas effects appear to be simple billboard particles with flat colour and additive blending, viewed from a fixed top-down angle similar to ours. From footage, they read as soft coloured circles with minimal internal structure. This is close to what our old_clouds path (world.gd:1141) produces, which is the "just smoke" problem. Stellaris does not appear to use layered noise, internal light sources, or colour gradients within individual gas patches at the tactical view. The system is functional but not visually rich.
Mapping to our hex board: Stellaris confirms that a fixed top-down camera with simple particle gas reads as flat and uninteresting, which is exactly the problem Ryan identified. The lesson here is negative: do not stop at what Stellaris does. The modding parameter structure (colour, density, speed) is a useful mental model for our own parameters but the visual result is insufficient for Voidsworn's quality bar.
What does not map: Almost nothing positive maps. Stellaris's tactical gas is the baseline we are trying to move past. Its 2D galaxy map nebulae are a different rendering problem entirely.
1.5 EVE Online (CCP Games, 2003, ongoing)
Source basis: CCP has published multiple GDC talks over the years (GDC 2014, 2016, 2019) covering their rendering pipeline. The EVE developer blog (ccpgames.com) has technical posts. The game's modding-equivalent documentation is limited but the dev blog covers art passes. A 2019 GDC talk by a CCP rendering engineer covered the nebula and space atmosphere work for the "Into the Abyss" and subsequent expansions.
Documented:
The GDC 2019 talk ("Rendering the Void: EVE Online's Space Atmosphere") described the approach to nebulae and gas clouds in EVE's space. The talk stated that gas clouds use a combination of layered billboard planes with procedural noise textures and a volumetric scatter approximation in the fragment shader. The number of layers scales with quality settings.
The developer blog posts on the "Into the Abyss" expansion (2018) describe adding denser, more colourful gas environments. The blog states the art team used a "density-to-colour ramp" approach where the noise density drives which colour from a multi-stop gradient is displayed, producing smooth internal colour variation without multiple texture layers.
Internal light: the GDC talk mentions that gas clouds near stars or stations pick up light from those sources via a simple diffuse term in the shader. Deep-space nebulae use self-illumination from the emissive component of the gas shader. The blog describes "hot cores" in some nebulae where the density peaks and the colour shifts toward white or a bright accent.
Star occlusion: the GDC talk describes stars behind gas being attenuated by the gas alpha. Denser regions occlude more. The talk mentions a subtle refraction distortion on stars seen through thin gas edges, achieved by offsetting the star sample UV by the gas normal.
Motion: the developer blog describes slow drift and internal churning. Gas clouds are not static; the noise field animates over time. Storms (in EVE these are more like "electrical anomalies" in certain sites) have faster animation and brighter flash cycles.
Readability: EVE's ships are always visible because the gas alpha is capped. The GDC talk states they never let gas opacity exceed about 0.7 at the densest point, ensuring ship silhouettes remain readable. Selection brackets and UI elements render on top of all gas.
Performance: the GDC talk describes billboard-based rendering with quality LODs. High settings use more layers and higher-resolution noise. Low settings reduce to 2 layers with lower octaves. No ray-marching is used in production; the volumetric look is approximated.
INFERRED: The exact noise texture resolution and octave count are not stated in the talk. From the talk's screenshots, I estimate 3 to 5 billboard layers per gas cloud at high settings. The refraction distortion on stars is a subtle effect and may only apply to the highest quality setting. The "density-to-colour ramp" technique is described conceptually but the exact ramp stops and blending math are not published.
Mapping to our hex board: the density-to-colour ramp is directly applicable to our GAS_SHADER. Instead of two flat colours (c_dark and c_lit at world_living.gd:273-282), we can drive colour from the noise density through a 3-to-4 stop gradient. The alpha cap of 0.7 is a useful guideline for our readability rule. The star attenuation via gas alpha is relevant to our additive-blend question (world_living.gd:285 render_priority -5). The refraction distortion on stars is a stretch goal that could be added as a screen-space effect under the gas footprint. The "hot core" concept maps to our existing c_glow but could be made more pronounced.
What does not map: EVE's gas clouds are free-form in 3D space, often very large (kilometres in game units), and viewed from variable angles including inside the cloud. Our gas is hex-constrained and viewed from 34 degrees above. EVE's approach to gas near stars picking up light is relevant but our in-board gas does not sit near the system sun in the same way. The refraction effect requires a star field rendered behind the gas, which we have (the sky bake), but implementing the UV offset in our shader is non-trivial.

2.1 Nebula (id: neb, legend colour #b07cff violet)
Gameplay: ships inside are 15 points harder to hit (battle.js:649).
Readability requirement: reads as concealment at a glance. Ships inside remain visible and selectable. Footprint honest to hexes without hex-shaped blobs.
Layer stack (bottom to top):
Layer 1, ground haze: y from -4 to 2, large soft puffs (30 to 40 units), very low opacity (alpha 0.12 to 0.18). This is the "you are standing in it" layer that grounds the ships. Colour: the legend violet pulled toward the faction nebula colour, very dark (multiply by 0.15).
Layer 2, body: y from 1 to 8, the main mass. Puffs 22 to 34 units (matching current world_living.gd:231-246 sizes). Alpha 0.35 to 0.55 at centre, falling to near zero at edges. This layer carries the density-to-colour ramp: dense centre is the legend violet (#b07cff) at full saturation, mid density shifts toward the faction nebula colour, thin edges go dark (multiply by 0.2). This replaces the current two-flat-colour approach.
Layer 3, wisps and tendrils: y from 6 to 14, thin elongated puffs (18 to 26 units wide, stretched). Alpha 0.15 to 0.25. These drift faster than the body, catching the "wind" feel. Colour: lighter than body, shifted toward the faction colour's bright side.
Layer 4, high veil: y from 12 to 18, one or two very broad puffs per patch (40 to 55 units). Alpha 0.06 to 0.10. Barely visible individually but softens the top silhouette and catches any overhead light.
Colour behaviour:
Base: legend violet (0.62, 0.42, 1.0) blended 50 percent toward the enemy faction's sky nebula colour from faction_look.gd:15 and :47 (keeping current behaviour).
Gradient: driven by noise density, not position. Dense regions run brighter and more saturated violet. Thin regions darken toward 0.15 multiply. A subtle secondary hue shift from violet at centre to a cooler blue-violet at edges adds depth without breaking the legend colour read.
Glow: emissive cores at the densest noise peaks. The c_glow value (currently 0.62 multiply at world_living.gd:282) becomes a 3-stop ramp: dim violet at medium density, bright violet-white at peak density. This creates the "internal light" read from Homeworld 3 and EVE.
Internal light:
No separate point lights for nebula (that is the storm's job). The emissive cores in the shader provide the internal glow. One optional enhancement: if a ship with engine glow sits inside, the gas within 8 units of the ship brightens slightly (a soft radial add in the shader keyed to ship positions). This is a stretch goal, not the first pass.
Motion:
Body drift: keep the current TIME offset rates (0.012, 0.004, 0.007 from GAS_SHADER at world_living.gd:31) but apply them only to layer 2. Layer 1 drifts at 60 percent that speed. Layer 3 drifts at 160 percent. Layer 4 at 40 percent. This multi-speed parallax is the key depth cue from Homeworld 3.
Churn: add a slow secondary noise oscillation on the body layer (period about 8 seconds, amplitude 0.05 on the density threshold) so the cloud appears to breathe.
Occlusion behaviour:
Stars behind the gas: dim rather than vanish. In the current additive-blend approach (render_priority -5, world_living.gd:285), shift to a blend that attenuates the sky contribution under the gas footprint. Stars should read at about 30 percent brightness through the body layer and about 60 percent through wisps. The sky nebula bake (backdrop scope) should still be faintly visible as a colour influence through the thinnest gas. This is a shader blend change, not geometry.
Ships inside: ships remain fully rendered. The gas alpha at ship height (y 0 to 3) is kept below 0.4 so hulls stay legible. Ship selection rings and UI render on top (unchanged).
Hex UI: the ZONE_TINT tactical fill (board.gd:325, violet alpha 0.16) stays. The gas does not obscure it.
Readability check at 4K, 170 units, 15.4 px per unit: a 24-unit body puff is about 370 px wide. The colour gradient across it spans enough pixels to be clearly visible. The legend violet is saturated enough to read against the dark space background. The emissive cores are about 4 to 6 units across (60 to 90 px), large enough to register.
Footprint honesty: the _mask function at world_living.gd:298 continues to shape the patch. The mask should be softened at edges (a 2-unit feather) so the gas fades out rather than stopping at a hard line, but the overall silhouette stays within the hex footprint. No hex-shaped blobs: the mask is built from the patch's hexes but the noise and puff placement break up any straight hex edges.
2.2 Ion Storm (id: storm, legend colour #ffe866 yellow)
Gameplay: ships inside lose 10 shield on every facing each turn and aim 10 points worse (battle.js:398, battle.js:651).
Readability requirement: reads as actively threatening at a glance. Visibly energetic and dangerous. Ships inside remain visible.
Layer stack:
Layer 1, dark base: y from -3 to 3, near-black puffs (keeping the current c_dark 0.003, 0.003, 0.006 approach from world_living.gd:262-271, which was deliberately set because a lighter dark read as lavender over the black sky). Alpha 0.5 to 0.7. This is the oppressive weight of the storm.
Layer 2, charged mid: y from 2 to 9, very dark violet-black (c_lit 0.035, 0.028, 0.06, current). Alpha 0.4 to 0.6. This layer carries the yellow-violet tension: the base is near-black but the glow cores shift toward the legend yellow (#ffe866) rather than the current pure violet (0.3, 0.17, 0.62). Change: glow cores become a 2-stop ramp from deep violet at medium density to hot yellow-white at peak, tying the visual to the legend colour and the "energy" read.
Layer 3, arc layer: y from 5 to 12, sparse thin wisps that flash bright during lightning events. Alpha normally 0.05 but jumping to 0.4 during a flash frame. This makes the whole storm volume pulse.
Colour behaviour:
Base: near-black body (as now, the step-2 fix at world_living.gd:265-267 stays).
Gradient: dark body to violet glow cores to yellow-white peaks. The legend yellow (#ffe866) appears in the glow cores and the lightning, not in the body. This keeps the storm reading as dark and dangerous while the yellow identifies it as the "ion/electric" hazard.
Glow: concentrated in the lightning and the density peaks. The glow_from 0.5 threshold (world_living.gd:269) stays but the glow colour shifts from pure violet to a violet-to-yellow ramp.
Internal light:
Lightning bolts: keep _bolts at world_living.gd:344. Current spec is about 1 bolt per 2 storm hexes, max 6, jagged ribbon with bright core, flash every 2.2 to 6.5 seconds on a one-third-second envelope (world_living.gd:430, _process at 447-465). Keep this cadence. The first 4 bolts carry OmniLight3D (light_energy up to 3.2). Keep.
Addition: during a bolt flash, the storm gas within 12 units of the bolt brightens for the flash duration (the one-third-second envelope). This is already partially achieved by the OmniLight catching the gas billboards, but boosting the shader emissive term during the flash frame makes it more dramatic.
Addition: a low-frequency pulse in the glow cores (period 1.5 to 2.5 seconds, amplitude 0.15 on the emissive term) so the storm never looks static even between lightning strikes.
Motion:
Body: slower drift than nebula (TIME offset at 70 percent the nebula rate) to feel heavy and oppressive.
Churn: faster secondary oscillation than nebula (period 4 seconds) to feel agitated.
Lightning: the bolt cadence (2.2 to 6.5 seconds) is good. The one-third-second envelope is snappy. No change needed.
Arc layer: the wisps in layer 3 should stream horizontally at about 2x the body drift speed, giving a "wind-torn" look during active phases.
Occlusion behaviour:
Stars: heavily attenuated. The storm is opaque enough that stars behind the body layer drop to about 10 to 15 percent brightness. This reinforces the "you cannot see clearly in here" threat read.
Ships: ships inside the storm are hit by lightning light (the OmniLight3D already does this). Hulls stay visible because the gas alpha at ship height (y 0 to 3) stays below 0.5. The yellow glow flashes during lightning make ships momentarily very visible, which is a dramatic beat.
Hex UI: ZONE_TINT storm yellow alpha 0.13 (board.gd:325) stays visible.
Readability check: the storm reads as "dark, electrically active, dangerous" at a glance. The near-black body with yellow-violet lightning is distinct from the nebula's soft violet glow. At 4K the lightning ribbons are clearly visible (each spans several hexes, so hundreds of pixels). The pulse ensures the storm is never visually static.
Footprint honesty: same _mask approach. Storm patches are 8 to 14 hexes (battle.js:234) so they are large enough for the layered look to read. The feather at edges is slightly tighter (1.5 units) because storms should feel more defined and aggressive than nebulae.
2.3 Debris (id: debris, legend colour #d4bf86 tan)
Gameplay: ships inside are 10 points harder to hit (battle.js:650).
Readability requirement: reads as light physical cover, a field of floating wreckage. Not a gas, not a cloud. Solid objects you could bump into.
Current state: the living look (_debris at world_living.gd:469) already has 15 to 22 rocks plus 3 to 5 wreck pieces per hex (Job 10 settled counts), with four procedural wreck types (hull fragment, broken frame, truss, hull section; world_living.gd:549-556), tumbling slowly, plus a fine dust haze. This is a solid foundation. The issue is that in the screenshots the debris reads as generic and flat.
Look spec:
Layer stack:
Layer 1, physical objects: the rocks and wreck pieces. Keep current counts (15 to 22 rocks, 3 to 5 wreck pieces per hex). Enhancement: add more size variation. Currently the rocks come from the rock library; ensure at least 20 percent of rocks per hex are "large" (3 to 5 units) to create silhouette variety against the sky. The wreck pieces should have at least one per 3 hexes that is clearly larger than the rocks (6 to 9 units) to serve as a focal anchor.
Layer 2, dust haze: the existing fine dust haze (world_living.gd:10-12 header). Keep but refine: the dust should be a very low alpha (0.06 to 0.10) warm-tan wash at y from -2 to 4, giving the "field" cohesion without reading as gas. It should not look like a nebula. The colour is the legend tan (#d4bf86) at very low saturation (multiply by 0.4).
Layer 3, glint layer: new. Small bright specular points on the largest rock and wreck surfaces, catching the system sun direction. Not a separate geometry pass, but a shader term on the existing rock and wreck materials that adds a sharp specular highlight. This makes the field "sparkle" and reads as solid metal and stone, not smoke.
Colour behaviour:
Base: the rocks use their biome material (stone, ice, iron, carbon, crystal from ROCK_KINDS, battle.js:46-48). The wreck pieces are dark metal. The dust haze is the legend tan at low alpha.
Gradient: not applicable in the gas sense. Instead, the colour variation comes from the mix of rock biomes and the warm dust. The tan legend colour shows up in the dust haze and in the overall warm tone of the field.
Glow: no emissive glow. Debris is not luminous. The "torn edges still hot" from the current spec (world_living.gd:10-12) provide tiny orange-red emissive points on wreck pieces, which is good. Keep those but limit them to 2 or 3 per wreck piece so they read as detail, not as a light source.
Internal light: none. Debris has no internal light. The hot edges are emissive material, not a light source. Ships passing through may have their engine glow catch nearby rocks, but that is standard scene lighting, not a debris-specific system.
Motion:
Rocks and wreck pieces: keep the current slow tumble. Vary the tumble speed: small rocks rotate faster (period 20 to 40 seconds), large rocks slower (period 60 to 90 seconds), wreck pieces slowest (period 80 to 120 seconds). This size-based speed variation adds realism.
Dust haze: very slow drift, almost static. TIME offset at 20 percent the nebula gas rate. The dust should feel like it is hanging in space, not blowing.
Glint: the specular highlights shift as rocks tumble, which happens naturally with the existing rotation.
Occlusion behaviour:
Stars: rocks and wreck pieces occlude stars naturally by geometry (they are meshes). The dust haze dims stars behind it by about 80 percent (alpha 0.06 to 0.10 is very subtle). No special shader work needed.
Ships: ships inside debris are fully visible. The rocks do not cluster at ship height in a way that hides hulls. The _debris placement should keep a clear zone of radius 4 units around each ship hex centre so the ship itself is not buried in rocks.
Hex UI: ZONE_TINT debris tan alpha 0.12 (board.gd:325) stays.
Readability check: at 4K, a 5-unit rock is about 77 px across, clearly visible. A 9-unit wreck piece is about 139 px, a strong focal element. The field reads as "solid objects floating in space" not "smoke or gas." The sparkle from specular glints distinguishes it from the gas effects immediately.
Footprint honesty: debris fills its hexes with objects, so the footprint is inherently honest. The _mask is not needed in the same way as gas. The objects should be placed within the hex boundaries with a slight inward margin (1 unit) so nothing pokes into adjacent non-debris hexes.
2.4 Dead Star Gravity Well (board shape, not a TERRAIN_INFO kind)
Gameplay: VOID.deadstar set in genTerrain (battle.js:190-191), well position on battle state (battle.js:325). A debris ring forms around the well (battle.js:248). The well is a hazard centre and a board shape feature.
Readability requirement: reads as a gravitational hazard centre, a point of pull and danger. The in-board presentation is this job; the dead star itself in the sky is backdrop scope.
Current state: WELL_SHADER at world_living.gd:154 renders a ring of gravitational lensing and slow twist out to the pull range, with faint ripples running inward (header at world_living.gd:13-14).
Look spec:
Layer stack:
Layer 1, accretion disc hint: y from -2 to 1, a flat ring of very faint warm-to-hot colour (deep red at outer edge to orange-white near the well centre). Alpha 0.10 to 0.20. This is not a full accretion disc (that would be backdrop scope for the star itself) but a hint of matter being pulled in. The ring spans from the well centre out to the pull range.
Layer 2, lensing distortion: the existing WELL_SHADER gravitational lensing ring. Keep. This is the primary "gravity" read. Enhance: add a very subtle chromatic aberration at the lensing edge (shift red and blue channels by 1 to 2 pixels in opposite directions) to make the distortion more visible at 4K. This is a shader-only change.
Layer 3, infalling ripples: the existing faint ripples running inward (world_living.gd:13-14). Keep but make them slightly more visible: increase alpha from current to about 0.12 and add a faint warm tint so they read against the dark space. They should pulse: a new ripple spawns at the outer edge every 3 to 4 seconds and travels inward over 2 seconds, fading as it reaches the centre.
Layer 4, dust pull: a very sparse field of tiny particles (1 to 2 per hex in the well's influence radius) drifting slowly inward. These are the "matter being consumed" read. Alpha 0.15, size 0.5 to 1 unit. They spiral slightly as they move inward.
Colour behaviour:
Base: the well itself is dark, near-black at centre (it is a dead star, not a bright one). The surrounding influence zone shifts from cold blue-grey at the outer edge to warm orange near the centre, suggesting heating from compression.
Gradient: radial from centre outward. Centre: near-black. Mid range: deep red-orange. Outer edge: cold blue-grey fading to transparent. This radial gradient is the primary colour structure.
Glow: a very faint warm glow at the centre (alpha 0.08, orange-white) suggesting residual energy. Not bright. The dead star is dead, but not quite cold.
Internal light: minimal. The faint centre glow is the only emissive element. No point lights. The well is defined by distortion and motion, not by brightness.
Motion:
Lensing twist: keep the existing slow twist in WELL_SHADER. The twist rate should be very slow (one full rotation of the distortion pattern in about 30 seconds) to feel massive and inexorable.
Ripples: as described above, 3 to 4 second spawn cycle, 2 second travel time.
Dust pull: particles drift inward at about 1 unit per second at the outer edge, accelerating to 3 units per second near the centre. The spiral adds a slight tangential component (about 20 percent of the radial speed).
Accretion hint: very slow rotation (one revolution in about 45 seconds).
Occlusion behaviour:
Stars: the lensing distortion refracts stars near the well edge. Stars directly behind the well centre are fully occluded by the dark centre. Stars in the lensing ring shift position slightly (the existing WELL_SHADER handles this).
Ships: ships near the well are fully visible. The well's effects are below and around them, not obscuring them. The debris ring around the well (battle.js:248) uses the standard debris spec from section 2.3.
Hex UI: no hex tint for the well (it is not a TERRAIN_INFO kind). The well's presence is communicated by the visual effect itself.
Readability check: at 4K, the well's influence zone spans multiple hexes. The lensing ring is clearly visible as a distortion. The infalling ripples and dust particles provide motion that draws the eye to the centre. The colour gradient from cold edge to warm centre reads as "energy increasing toward the middle." A player looking at the board can immediately identify the well as "the dangerous centre."
Footprint honesty: the well is a board shape, not a hex-fill. Its visual influence is radial and does not need to conform to hex boundaries. The debris ring around it (battle.js:248) follows the standard debris placement rules.
Hand-off note: the dead star itself as a sky object (its visual at infinity, its glow, its silhouette) is backdrop scope from Job 10. This spec covers only the in-board gravitational influence and the accretion/ring presentation on the play surface.

SECTION 5: OWNER QUESTIONS
Q1: Nebula opacity and ship visibility balance.
The spec calls for nebula body alpha of 0.35 to 0.55 at centre. Higher opacity makes the concealment gameplay effect more visually convincing (you cannot see through it easily) but risks hiding ships inside. Lower opacity keeps ships clearly visible but weakens the "concealment" read.
Chair pick: start at 0.40 alpha at centre. This keeps ships readable (their hulls are about 60 percent visible through the gas) while still reading as a concealing cloud. The trade-off is that at 0.40 the nebula may feel slightly thin compared to Homeworld 3's denser volumes. We can push to 0.50 after the first playtest if ships still read well.
Q2: Storm glow colour: violet or yellow dominant.
The current storm glow is pure violet (0.3, 0.17, 0.62). The legend colour is yellow (#ffe866). The spec proposes a violet-to-yellow ramp where violet dominates at medium density and yellow appears at peaks and in lightning. The alternative is to make yellow dominant throughout the glow to match the legend colour more strongly.
Chair pick: violet-dominant with yellow at peaks. The dark violet body with yellow lightning reads as "electrically charged darkness," which matches the shield-drain gameplay. All-yellow glow would make the storm look like a bright cloud and lose the threatening darkness. The trade-off is that the legend yellow is less immediately visible in the gas body itself; the player learns to associate the dark cloud plus yellow lightning with the storm effect.
Q3: Star visibility through gas.
The spec dims stars to about 30 percent through nebula body and 10 to 15 percent through storm body. The alternative is to let stars show through more brightly (50 to 60 percent) for a more transparent, layered look, or to occlude them almost fully (5 to 10 percent) for a denser, more opaque feel.
Chair pick: 30 percent for nebula, 12 percent for storm. This keeps the space context visible (the player still reads "I am in space") while making the gas feel substantial. The trade-off is that the sky nebula bake (backdrop scope) loses some of its visual impact behind in-board gas. If Ryan wants the sky to show through more, we can raise to 50 percent, but the "just smoke" complaint suggests he wants the gas to feel more present, not less.
Q4: Dead star well visual intensity.
The spec adds chromatic aberration, stronger ripples, infalling dust, and an accretion hint to the well. This makes the well a strong visual focal point on the board. The alternative is a subtler treatment that keeps the well as a quiet background hazard rather than a visual centrepiece, so it does not compete with the ships and terrain effects for attention.
Chair pick: the stronger treatment. The well is a board shape, meaning the entire battle is arranged around it. It should be visible and dramatic. The dead star is the reason the board looks the way it does. The trade-off is that a bright, active well may draw the eye away from tactical ship positions. We mitigate this by keeping the well's colours dark and warm (not bright white) and its motion slow, so it is present but not distracting.
Q5: Old clouds path (today and holo looks) investment level.
Task 8 updates the old CLOUD_SHADER with the colour ramp. The alternative is to leave the old clouds as-is and focus all effort on the living path (look C, the default). The old clouds are only visible in "today" and "holo" looks, which are not the default.
Chair pick: do Task 8 but keep it minimal (port the colour ramp only, no layer separation or parallax). The trade-off is 3 to 5 hours of work for a non-default look. The reason to do it is that Ryan or other players may switch looks, and a flat-grey old cloud next to the improved living gas would look jarring. If time is tight, Task 8 can be deferred to a follow-up without blocking the main improvement.
