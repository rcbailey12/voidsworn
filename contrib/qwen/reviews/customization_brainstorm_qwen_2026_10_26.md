Blind Review: Ship Customization Brainstorm, Voidsworn
1. CANDIDATE VERDICTS
A. Authored Hull Variants. Verdict: SECONDARY.
Feel: moderate. Unlocking a variant reads more like buying a new chassis than wrenching on the one you have. It scratches the "new toy arrived" itch, not the "I built this with my hands" itch. Fun: the decision is real but front-loaded; you pick a variant once and live with it, so it adds a choice at the moment of unlock and very little after. Balance cost: low, and this is the candidate's strongest asset. Each variant is its own hull row, so the existing simulation pipeline (roughly 19,500 bot battles, admiral win rates currently spread 57 to 89 percent in sector 1) can absorb them with no new combinatorial explosion. Build cost: moderate. Each variant needs a visual pass (even if it is just a different weapon mount silhouette and a stat card), a data row, and a faction arc unlock gate. Enemy AI does not need to change because variants behave like hulls. Verdict reasoning: this is already inside the project's own plan, it feeds the content pipeline cleanly, and it keeps balance simulation tractable. It does not, by itself, deliver the mech bay fantasy the owner named. It is a strong supporting layer, not the headline.
B. Dock Refits with a Nemesis Clock Cost. Verdict: SECONDARY, conditional on scope.
Feel: good. The image of limping into a shipyard, swapping a torpedo rack for a lance battery while the Nemesis clock ticks louder, is exactly the kind of pressured tinkering that makes a dock feel like a place and not a menu. Fun: the clock cost converts what would be free optimization into a genuine gamble. "Do I fix my loadout now, or do I take the risk of the Nemesis arriving one node sooner?" That is decision weight, not spreadsheet time, as long as swaps stay limited (one weapon pod, one module). Balance cost: manageable if the swap space is small. The simulation cannot exhaustively cover every combination, but if swaps are restricted to one pod and one module per dock visit, the combinatorial surface stays within a range that targeted spot-check sims can cover. Build cost: moderate. Needs a dock UI, visual mount swapping for the changed pod, and the Nemesis clock integration. Enemy AI needs no change if swaps are bounded. The risk is scope creep into candidate E territory. Keep the slot count low and the clock cost real, and this works as a complement to something deeper.
C. Flagship Only Layered Customization. Verdict: PRIMARY.
Feel: strong, and the Rogue Trader comparison is not just marketing. In Rogue Trader (Owlcat, 2023), your voidship is present in every space battle, carries your officer crew, and its customization is the single deepest mechanical expression of your build. The rest of the fleet is contextual. The player feels ownership because the ship accumulates history: scars, named officers, chosen weapons. Voidsworn's flagship already accumulates scars, hosts the admiral, anchors Hegemony formation sync, and is the Nemesis duel target. The fantasy infrastructure is already there. Fun: layered (pod swap plus module plus section tuning) gives three distinct decision axes on one hull, which is enough depth to feel like a bay session without becoming a pre-battle spreadsheet for twenty ships. Balance cost: the lowest of any candidate that adds real customization. One hull out of roughly twenty on the board can be deeply tuned without breaking the simulation pipeline. You sim the flagship's configuration space in isolation; the other nineteen ships remain fixed hull rows. Build cost: bounded. One ship needs visual mount variants, one ship needs the UI, enemy AI only needs to handle one customized adversary. This is the cheapest way to deliver the most felt customization per unit of development effort.
D. Section Tuning for Every Ship. Verdict: SECONDARY, as a light layer only.
Feel: this is the most direct BattleTech armour-allocation itch. Sliding fixed points between prow, mid, and engine, deciding "do I want thicker prow armour for the broadside exchange or more engine thrust to control range," is tactile and legible. Fun: the danger is real. If this is available on all twenty ships before every battle, it becomes a pre-fight spreadsheet. The standing rule says decision weight over fleet size, but twenty ships times three sections times a slider each is not decision weight; it is chore time. Balance cost: tricky. Section tuning shifts stats without changing hull identity, so the simulation needs to model stat distributions rather than fixed hull rows. That is doable but adds a dimension to every sim run. Build cost: low on visuals (no new models, just stat shifts), moderate on UI (a tuning panel per ship). My read: this is a fine light layer if it is dock-only, reversible, and capped at one or two points of shift. If it becomes a deep per-ship system, it will eat the fun budget.
E. Full Open Hardpoint Lab. Verdict: REJECT.
Feel: this is the most literal translation of the BattleTech MechLab (Harebrained Schemes / Paradox, 2018), and I respect that the pack includes it honestly rather than as a strawman. In BattleTech, the MechLab works because the game is built around it: four mechs, slow pace, every ton and every hardpoint matters, and the campaign gives you time between fights to tinker. Voidsworn is a fleet tactics roguelite with roughly twenty ships, a Nemesis clock pressuring tempo, and a simulation-led balance process. Fun: for a subset of players this is peak fun. For the median player it is a forty-minute pre-battle loadout screen, which is the exact opposite of the cinematic, high-quality space battle feel the project targets. Balance cost: fatal. The pack states plainly that free-form loadout combinations cannot be exhaustively simmed. With 25 weapons across multiple hardpoint classes on 20 ships, the combinatorial surface is unbounded relative to the current pipeline. Admiral win rates currently span 57 to 89 percent in sector 1 and 38 to 98 percent at bosses; open hardpoint swapping would widen those spreads unpredictably and make the simulation baseline meaningless. Build cost: enormous. Every weapon needs a visual mount on every hull's hardpoint, enemy AI needs to handle arbitrary player loadouts, and the Nemesis adaptation system needs to respond to configurations it was never designed to model. I cannot verify whether a reduced version of this (say, two open hardpoints instead of all) would be viable. UNSURE. But as stated, full open lab: reject.
2. THE CENTRAL QUESTION
Does flagship-only layered customization (candidate C) do enough of the mech bay job on its own?
Yes, with one condition.
The condition: the rest of the fleet must still get a light, low-effort touch (section tuning at dock, or a single module swap) so the player feels like they are managing a bay with one prized machine in the center, not ignoring nineteen ships. Without that, the fleet becomes scenery and the flagship becomes a separate game mode bolted onto a fleet game.
The comparable where it worked: Rogue Trader (Owlcat, 2023). Your voidship is the only ship you directly customize. You choose its weapon batteries, its macro-cannon loadout, its crew officer posts (which function like module slots, granting the ship active and passive abilities). Every space battle features it. You name it. It accumulates damage and repair history across the campaign. The player feels: "That is MY ship. I chose those lances. I posted Yrliet on the bridge. Those scars on the hull are from the battle at Footfall." The surrounding escort fleet is auto-resolved or lightly managed. The fantasy is carried entirely by one hull because that hull is always present, always narratively central, and always the vessel through which the player's choices are expressed. Voidsworn's flagship is in the same structural position: always present, carries the admiral, accumulates scars, is the Nemesis rivalry target, anchors Hegemony formation. The fantasy rails are already laid.
The comparable where concentrated customization did NOT carry the fantasy, and fleet-wide tinkering was the point: Starsector (Fractal Softworks, in development since 2011, stable releases ongoing). In Starsector, you fit every ship in your fleet. You choose hull mods, weapon loadouts, fighter wings, and officer skills per ship. The fantasy IS the fleet. Walking through your fleet screen and tuning each destroyer, each cruiser, each carrier, is the core loop. If you locked customization to the flagship in Starsector, the game would collapse because the player's identity is "fleet commander who builds a fleet," not "captain of one amazing ship." The player feels: "I built this task force. That Medusa has the right PD loadout to screen my Onslaught. That Astral's fighter complement covers my flank." That is a different fantasy. Voidsworn's fantasy, as described in the pack, leans more toward Rogue Trader (small fleet, cinematic, one flagship at the center, admirals as characters) than Starsector (sandbox fleet building). The pack's own feel targets (cinematic space battles, Star Wars film drama, Rogue Trader voidship battles) all point to the concentrated model.
The second comparable where it worked, for reinforcement: FTL: Faster Than Light (Subset Games, 2012). One ship. All customization (weapons, drones, crew, subsystems) lives on that one ship. The player feels ownership of a single vessel and its crew. The fantasy is "my ship, my crew, my choices," and it sustains an entire game. The flagship in Voidsworn does not need to carry the WHOLE game the way the Kestrel does in FTL, because there is a fleet around it. But it needs to carry the customization fantasy, and it can.
What the player feels in the Rogue Trader / FTL model: pride, ownership, a sense of accumulated history. "I remember when I swapped the macro-cannon for a lance battery before the finale." What the player feels in the Starsector / BattleTech model: craftsmanship, breadth, the satisfaction of a well-tuned machine shop. Both are valid. Voidsworn's stated feel targets and its structural design (admirals ride flagships, scars accumulate on flagships, Nemesis duels target flagships, roughly 20 ships on board so per-ship depth must be bounded) all point toward the first model. The one condition (light fleet touch) prevents the second model's players from feeling entirely locked out.
3. INTERACTION RISKS
I am ranking candidate C (flagship-only layered customization) highest, with a light section-tuning layer (D, scoped down) as a companion. Here are the three worst interactions for C.
Risk 1: Nemesis adaptation to the player's most-used weapon kind.
The pack states the Nemesis adapts to the player's most used weapon kind. If the flagship has a swappable weapon pod, the player's obvious counter-strategy is: fight three battles with lances, let the Nemesis adapt to lances, then dock and swap to torpedoes before the Nemesis fight. The adaptation becomes a thing the player routes around rather than a pressure they must solve. This is a REAL problem, not a paper one, because it directly undermines the Nemesis system's purpose (making the player's success generate future danger) and turns the flagship's swappable pod into a Nemesis-kill button. Cheapest guardrail: the Nemesis adaptation tracks the player's most-used weapon kind across the last three to five battles, not the current loadout. A swap at dock does not reset the adaptation memory. Additionally, if candidate B's clock cost is applied, a major weapon swap ticks the Nemesis clock, so dodging the adaptation costs tempo. This keeps the adaptation honest and the swap meaningful without adding new systems.
Risk 2: Hegemony flagship anchor and formation sync.
The pack states Hegemony formation sync anchors on the flagship, and that Hegemony's loss condition is losing formation sync without the flagship. If section tuning (the layer within C) lets the player shift engine thrust or prow armour on the flagship, the flagship's speed and positioning change. If formation sync is keyed to the flagship's position or movement speed, a tuned flagship could either drag the formation into bad positions or race ahead and break sync unintentionally. This is a REAL problem for Hegemony players specifically, because their faction identity is mechanically tied to flagship behaviour. Cheapest guardrail: formation sync keys to the flagship's grid position, not its speed stat. Section tuning can change how fast the flagship gets to a position, but the formation reads where it IS, not how it moves. This requires no new mechanic, just a design rule that sync reads position, and it should be documented so Hegemony players are not surprised.
Risk 3: Faction weapon identity dilution.
Six factions have distinct weapon traits (Hegemony plasma in light blue, Chorus bio, Ithar cannot Brace, Deathless has no shields). If the flagship's swappable weapon pod can mount any weapon kind, a Hegemony flagship could mount Chorus bio weapons or Ithar gauss, diluting the visual and mechanical identity that makes factions legible. This is a REAL problem, because faction identity is described in the pack as both visual and mechanical, and the flagship is the most visible ship in the fleet. Cheapest guardrail: the swappable pod is restricted to the flagship's own faction weapon list plus one "captured tech" slot that opens only after a specific campaign milestone (for example, after the first Nemesis encounter or after capturing a ship from another faction). This preserves faction identity in the normal case and makes cross-faction weapons feel earned and rare rather than default. UNSURE whether the existing capture economy already gates cross-faction hardware in a way that would make this redundant; the pack describes strip-and-ransom but does not specify whether captured weapons transfer to the player's inventory.
4. MY HYBRID
I would propose a system I am calling "Flagship Bay with Fleet Touch." It is candidate C as the core, with a scoped-down D as the fleet layer, and A landing as planned in the background. It is not any single candidate unmodified.
What can be swapped, and on which ships:
Flagship (one ship, the admiral's ship):
One swappable weapon pod. The pod is drawn from the flagship's faction weapon list plus one captured-tech slot (see Risk 3 guardrail). Swapping the pod requires a dock or shipyard node.
One module slot. Modules already exist as equippable ship items. The flagship gets one slot that can be changed at dock. This is not a new system; it is a new permission.
Section tuning: the player may shift up to two fixed points between prow, mid, and engine. For example, move one point from engine thrust to prow armour, or one point from mid shields to engine speed. Reversible at any dock. This is the BattleTech armour-allocation itch, scoped to two points so it stays a meaningful choice and not a solver.
All other ships in the fleet (nineteen or so):
Section tuning only, same rules: up to two points shifted between prow, mid, and engine, at dock, reversible. No weapon swaps. No module swaps. This is the light fleet touch that keeps the player from feeling like the other ships are props.
Authored hull variants (candidate A) land as planned: one or two variants per hull, unlocked through faction arcs, simmed as their own rows. These are not player-driven swaps; they are content unlocks that give the fleet variety over a campaign.
Where and when:
All swaps and tuning happen at shipyard nodes on the campaign map, or at dock-type map nodes. Not between sectors in open space. Not during battle. Not from a menu between fights. The player must route to a dock, which means the route planner already values these nodes, and choosing to dock is itself a decision (you spend a node on tinkering instead of on a boon, a relic, or a battle).
If candidate B's Nemesis clock cost is applied: swapping the flagship's weapon pod ticks the Nemesis clock by one increment. Module swaps and section tuning do not tick the clock. This keeps the pressure real for the big change and free for the small ones.
What it costs:
Weapon pod swap: credits (tuned, but in the range of a minor refit cost already in the game) plus one Nemesis clock tick.
Module swap: credits only, no clock tick.
Section tuning: free, but only at dock. The cost is opportunity: you spent a map node on docking instead of something else.
Hull variants (A): unlocked through faction arc progression, no credit cost. They are rewards, not purchases.
What is explicitly NOT swappable:
Hull identity. You cannot turn a destroyer into a cruiser. The hull is the hull.
Weapon hardpoint count or class. If a hull has two battery mounts and one lance mount, you cannot change those numbers. You can only swap what goes INTO the pod on the flagship, and the pod's class is fixed by the hull.
Faction affiliation. A Hegemony flagship does not become a Chorus flagship by mounting a bio weapon in the captured-tech slot. The ship's faction, shield trait, and loss condition do not change.
Admiral and Command abilities. These ride the flagship and are not swappable. You cannot reassign an admiral mid-run.
Enemy ships. Captured ships join the fleet as-is (strip-and-ransom for consequences, auto-scrap if fleet is maxed). You do not customize captured ships. They come with their hull's default loadout.
What this is not:
This is not candidate E. There is no open hardpoint lab. There is no free weapon-kind swapping on all ships. The combinatorial surface stays small enough that the simulation pipeline can handle it: the flagship has a bounded pod list (its faction's weapons plus a small captured-tech set), one module slot, and a two-point section shift. That is a few hundred configurations at most, all simmable. The fleet ships have a two-point section shift, which is a small stat perturbation on existing hull rows.
5. SEQUENCING
Must be decided or reserved NOW, during the Godot migration, before cutover:
Ship data schema must separate hull base stats from section allocations and from weapon mount assignments. If the current data structure stores a ship as a flat object ("this hull has these weapons, these stats"), and the Godot migration cements that schema into save files, then future section tuning and pod swaps will require a save migration or a hacky overlay. The schema should store: hull identity, faction, base stat block, section allocation as a separate sub-structure (prow points, mid points, engine points), and weapon mounts as a list of slot objects (slot ID, slot class, currently equipped weapon ID). This costs almost nothing to do now during the data layer rebuild and costs a lot to retrofit. I am confident this is a real now-item because the Godot migration is the one time the data layer is already open.
Save format must not hardcode weapon-to-hull bindings as immutable. If a save file writes "Ship 7 is Hull 12 with Weapon 4 in slot 1 and Weapon 9 in slot 2" as a flat string, future pod swaps break save compatibility. The save should reference slot objects that can be reassigned. This is the same point as above applied to serialization. Same reasoning: the migration is the window.
Visual mount point conventions. If the flagship (or any ship) will ever show a different weapon model in a pod, the 3D or 2D scene needs a named mount node (a Socket3D, a Marker2D, an attachment point in the scene tree) where the weapon visual attaches. If the current art pipeline welds weapon visuals into the hull mesh, swapping them later requires re-rigging every hull. The convention (one named mount node per pod slot, weapon visuals are separate scenes parented to the mount) should be established now, even if only one ship uses it. This is cheap now and expensive later. I cannot verify whether the current art pipeline already uses separate weapon meshes or welded meshes. UNSURE. If they are already separate, this item is free. If they are welded, it needs to be flagged before the migration locks the pipeline.
Can safely wait until after cutover with no loss:
The actual customization UI (dock screen, tuning sliders, pod swap interface). This is a feature layer on top of the data schema and can be built any time after the migration stabilizes.
Nemesis adaptation logic changes (tracking weapon kind over multiple battles instead of current loadout). This is a rules change in the Nemesis system, not a data structure change. It can be patched in without touching saves or schemas.
Enemy AI responses to player loadout variety. Since the hybrid keeps the combinatorial surface small, enemy AI does not need to handle arbitrary configurations. Any needed adjustments can be made when the feature is prototyped.
Hull variant content (candidate A). The story audit trigger already waits on variant rows existing. The rows can be authored and inserted post-cutover without schema changes, as long as the schema from item 1 above is in place.
Section tuning implementation. The tuning logic is a UI and rules layer. The data schema (section allocations as a sub-structure) needs to exist, but the actual tuning interface and balance pass can wait.
Captured-tech slot unlock conditions and cross-faction weapon rules. These are design and content decisions that depend on how the capture economy lands in playtesting. No schema dependency.
OVERALL VERDICT
The single customization addition I would make first is candidate C, flagship-only layered customization, implemented as the hybrid I specced above: one swappable weapon pod, one module slot, and two-point section tuning on the flagship, plus two-point section tuning on every other ship as a light fleet touch, all gated to dock nodes, with a Nemesis clock tick on the weapon pod swap. It is the cheapest path to the most felt customization per unit of build effort, it fits the project's stated feel targets (Rogue Trader's one-ship fantasy, BattleTech's tactile tuning, cinematic focus), it keeps the simulation-led balance process intact because the combinatorial surface is one ship's bounded configuration space, and it leverages infrastructure the game already has (scars, admirals, Nemesis duels, formation sync). The one addition I would never make is candidate E, the full open hardpoint lab. It breaks the simulation pipeline that the project depends on for balance, it turns a cinematic turn-based fleet game into a pre-battle spreadsheet, it multiplies visual and AI build cost across every hull and every weapon combination, and it solves a fantasy (I am a MechLab technician fitting forty tons of hardware) that is fundamentally at odds with the fantasy Voidsworn is actually building (I am an admiral with a storied flagship and a fleet at my back, and the Nemesis is coming). The MechLab is a different game's heart. Voidsworn's heart is the bay where you walk up to YOUR ship, swap the pod, check the scars, and go fight.
