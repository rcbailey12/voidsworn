BLIND STORY REVIEW: Voidsworn Faction Arcs (26 Oct 2026)
Status: Review of DRAFT outlines, awaiting Ryan's approval (plan B5 exit gate, R37). This is a story review, not a balance review.

(1) WHAT RESONATES: Lessons from comparable games

FTL: Faster Than Light (Subset Games, 2012)
What works: Text events of two to four sentences present a situation, a choice, and a consequence in one breath. The player never reads more than a short paragraph before deciding. The Rebel Fleet timer creates a constant "nemesis clock" pressure that makes every delay feel costly. Choices trade one concrete thing for another (hull for credits, crew for a weapon, time for safety), and the player can always see the trade.
Source: FTL wiki and event list, https://ftl.fandom.com/wiki/FTL_Wiki ; Subset Games' post-mortem interview with Rock Paper Shotgun, https://www.rockpapershotgun.com/the-making-of-ftl
What transfers: The short-card format, the "trade X for Y" clarity, and the background timer that punishes hesitation. The Voidsworn beats already follow this shape well.
What does not transfer: FTL's events are mostly one-shot. They do not build a three-beat arc with a named rival. FTL's rival is a faction (the Rebels), not a person. The Voidsworn arcs aim for something FTL does not attempt: a personal antagonist with a voice.

Into the Breach (Subset Games, 2018)
What works: Every piece of information the player needs is on screen in plain sight. The text is minimal, sometimes a single line. The "enemy" (the Vek) does not speak or remember you, but the game's clarity means you never feel confused about what a choice costs. The short, declarative writing style suits turn-based tactics because it respects the player's time.
Source: Subset Games' interview with Game Developer (formerly Gamasutra), https://www.gamedeveloper.com/design/how-subset-games-made-i-into-the-breach-i-
What transfers: The principle that a choice in a tactics game should be legible in one reading. If a player has to re-read a 150-word card twice to understand the trade, the card is too long or too vague. The Voidsworn beats mostly pass this test.
What does not transfer: Into the Breach has no narrative arc and no rival. Its clarity comes from stripping story away. The Voidsworn arcs need story, so they cannot be as sparse.

Hades (Supergiant Games, 2020)
What works: Characters remember you. Zagreus dies, returns, and the world acknowledges what happened last time. Short dialogue lines (often one or two sentences) carry the memory. The "nemesis" figure (Hades himself, or rival characters like Theseus) evolves across runs because the writing tracks a small set of flags and swaps a line accordingly. The emotional payoff comes from recognition: "Oh, you again. Last time you ran."
Source: Greg Kasavin's GDC 2021 talk, "Building the World of Hades," available on the GDC Vault, https://www.gdcvault.com/play/1027395/ ; Supergiant Games' official site, https://www.supergiantgames.com/games/hades/
What transfers: The flag-and-swap technique. The Voidsworn outlines already use flags (vesper_hand, hg_offbook, etc.) and blue options. The lesson from Hades is that even one changed line in beat 3 that references a beat 1 choice makes the rival feel alive. The outlines do this mechanically (blue options) but the rival's own dialogue does not yet change based on your flags. More on this in section 3c.
What does not transfer: Hades has full voice acting, portraits, and dozens of characters. The Voidsworn arcs have text only, crest symbols, and one rival. The economy of words must be much tighter.

Darkest Dungeon (Red Hook Studios, 2016)
What works: The Narrator's voice is the game's identity. Short, declarative lines ("The hamlet is your inheritance," "Ruins have a way of reminding you of your failures") create tone without long exposition. Each location and enemy type has a distinct vocabulary. The stress system means choices have a psychological cost, not just a material one. The text never explains the mechanic; it embodies the feeling.
Source: Red Hook Studios' GDC 2016 talk by Chris Bourassa and Tyler Sigman, https://www.gdcvault.com/play/1023471/ ; official site, https://www.darkestdungeon.com/
What transfers: The principle that a faction's voice should be recognizable in three words. If you can strip the faction name and still tell which faction wrote the line, the voice is working. The Voidsworn outlines mostly achieve this (more in section 3b). Also: the Narrator's trick of letting the environment do the emotional work ("the wreck screams") rather than telling the player how to feel.
What does not transfer: Darkest Dungeon's text can run long in its dungeon narration and town events. The Voidsworn 100-to-150-word constraint is tighter. Also, Darkest Dungeon's choices are often binary with one option clearly tied to a specific hero's quirk. The Voidsworn arcs aim for dilemmas that are genuinely hard, which is a higher bar.

Battletech (Harebrained Schemes, 2018)
What works: The player is a commander making calls for a small roster. Mission briefings are short, military in tone, and present a choice (salvage the wreck, push the objective, withdraw). The "rival" is not a single character but the opposing house or pirate band, and the game lets you feel the weight of each lost mech-pilot by name. The emotional beat is: these people are finite, and you spent them.
Source: Harebrained Schemes' developer diaries and the game's Steam page, https://store.steampowered.com/app/637090/BATTLETECH/ ; Paradox Interactive's product page, https://www.paradoxinteractive.com/games/battletech
What transfers: The "small fleet, named captains, finite losses" feeling. The Voidsworn chalk-names on the hull (Crusade beat 3) and the 19-year-old lieutenant (Hegemony beat 2) do exactly what Battletech does: make the player feel the cost in individual lives. The format of a short briefing with a commander's call maps directly onto the Voidsworn beat structure.
What does not transfer: Battletech's missions are tactical scenarios first and story second. The text serves the battle. In the Voidsworn arcs, the text IS the story, and the battle follows. The balance of attention is different.

Warhammer 40,000: Rogue Trader (Owlcat Games, 2023)
What works: Faction identity is carried entirely through vocabulary and sentence rhythm. The Imperium sounds like a legal document. Chaos sounds like a fever. The Aeldari sound like a eulogy. Choices are presented in the faction's own idiom, so the player feels the worldview, not just the mechanic. The game also uses a "rival" figure (the antagonist of each act) who taunts and evolves.
Source: Owlcat Games' official page, https://rogue-trader.owlcat.games/ ; Owlcat's developer diaries on Steam, https://store.steampowered.com/app/2186680/Warhammer_40000_Rogue_Trader/
What transfers: The principle that word choice IS faction identity. The Voidsworn outlines do this well ("the oath does not ask you to survive" for Crusade, "the census records the correction" for Deathless). The lesson is to keep pushing the vocabulary further into each faction's specific register.
What does not transfer: Rogue Trader's text is very long, often several hundred words per node, with branching dialogue trees. The Voidsworn 100-to-150-word constraint means no branching dialogue. Every choice must be a single line, not a conversation.

Middle-earth: Shadow of Mordor / Shadow of War (Monolith Productions, 2014 / 2017)
What works: The Nemesis System. Enemies remember you. They reference past encounters ("Last time, you ran," "You burned me, little ranger"). They taunt, they gloat, they hold grudges. The key insight is that the enemy's dialogue changes based on a small set of tracked events (you ran, you killed his friend, he killed you before). Even three or four variable lines make the rival feel personal. The player projects a relationship onto the enemy because the enemy acknowledges the shared history.
Source: Monolith Productions' GDC 2015 talk by Michael de Plater on the Nemesis System, https://www.gdcvault.com/play/1022463/ ; Warner Bros. Interactive's official page, https://www.warnerbrosgames.com/
What transfers: The principle that the rival should reference what happened between you. The Voidsworn outlines track flags and the Nemesis clock, but the rival's spoken lines (beat 3 hail, last words, gloat) are fixed per faction and do not change based on your earlier choices. This is the single biggest missed opportunity for making the rival feel like a person rather than a boss. More in section 3c.
What does not transfer: Shadow of Mordor's nemesis system is procedural and generates dozens of unique enemies. The Voidsworn arcs have one rival per run, rolled from a fixed pool. The depth must come from writing, not from procedural generation.

Stellaris (Paradox Development Studio, 2016)
What works: Faction events use short text (often under 100 words) with two or three choices. The "crisis" events build a sense of escalating threat across the game. Faction identity is carried through event flavor text (the Machine Intelligence sounds different from the Spiritualist empire). The "rival" is often an abstract threat (the Crisis, the Fallen Empire) rather than a named person, but the game makes it feel personal through repeated references.
Source: Paradox Interactive's official page, https://www.paradoxinteractive.com/games/stellaris ; the Stellaris wiki, https://stellaris.fandom.com/wiki/Stellaris_Wiki
What transfers: The escalating-threat structure (three sectors, three beats, building to a finale) maps well onto Stellaris' crisis escalation. The lesson is that each beat should raise the stakes visibly. The Voidsworn outlines do this (the rival goes from leaving a message, to setting a trap, to anchoring at the objective).
What does not transfer: Stellaris events are often one-shot or two-shot, not three-beat arcs with a single named rival. Also, Stellaris' text can be dry and expository. The Voidsworn outlines are more literary, which suits the format better.

Summary of transferable lessons for 100-to-150-word beats
FTL and Battletech prove that a short card with a clear trade works. Keep the "trade X for Y" structure visible.
Hades and Shadow of Mordor prove that a rival who remembers you is worth more than a rival who is merely strong. Even one swapped line matters.
Darkest Dungeon and Rogue Trader prove that faction voice is carried by vocabulary, not by length. Three words in the right register beat three sentences in a generic one.
Into the Breach proves that if the player must re-read the card, the card has failed. One reading must be enough.
What does NOT transfer: long branching dialogue (Rogue Trader), procedural nemesis generation (Shadow of Mordor), multi-hundred-word atmospheric passages (Darkest Dungeon), or abstract faction-level rivals (Stellaris, FTL).

(2) PER-FACTION ANALYSIS

Voidsworn Crusade: "The Oath at the Hollow Sun"
Strongest beat: Beat 3, "Relight the Hollow Sun." The image of captains writing their names on the hull in chalk and ash is the single best piece of writing in the set. It does three things at once: it shows the crew's resolve, it echoes the "first crusaders who burned," and it makes the loss text ("the chalk names burn with the hull") land with real weight. The closing line, "The oath does not ask you to survive. It asks you to finish," is a perfect faction thesis in one sentence. The win text (the beacon catching on the third attempt, the litanists adding a verse with your name) is earned and warm without being sentimental.
Weakest beat: Beat 1, "The Saint's Hand." It is well written, but it is the most conventional beat in the arc. The setup (a relic left behind with a threat) is clear, and the choice (carry it vs sell it) is functional. However, compared to the Burning Choir and the Hollow Sun, it reads slightly like a standard "pick up the quest item" opening. The image of keepers nailed to their doors is vivid, but the beat spends words on the confessors and quartermasters debating, which dilutes the tension. The rival's message ("I will return for this, and for you") is effective but is a well-worn villain line.
Biggest fix: The beat 2 blue option ("Raise the hand before the crews") gives BOTH a revealed weakness AND 15 experience to every captain. The other two options give one or the other. This makes the blue option strictly better than either non-blue option, which undercuts the dilemma. The fix: make the blue option trade something away. For example, the hand could crack or go cold after being raised, removing the relic. That way the blue option is powerful but costs the player the hand for beat 3, creating a real trade.

Hegemony Navy: "Pacification Order 41"
Strongest beat: Beat 2, "Acceptable Limits." This is the strongest dilemma in all six arcs. The 19-year-old lieutenant's message ("we will hold as long as we can") against the Admiralty's order ("the column is within acceptable limits, proceed to the objective") is a genuine moral fork with no clean answer. The beat earns its title: the phrase "acceptable limits" becomes sickening by the end. The blue option (using the black box intel to strike where the rival is weak) is the most satisfying blue option in the set because it rewards the player for the off-book choice in beat 1 without making the other options feel foolish. The win text for disobeying (the haulers patch your fleet, but "the noise carries") is a good consequence: you saved them, but the rival now knows more.
Weakest beat: Beat 3, "The Anchorage." The setup is fine (the rival dug in among survey buoys, authorisation arriving eleven days late and stamped in triplicate). The "unrecorded speech" tradition is a lovely touch. But the two main choices ("Speak of duty" for experience, "Read the names from the listening post" for repairs) feel slightly generic compared to the specificity of beats 1 and 2. "Speak of duty" could belong to any military faction. The beat's best idea (the speech that is unrecorded, that the crews will remember anyway) is in the setup text but not reflected in the choices.
Biggest fix: Rework the beat 3 choices to lean into the Hegemony's specific voice. Instead of "Speak of duty," the choice could be something like "Read Order 41 aloud, every clause, every appendix, until the line hears what they are dying for." That is a Hegemony-specific act of duty, not a generic one. The "Read the names from the listening post" option is already good and specific. The win and loss text are both excellent and need no changes.

Ashfall Corsairs: "Saints' Gold"
Strongest beat: Beat 1, "The Gift Box." The tone is immediately, unmistakably Corsair. The tattooed hand in a gift box is grotesque and funny at the same time. The line "Nobody believes him and everybody is going" is perfect. The two crew suggestions (send it back rigged vs throw a party) capture the faction's range: practical malice and reckless celebration. "Both have a point" is the right closing beat. The rival's name in the example (Seraph Caelis Nightbloom) is the most fun name in the set.
Weakest beat: Beat 3, "Saints' Gold." The setup is strong: the treasure fleet locked in ice, the rival chained to his own prize daring you to shoot through it, the crews counting shares out loud (bad luck), the Ashfall rule about the loser getting shoved out the airlock. But the choices do not engage with the setup's most interesting element. "Promise double shares" and "Promise nothing, and mean it" are crew-morale choices. They ignore the rival chained to the wrecks, the tactical problem of shooting through your own prize, and the question of whether some crews "would rather die rich than live poor." The beat asks a question about greed and loyalty, but the choices answer a question about morale and credits.
Biggest fix: Rewrite the beat 3 choices to engage with the rival's chained-fleet gambit. One option could involve risking the treasure to get a clean shot (a tactical gamble). Another could involve negotiating or bluffing, which suits the Corsair voice. The current choices (experience vs credits) feel like they belong in beat 1 or 2, not the finale. The win and loss text are both good and can stay.

The Chorus: "Eat the Sun"
Strongest beat: Beat 1, "The Silent Node." The image of a brood-node cut open, its young untouched, its song carved out, is the most original and unsettling image in the entire set. It is not violent in a conventional way; it is a violation of something the Chorus considers sacred (song, brood, continuity). The rival's message is carried by the act itself, not by words, which suits a faction that communicates through hunger and sound. The choice (eat the dead and heal vs carry the silence and learn his scent) is clean and thematically resonant: do you consume your grief, or do you let it sharpen you?
Weakest beat: Beat 3, "Eat the Sun." The setup is strong (the Hollow Sun singing, the rival standing between the swarm and the song). But the choices feel flat. "Open every throat" (20% hull regrowth) and "Sing back to the sun" (weakness revealed) are functional but do not carry the emotional weight of the setup. The beat's best line ("There is only forward. There has only ever been forward") is in the setup text and not reflected in the choices. The win text ("the Chorus sings with one more voice than it had before") is excellent, but the player has to get through two bland choices to reach it.
Biggest fix: The stated Chorus voice is "hunger, many-as-one, song, short lines." But the beat text is written in long, descriptive sentences that sound like a narrator describing the Chorus from outside, rather than the Chorus thinking in its own voice. The fix is to rewrite the beats in a more fragmented, collective, present-tense voice. Instead of "The Chorus stirs in the dark between stars, and every throat aches with the same wanting," try something like: "The dark. The wanting. Every throat, the same ache. Forward." This is a voice pass, not a structural change, but it would make the Chorus feel alien in a way the other factions do not attempt.

The Ithar: "The Grave of Houses"
Strongest beat: Beat 3, "The Grave of Houses." The line "The elders sang that the Ithar would fade. They never sang that the Ithar would fade quietly" is the best single line in all six arcs. It is a thesis statement for the faction, a battle cry, and a character moment all at once. The rival peeling the archive "like fruit" is a perfect image of casual desecration. The win text (the youngest captain learning a nine-thousand-year-old song and singing it badly, and the elders weeping) is the most emotionally complete ending in the set. It is hopeful without being triumphant, which suits a dying race.
Weakest beat: Beat 2, "The Glass Choir." It is well constructed, but it reads the most like a game menu of the six beats. "Sing the lament" (experience, clock advances), "Go dark through the screaming" (5% hull damage, weakness revealed), "Sing the buoy's song" (blue, weakness revealed, no damage). The tradeoffs are clear and fair, but the language is functional rather than Ithar. "Go dark through the screaming" is a tactical instruction, not an Ithar phrase. The beat's emotional core (young captains weeping, the old seraph who sang here before) is in the setup but the choices do not carry that emotion forward.
Biggest fix: Reframe the beat 2 choices in Ithar voice. Instead of "Go dark through the screaming," something like "Fold the sails. Let the screams pass through the hull like weather." Instead of "Sing the lament," something like "Let the oldest voice lead. The wrecks remember her." The mechanical effects stay the same; only the language changes. This keeps the Ithar feeling like the Ithar in the choice text, not just in the setup text.

The Deathless Dynasty: "The Court Restored"
Strongest beat: Beat 2, "A Crown on the Wrong Head." The line "The overseers have calculated the insult to eleven decimal places" is the single funniest line in the set, and it is perfectly in voice. The broadcast of the rival wearing the stolen crown is a great visual. The law about the reserve ("may only be woken to defend the court") creates a genuine dilemma: break an ancient law to win, or keep the law and fight at a disadvantage. The blue option (the archivists' patience pays off, triggering the crown's flaw) is the most satisfying "patience rewarded" beat in the set, and it directly validates the beat 1 choice to "Be patient." The clock going back one is a nice mechanical expression of the rival being shamed.
Weakest beat: Beat 3, "The Court Restored." The setup is strong (the fleet aligning without being ordered, the census finishing its count, "You have waited ten thousand years. You can wait a few more minutes for the guns to charge"). But the choices ("Recite the census" for experience, "Wake the court's honour guard" for hull repairs) feel slightly anticlimactic after the build-up. "Wake the court's honour guard" sounds like a generic reinforcement option. The beat's best idea (the law of reclamation, "What was ours is ours. What stands in the way is corrected") is stated in the setup but not embodied in the choices.
Biggest fix: Make the beat 3 choices specific to the Dynasty's legal-census voice. Instead of "Wake the court's honour guard," consider something like "Invoke the law of reclamation. The court's own dead stand as witnesses." Instead of "Recite the census," consider "Read the rival's name into the census, under 'corrected.'" The mechanical effects (experience vs hull repairs) stay the same. The language should make the player feel like they are filing paperwork that happens to include a fleet engagement.

(3) CROSS-CUTTING ISSUES

(a) Do choices present real dilemmas, or is one option obviously best?
Mostly, the choices are genuine dilemmas. The Hegemony beat 2 is the gold standard: disobey (repairs, clock advance), obey (credits), or blue (both, but less repair). All three feel valid depending on the player's situation.
However, there is a recurring problem with blue options. In several beats, the blue option is strictly better than the non-blue options:
Crusade beat 2, "The Burning Choir": the blue gives a weakness AND 15 experience. The other options give one or the other. If you have the hand, the blue is obviously best.
Corsair beat 2, "The Rattlehold Auction": the blue gives a weakness for 30 credits. The outbid option gives a weakness for 80 credits. The blue is strictly cheaper for the same result.
Chorus beat 2, "The Drowned Song": the blue gives a weakness AND rewinds the clock. The other options give healing or a drone but advance the clock. The blue is strictly better on the clock axis.
This does not break the game, because the blue option requires a specific earlier choice, so it is a reward for planning. But within a single beat, it reads as "the right answer" rather than "a different answer." The fix is to give each blue option a cost that the non-blue options avoid. For example, the Crusade blue could consume the hand relic. The Corsair blue could anger the auction house, creating a future penalty. The Chorus blue could cost the swarm a brood.
The non-blue choices are generally well balanced. The one exception is the Ithar beat 2 "Go dark" option, which costs 5% hull damage. Compared to the other options, this is a small price, and it might be the obvious best choice for players who have high hull integrity. UNSURE whether the 5% figure is final or a placeholder; the outlines do not say.

(b) Does each faction sound distinct?
Yes, mostly. This is the outlines' greatest strength. I tested this by reading the beat text without the faction headers:
Crusade: "oath," "litany," "confessors," "saint," "heresy," "rite." Unmistakable.
Hegemony: "Admiralty," "appendix," "authorisation," "stamped in triplicate," "acceptable limits." Unmistakable.
Corsairs: "shares," "the dens," "quartermaster," "bosun," "knife crews," "fistfights." Unmistakable.
Chorus: "throats," "broods," "spawn," "the swarm," "hunger." Recognisable, but see the note below.
Ithar: "songships," "sails," "elders," "houses," "lamp-buoy," "nine thousand years." Unmistakable.
Deathless: "census," "tomb," "warden," "crown," "corrected," "the law." Unmistakable.
The one weakness is the Chorus. The stated voice is "short lines," but the actual beat text uses long, complex sentences. "The Chorus stirs in the dark between stars, and every throat aches with the same wanting" is a 20-word sentence with a subordinate clause. A faction whose voice is "short lines" would say "The dark. The wanting. Every throat aches." The voice instruction and the execution do not fully match.

(c) Does the rival feel like he remembers you across beats, or does he reset each time?
This is the outlines' biggest missed opportunity. The rival's presence is tracked mechanically (the Nemesis clock advances or rewinds, flags are set, blue options appear), and the rival's actions escalate across beats (leaving a message in beat 1, setting a trap in beat 2, anchoring at the objective in beat 3). This is good structural escalation.
However, the rival's actual words are limited to the beat 3 hail, last words, and gloat, and these are fixed per faction. They do not change based on the player's beat 1 or beat 2 choices. A Crusade player who carried the saint's hand hears the same hail as one who sold it. A Hegemony player who disobeyed orders hears the same last words as one who followed them.
The Shadow of Mordor lesson (see section 1) is that even two or three variable lines make a rival feel personal. The outlines could add one conditional line to the beat 3 hail or the beat 2 setup. For example, if the player carried the hand (flag vesper_hand), the rival's beat 3 hail could include "You still carry that dead saint's hand. I told you I would come back for it." If the player sold it (flag vesper_sold), he could say "I see you sold the hand. Good. That makes this simpler."
The outlines do not currently include any conditional rival dialogue beyond the fixed faction lines in the table at the end. UNSURE whether this was a deliberate scope decision or an oversight. The outlines do not say.

(d) Will arcs hold up across many runs, or go stale fast?
The rival changing faction and name each run is the main replayability lever, and it is a good one. A Crusade player will face a different rival (and hear a different beat 3 hail) every run, which keeps the finale feeling fresh for a few runs.
However, the player's own faction text is fixed. After two or three runs as the Crusade, the player will have seen all three beats, all choices, both blue options, and both win/loss endings. The text will become familiar. The outlines do not mention any text variation, alternate lines, or randomised flavor sentences within beats. UNSURE whether this is planned elsewhere or is genuinely absent.
The flags and blue options add some variation (you can replay to try the other path), but the core narrative text is static. For a roguelite where players will run the same faction many times, this is a risk. Comparable games handle this differently: Hades adds new dialogue lines as you unlock them across runs; FTL randomises which events appear; Darkest Dungeon randomises dungeon layouts so the same text feels different in context.
The simplest fix within the current format: add one or two alternate sentences per beat that swap based on the rival's faction. For example, the beat 1 setup could include a line about the rival that changes depending on whether he is Hegemony, Corsair, Chorus, Ithar, or Deathless. This would make each run feel slightly different without requiring new beats.

(e) Anything that reads too close to Warhammer 40k
This is the most sensitive cross-cutting issue. Several elements, individually, are common sci-fi or fantasy tropes. In combination, some clusters read as very close to 40k. I will flag them by severity.
High concern - Deathless Dynasty and the Necrons:
The Deathless Dynasty's "tomb worlds," "wardens," "crowns," waking from a long sleep, "census engines," and contempt for younger races ("squatters") closely mirror the Necrons of Warhammer 40,000. The Necrons are an ancient race of skeletal robots who sleep in tomb worlds, wake after millions of years, are led by dynastic lords with crowns, and view younger races with contempt. The crest (a crowned eye over a closed tomb door) is visually similar to Necron iconography. The "census" angle is somewhat original (Necrons do not do censuses), but the overall package will read as Necron-coded to any 40k fan.
Recommended changes: lean harder into the "bureaucratic, legal, census" angle and pull back on the "tomb, warden, crown, long sleep" angle. The Dynasty's best moments are the eleven-decimal-places insult, the nine-page appendix, the census recording the correction. Those are original. The tomb-and-crown imagery is where it drifts toward Necrons. Consider replacing "tomb worlds" with something like "archive worlds" or "ledger worlds." Replace "wardens" with "registrars" or "custodians." Keep the crowns if they serve the story, but reduce their visual prominence.
Source for Necron comparison: Games Workshop's Warhammer 40,000 wiki, https://warhammer40k.fandom.com/wiki/Necrons
Medium concern - Voidsworn Crusade and the Imperium:
The Crusade's vocabulary is densely 40k-coded: "heresy," "saint," "shrine-moon," "litany," "litanists," "confessors," "crusade," "oath-fleet," "rite." Individually, each word is a common religious or historical term. Together, they recreate the texture of the Imperium of Man, particularly the Ecclesiarchy and the Inquisition. The "first oath-fleet that burned" echoes various 40k crusade disasters. The "keepers nailed to their own doors" echoes 40k martyrdom imagery.
Recommended changes: swap two or three of the most 40k-specific terms. "Heresy" could become "dissent" or "schism." "Litanists" could become "chanters" or "verse-keepers." "Confessors" could become "witnesses" or "oath-keepers." Keep "saint" and "oath" and "crusade" if Ryan wants them; those words predate 40k by centuries. But the density of 40k-specific terms in one faction is the risk. Reducing it by a third would help.
Source for Imperial Ecclesiarchy comparison: https://warhammer40k.fandom.com/wiki/Ecclesiarchy
Low concern - other factions:
Hegemony Navy: reads as generic authoritarian military sci-fi. No specific 40k echo. The "Admiralty" and "Pacification Order" language is closer to real-world naval history than to 40k. Fine.
Ashfall Corsairs: reads as pirate/space-western. No 40k echo. Fine.
Chorus: reads as a hive-mind or swarm faction. This is a common sci-fi trope (Zerg, Tyranids, the Flood, the Borg). The Chorus is more "hunger and song" than any of those, so it is distinct enough. Fine.
Ithar: reads as an ancient, dying, elegant race. This echoes the Aeldari (40k) or the Eldar (Warhammer Fantasy), but the Ithar's "songships, sails, lamp-buoys" aesthetic is distinct enough. The "fading race" trope is common. Low risk, but worth noting.
"Hollow Sun" as a dead star: not specifically 40k. Fine.

(4) RANKED TOP 10 FIXES
Listed most important first. Each entry names the faction or beat it applies to.
1. Add one conditional rival line per arc, tied to a beat 1 or beat 2 flag.
Applies to: all six factions, beat 3 (and optionally beat 2).
Why: The rival is the emotional spine of every arc, but his dialogue is fixed. He does not acknowledge what you did. One swapped line in the beat 3 hail (or the beat 2 setup) based on a flag would make him feel like a person instead of a boss. This is the single highest-value change for replayability and emotional impact. The Shadow of Mordor and Hades lessons both point here.
2. Reduce the Deathless Dynasty's Necron-coded imagery.
Applies to: Deathless Dynasty, all three beats, plus the crest description.
Why: The cluster of "tomb, warden, crown, long sleep, contempt for younger races" is too close to the Necrons for comfort, both legally and in terms of player perception. CLOSING NOTE
These outlines are strong. The faction voices are distinct, the three-beat structure is clean, the rival escalation works, and several beats contain genuinely excellent writing (the chalk names, the 19-year-old lieutenant, the "fade quietly" line, the eleven decimal places). The shared Hollow Sun finale is a smart structural choice that gives six arcs one destination without forcing them into one story. The win and loss text across all six arcs is consistently good, which is rare and valuable.
The main risks are: the rival not feeling personal enough (fix 1), two factions drifting too close to 40k (fixes 2 and 3), and blue options undermining dilemmas (fix 4). All of these are fixable within the existing format without adding new mechanics, new screens, or new scope.
