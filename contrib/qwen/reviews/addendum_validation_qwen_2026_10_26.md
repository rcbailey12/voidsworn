Blind review: addendum validation, feel and fun lens
Reviewer role: feel-and-fun lens only. I am not re-auditing code or tooling correctness. I am judging whether these twenty items protect or buy the cinematic battle feel, ship quality, and fun the owner described, and naming what is absent.

1. ITEM VERDICTS
Item 1 - Repo hygiene test suite. KEEP. No direct feel connection; call it what it is, which is pipeline protection. It earns its slot because three of the four defect classes it targets (doc drift, false completion claims, constant drift) are the same classes that would silently degrade a damage-mark pass or a balance tuning pass and nobody would notice until the owner plays. It protects the conditions under which feel work can land correctly. Worth the cost before cutover.
Item 2 - Golden-image screenshot suite. KEEP, and it is the most feel-protective item in the addendum. The shot list directly names battle damage states (stage 2 and stage 4), a flagship close-up per faction, and the Nemesis strike warning. The silent BoxMesh fallback check means a broken ship model gets caught before the owner has to find it by eye during a playtest. The per-environment golden rule and manual-blessing-only rule correctly keep the owner's eye as the visual verdict, which matches the project ruling that visual verdicts belong to the owner, never to a tool. This is the item that guards "ships must read as high quality" during the migration when everything is most fragile. Before cutover is the right gate.
Item 3 - Generate gen/ in build, track inputs, model provenance, auto LOD. KEEP. The feel connection is indirect but real: auto mesh LOD with visibility ranges is what lets 20 ships render at acceptable frame rates so the spectacle is possible at all, and the model provenance file protects against a licence issue pulling a ship model out of the game after the art pass. The LOD bakeoff caution (test distances, do not trust defaults) is the right instinct because bad LOD pop at fleet distance would break the Sins 2 readability target. Before cutover is correct.
Item 4 - Headless export, version stamping. KEEP, cheap, no feel connection. This is build plumbing. It does not protect or buy any feel or fun. It is worth doing because it costs almost nothing and kills the FEATURES.md metadata disagreement, but I will not pretend it serves the cinematic brief. Before cutover is fine.
Item 5 - Atomic saves, split settings. KEEP, cheap, no feel connection. Data integrity. A torn save is a fun-destroyer in a roguelite (losing a run to corruption is not "consequence," it is a bug), so there is a thin fun link, but the item is really about not shipping a broken save format to Steam Auto-Cloud. Before cutover because items 14 and the Steam path depend on it.
Item 6 - GdUnit4 scoped decision. KEEP as a scoped decision, not a mandate. The feel connection runs entirely through item 2: the scene runner's input simulation is what lets the screenshot suite reach combat states (a salvo landing, a damage state change) without hand-scripting every camera setup. If the owner declines GdUnit4, someone has to build that state-reaching another way, and the screenshot suite gets harder. The fuzzer for save mutation is a fun-protector (corrupt saves) but secondary. The guardrail that the parity harness stays the rules authority is correct and I endorse it. Before cutover, decision only, not installation.
Item 7 - Music credits file and loudness target. KEEP, and the loudness target is the feel half. The credits schema is process. But the -14 LUFS, -1 dBTP loudness target (consistent with EBU R128 broadcast practice and streaming-platform normalisation; see https://tech.ebu.ch/docs/r/r128.pdf) is a feel decision because it sets the dynamic ceiling for every track and every sound effect before the first one is made. Star Wars film sound works because the mix has headroom for the quiet moments before the Star Destroyer flyover hits. Setting this target now, before any audio content exists, is cheap and prevents a painful remaster later. The template-now, complete-before-Steam split is the right timing.
Item 8 - Godot API lookup from pinned 4.7 source. KEEP, no direct feel connection. This is agent-productivity tooling. It prevents the class of bug where an agent writes a Godot 3 camera API call into a 4.7 scene and the cinematic camera silently does nothing. That is a feel-protection link, but it is second-order. The real value is faster and more correct engine work across the board. Before cutover is fine because the migration is when the wrong-API risk peaks.
Item 9 - Verification-before-completion checklist. KEEP, no feel connection. It counters the false-completion incident. A false "done" on a damage-mark pass or a balance change is a feel risk, but the checklist itself is generic process. Cheap, plain text, no reason to resist.
Item 10 - Path-scoped rules, prompt audits, worktree isolation. KEEP, no feel connection. Agent workflow hygiene. None of this touches how a battle looks or plays. It is cheap and reduces merge collisions during the migration, which indirectly protects the schedule that feel work needs. Not a feel item; do not dress it up as one.
Item 11 - gdlint gate, gdformat in check mode. KEEP, no feel connection. Code style enforcement. The data-loss warning on gdformat for parity-mirrored files is correctly noted. This has zero bearing on cinematic feel or fun. Cheap gate, fine to have.
Item 12 - Single-threaded differential run. REORDER to later (after the big effects pass). This is a one-off diagnostic for a threading finding. It does not protect feel or fun. It is cheap, but it is also the kind of task that sits in a before-cutover list and crowds out a day that could go to blocking in camera angles or placeholder sound. Run it after the battle is playable in Godot, when the threading question matters for real frame times rather than as a migration checkbox.
Item 13 - Frame-budget benchmark at 20-ship scale. KEEP, and this is the second most feel-protective item. The owner's north star includes roughly 20 ships on the board. Sins of a Solar Empire 2 (Ironclad Games / Stardock, 2024; https://store.steampowered.com/app/2064650/Sins_of_a_Solar_Empire_II/) works because you can zoom from fleet view to a single capital ship without the frame rate collapsing. If the Godot build cannot hold a frame budget at 20 ships with damage decals, weapon flashes, and the plasma-light-blue ruling all active, every cinematic ambition is capped. Scripting this now, with committed Monitors readouts per change, means every subsequent feature (the big effects pass, DrawableTexture2D damage marks, AreaLight3D) is measured against a known budget. Before cutover is the right gate; do not move it.
Item 14 - Steam integration, cloud saves, CI build-and-inspect. KEEP at before-Steam timing. No feel connection. Shipping plumbing. GodotSteam (MIT; https://github.com/GodotSteam/GodotSteam) is the settled path. The CI rendering recipe (xvfb, mesa-vulkan) is infrastructure. None of this changes how a battle feels. Correct timing.
Item 15 - Localisation scaffolding, English only. KEEP at before-Steam timing. No feel connection. The 1,500-plus string argument is real: retrofitting tr() calls across a shipped codebase is miserable. But this is a shipping task, not a feel task. The pseudolocalisation overflow pass is worth doing because it catches UI strings that truncate, and a truncated battle log or cut-off damage number is a small fun-paper-cut. Correct timing.
Item 16 - Captions routed to battle log. KEEP at before-Steam timing. Marginal feel connection. Audio-only cues (a torpedo launch sound, a critical-hit sting) that a deaf or hearing-impaired player cannot perceive are a fun gap. Routing them to the existing battle log is the cheapest fix. The feel link is thin but real: if a player cannot tell a torpedo is in flight because they cannot hear the launch cue, the BattleTech-weight "decision that costs something" moment is degraded. Correct timing.
Item 17 - Capsule art checklist, codesigning later. KEEP at before-Steam timing. No feel connection. Store marketing. The exact-size checklist is cheap. Codesigning at $9.99/month (per Microsoft Learn; https://learn.microsoft.com/en-us/azure/security/trusted-signing/overview) is correctly deferred to when a public demo exists. No gameplay feel link.
Item 18 - Editor/runtime MCP bridge, read-only trial. KEEP at later-with-trigger timing. No feel connection. A development convenience gated on hitting a wall. The security advisory note on Godot AI v4 is well taken. None of this changes how the game plays. Correct to defer.
Item 19 - Generated repo map. KEEP at later-with-trigger timing. No feel connection. Sequenced behind item 1, which is correct because a stale map is worse than no map. This is developer navigation aid for a 3,724-line rules file. No feel link.
Item 20 - Godot 4.7 rendering features (AreaLight3D, DrawableTexture2D, Control offset transforms). KEEP at later-with-trigger timing, and flag DrawableTexture2D as the feel-critical one. The addendum itself calls DrawableTexture2D "a possible damage-mark lead," and the evidence pack says the current damage marks are PARTIAL against the reference (generic scorch decals instead of per-weapon signature marks). Per-weapon persistent marks (battery scorch, torpedo silhouette change, plasma light-blue ruling) are a core ship-quality pillar. AreaLight3D matters for the plasma and engine-glow look. The timing gate ("when the fx work reaches them") is correct because the big effects pass is scheduled after a full battle plays in Godot, and these features should land in that pass, not before it. But I want the trigger stated more explicitly: DrawableTexture2D investigation should begin no later than the start of the big effects pass, not after it.

2. WHAT IS MISSING FOR CINEMATIC FEEL
The addendum is entirely tooling and process. It contains zero items that create cinematic feel. The following five additions would most buy the stated feel targets. I rank them by impact-per-effort at the project's current stage (migration in progress, big effects pass not yet started, no music content, no battle cut-ins).
Rank 1: A scripted battle camera direction system.
What it is: a small state machine that controls the Godot camera during a turn-based battle - wide fleet establishing shot at turn start, push-in to the firing ship on weapon activation, a brief hold on the impact point when a salvo lands, a slow pull-back to wide as the turn resolves. Not free-roaming; authored beats. In Rogue Trader's voidship combat (Owlcat Games, 2023; https://store.steampowered.com/app/2186680/Warhammer_40000_Rogue_Trader/), the camera frames each broadside and torpedo volley as a discrete cinematic moment even though the underlying system is strictly turn-based with firing arcs and travel time. Sins 2 does the same at RTS scale: the zoom from fleet view to a capital ship firing its ultimate ability is the game's signature spectacle moment, and it is a camera move, not a rendering trick. In the Star Wars films, the directorial rule is that every weapon firing gets a reaction shot: the gun fires, then the camera is already on the thing being hit. A turn-based game can replicate that beat structure exactly because it knows in advance what will fire and what will be hit.
Which reference does it best: Rogue Trader for turn-based framing, Sins 2 for scale zoom, Star Wars (any of the original trilogy space battles, but the Death Star trench run in A New Hope is the tightest example of fire-impact-reaction cutting) for the beat structure.
Effort shape: medium. The camera logic is a state machine over the existing turn resolution order. The hard part is tuning timing and easing so it feels authored rather than robotic. No new rendering work.
Gate: before the big effects pass. Camera direction multiplies every visual effect that lands after it. Without it, the effects pass produces pretty explosions that the player watches from a static wide shot, which is a screenshot, not a battle.
Rank 2: Music and sound design content, with a dynamic layering system.
What it is: not just "a soundtrack." A layered music system with at minimum three intensity layers (ambient exploration, tactical tension, capital-ship engagement climax) that crossfade based on battle state (ships remaining, a capital ship entering critical, a torpedo in flight). Plus authored sound effects for every weapon family, every critical hit, every ship death, and the ambient bed of a fleet at idle. The evidence pack says music content is entirely absent (plumbing exists, no tracks) and the ambient audio bed is partial. The -14 LUFS target in item 7 is a good start but there is nothing to normalise yet.
Which reference does it best: the Star Wars films, unambiguously. John Williams' score is not background; it is the emotional narration. The Imperial March does not accompany Darth Vader's entrance; it IS the entrance. In Sins 2, the orchestral score swells when a capital ship uses an ability and drops to tension strings during fleet manoeuvre (https://store.steampowered.com/app/2064650/Sins_of_a_Solar_Empire_II/; the soundtrack is by Paul Shuey and others). In EVE Online (CCP Games; https://www.eveonline.com/), the sound design carries the weight: the slow capacitor hum, the distant thud of a torpedo impact you cannot see, the silence of a pod kill. That audio weight is what makes EVE feel consequential even when the visuals are sparse.
Effort shape: large for a full score, but a small vertical slice (one battle's worth of layered music, weapon sounds for the three main families, one critical-hit sting, one ship-death sound) is medium and would unblock the entire audio direction conversation.
Gate: before the big effects pass. Sound and vision should be blocked together, not sequentially. A silent explosion reads as a placeholder no matter how good the particle effect is.
Rank 3: Destruction spectacle and ship-death choreography.
What it is: when a ship dies, it does not simply vanish or play a single particle burst. It breaks along pre-authored fracture lines, tumbles, vents atmosphere or plasma (light blue per the ruling), and the camera (see rank 1) holds on it for one to two seconds. Capital ship deaths are longer and louder. Wreckage persists on the battlefield for the rest of the engagement as a navigation hazard or at least a visual record. The damage-mark system (currently PARTIAL: generic scorch decals instead of per-weapon signature marks) feeds into this: a ship that has taken torpedo hits to the port side and battery fire across the bow should look like it has been in a fight before it dies, so the death is the culmination of a visible story.
Which reference does it best: Sins 2 for capital-ship death spectacle (the ship breaks apart in stages with distinct explosion beats). The Star Wars films for the principle that every ship death is a moment: in The Empire Strikes Back, the Star Destroyer collisions and the Millennium Falcon's escape through the debris field give weight to destruction. In BattleTech (Harebrained Schemes / Paradox Interactive, 2018; https://store.steampowered.com/app/637090/BATTLETECH/), a mech's destruction is a multi-second event with component losses visible before the final fall, and that deliberate pacing is exactly the turn-based weight the owner wants.
Effort shape: large for full fracture-line destruction, but medium for a staged death (two to three break-apart phases, a persistent wreck mesh, a sound sting) that would cover the feel gap.
Gate: before Steam. The big effects pass is the natural home, but the design of the death choreography (how many stages, how long the camera holds, whether wreckage persists) should be decided before that pass starts so the effects team builds toward it.
Rank 4: Pre-battle and post-battle presentation bookends.
What it is: the thirty seconds before the first turn and the fifteen seconds after the last. Pre-battle: the fleet drops out of warp or approaches the engagement zone; the camera sweeps the formation; the Nemesis (if present) gets a line; the enemy admiral's fleet is revealed. Post-battle: the surviving ships hold position among wreckage; a brief result card appears (ships lost, criticals scored, Nemesis scar applied); the music resolves. These are the moments Rogue Trader uses to frame voidship combat as an event rather than a system state. Star Wars Zero Company (Bit Reactor / Respawn Entertainment; UNSURE on exact release status as of my knowledge, announced as a turn-based Star Wars tactics title) uses mission briefing and debrief sequences to bracket tactical play with narrative weight, making each engagement feel like a chapter rather than a skirmish.
Which reference does it best: Rogue Trader for the in-engine fleet reveal before voidship combat. Star Wars films for the establishing-shot grammar (the slow pan across the fleet before the battle starts is a franchise signature). EVE Online for the post-battle weight: the silence after a fleet engagement, the wreck field, the local chat where the kill report lands.
Effort shape: small to medium. No new systems; it is sequencing existing assets (ship models, camera, a text card, a music sting) into authored moments.
Gate: before Steam, but a minimal version (a two-second fleet reveal, a one-second result hold) should be in before cutover so the battle flow has bookends from the first playable Godot build.
Rank 5: Battle pacing controls and turn rhythm.
What it is: explicit control over how long each phase of a turn takes to present. Not the simulation speed; the presentation speed. How long the camera holds on a firing arc solution before the weapons fire. Whether torpedo travel time is shown in real-time or compressed. Whether the player can fast-forward through a turn they have already decided (BattleTech 2018 lets you toggle between watching the full animation and skipping to results, and that toggle is a respect-for-the-player-time feature that protects fun without sacrificing spectacle for the moments you want to watch). A "cinematic mode" toggle where everything plays at full authored speed, and a "tactical mode" where animations compress to keep decision density high.
Which reference does it best: BattleTech (2018) for the animation-speed toggle and the deliberate weight of each mech's movement and firing phase. Rogue Trader for the phase structure (movement, then firing, then resolution) that gives each turn a readable rhythm. Sins 2 for the speed controls that let you watch a capital-ship duel at full spectacle or fast-forward a fleet repositioning.
Effort shape: small for the speed toggle, medium for authored pacing beats tied to the camera system in rank 1.
Gate: before the big effects pass, because pacing determines how long each effect needs to be visible and therefore how complex each effect needs to be.

3. WHAT IS MISSING FOR FUN AND BALANCE
The project's balance loop is simulation-led (19,500 bot battles, admiral win rates, faction matrices) plus the owner's playtests. The following five additions would most improve the fun side of that loop.
Rank 1: Playtest instrumentation with decision-point logging.
What it is: every time the player makes a choice in a battle (which ship to activate, which weapon to fire, which target to select, whether to use an officer ability, whether to attempt boarding), log the decision, the options presented, the time spent deciding, and the outcome. At battle end, log total turns, total decisions, decisions per turn, average decision time, and which ship classes and weapon families were used. Store as structured JSON alongside the existing bot-battle logs.
What gets measured: decision density (decisions per turn), decision time distribution, weapon-family usage spread per admiral, boarding attempt rate, officer ability usage rate, Nemesis encounter frequency.
What decision it changes: if decision density drops below a threshold in sector 3 and beyond (where more ships are on the board per the difficulty ramp), the team knows the fleet size is diluting per-activation weight and can tune activation grouping or reduce ship count. If a weapon family is never chosen, its damage marks and sound effects are being built for a weapon nobody fires. If boarding is never attempted, the verb is not fun or not discoverable.
Which reference does it best: BattleTech (2018) post-mission reports show exactly which weapons hit, which locations were damaged, and how long the mission took, giving the player and the designer a shared language for "what happened." Harebrained Schemes iterated heavily on mech and weapon balance post-launch using player telemetry (https://store.steampowered.com/app/637090/BATTLETECH/; the 1.0 to 1.9 patch notes track visible balance shifts driven by usage data).
Effort shape: small. The data already exists in the simulation; it needs structured logging and a simple reader.
Gate: before cutover. The owner's next playtest in Godot should produce this data from the first session.
Rank 2: Battle-length and pacing histogram from bot battles.
What it is: extend the existing 19,500-battle simulation to record not just win rates but turn count distribution, damage-per-turn curve, the turn at which the first ship dies, the turn at which the first capital ship enters critical, and the ratio of "meaningful turns" (turns where at least one ship's capability changes due to a crit, a kill, or a boarding) to total turns. Visualise as a histogram per admiral, per faction, per sector.
What gets measured: whether battles in sector 1 are too short (no tension arc) or sector 5 battles drag past the fun window. Whether certain admirals produce flat damage curves (no escalation, no drama) versus ones with sharp spikes (one lucky crit ends the battle, which feels unfair).
What decision it changes: if the median battle length at sector 3 is 40 turns and decision density is 1.2 per turn, the battle is a slog and the difficulty ramp needs restructuring. If an admiral's battles all end on turn 6 with a single torpedo crit, that admiral is not a tactical challenge but a coin flip. The owner's playtest sign-off becomes sharper: instead of "that felt long," the data says "sector 4 battles average 34 turns with 22 of them below the meaningful-decision threshold."
Effort shape: small. The simulation already runs; add fields to the output and a plotting script.
Gate: before cutover. The next simulation pass should collect this.
Rank 3: A tuning dashboard for the owner's playtest loop.
What it is: a single HTML page (or a Godot debug panel) that, after a playtest session, shows: the battle just played, turn by turn, with damage dealt and taken per ship, crits landed, weapon families used, time per turn, and a comparison against the bot-battle baseline for the same admiral and faction. Highlight deviations: "You took 40 percent more damage from this admiral than the bot median. You used plasma 70 percent of the time; bots use it 30 percent."
What gets measured: the gap between the owner's experience and the simulation baseline, per session, without the owner having to read raw logs.
What decision it changes: the owner can say "this admiral felt too aggressive" and the dashboard shows whether the simulation agrees (bot win rate spike, damage output above median) or whether it is a presentation issue (the admiral is statistically fine but the camera never shows the incoming fire, so it feels like a sneak attack). This separates balance problems from feel problems, which is the exact distinction this review lens cares about.
Effort shape: medium. Requires wiring the playtest log format to a renderer. The data pipeline from rank 1 feeds it.
Gate: before Steam, but a text-only console version before cutover.
Rank 4: A replay and spectator mode for bot battles.
What it is: the ability to watch a bot-vs-bot battle in the Godot renderer at selectable speed, with the camera system from section 2 rank 1 active. Not a new simulation; the bot battles already run. This lets the owner (or a reviewer) watch a hundred battles in an evening at 4x speed and spot visual problems: a weapon that never appears to fire, a damage mark that does not show up at fleet distance, a ship class that is visually indistinguishable from another at range.
What gets measured: visual readability and spectacle at fleet scale, across the full range of ship classes and weapon families, without the owner having to play every combination.
What decision it changes: "The battery weapons are invisible at fleet zoom" becomes a finding from watching ten replays rather than a discovery during a playtest in sector 4. "The Nemesis strike warning is not readable on the campaign map" surfaces in a spectator pass over the campaign map. This is also the fastest way to validate the screenshot suite's shot list: if the spectator camera passes through a composition that looks great, add it to the goldens.
Effort shape: medium. The bot simulation exists; the renderer needs a "watch mode" that replays a logged battle. The camera system (section 2 rank 1) makes it cinematic rather than a top-down dump.
Gate: before the big effects pass. The effects team needs to see their work at fleet scale in motion, not just in a static screenshot.
Rank 5: Hot-reload for feel parameters during a live battle.
What it is: a debug overlay (or the editor bridge from item 18, if adopted) that lets the owner adjust weapon damage, movement speed, turn timer, camera hold duration, and animation speed during a live battle and see the effect immediately, without restarting. Changes are logged and can be committed as balance patches.
What gets measured: nothing directly, but it collapses the iteration cycle for every feel parameter from "edit data, rebuild, restart, play to the relevant battle, observe" to "drag a slider, watch the next turn."
What decision it changes: the owner can find the camera hold duration that makes a broadside feel weighty versus the one that makes it feel sluggish, in thirty seconds instead of thirty minutes. The BattleTech-weight "decisions that cost something" feel is tuned in milliseconds of animation timing, and those milliseconds cannot be found through a data file edit cycle.
Effort shape: small for a debug overlay with a few sliders wired to existing data. Medium if it needs to touch the camera state machine.
Gate: before the big effects pass. The effects pass will generate dozens of "is this timing right?" questions that should be answerable in-session.

4. RISKS
Risk 1: The screenshot suite (item 2) freezes the art direction before the big effects pass.
The golden images are blessed manually, and the addendum correctly says no auto-bless. But the practical risk is that once ten golden images are committed and the CI diffs against them, every visual change in the big effects pass (new damage decals, plasma colour tuning, AreaLight3D glow) triggers a diff failure that requires a manual blessing commit. If the effects pass generates fifty visual changes, that is fifty blessing commits, and the team will start to feel that the screenshot suite is a tax on iteration. Worse, if the early goldens are blessed before the art direction is settled (the damage marks are PARTIAL; the per-weapon signature marks do not exist yet), the goldens encode a placeholder look as the reference, and the "bless the change" step becomes "bless the fix that makes it look like what we always wanted," which inverts the purpose.
Guardrail: do not bless the battle-damage and flagship-close-up goldens until the per-weapon damage marks and the plasma-light-blue ruling are implemented at reference quality. The shot list can exist and the capture pipeline can run, but those specific goldens should be marked DRAFT and excluded from CI failure until the owner blesses them after the effects pass. Title screen, settings, and choice-card goldens are safe to bless now.
Risk 2: The frame-budget benchmark (item 13) caps spectacle by being measured against the wrong target.
If the benchmark is set at 20 ships with placeholder materials and no damage decals, it establishes a frame budget with headroom that the big effects pass will consume. If it is set at 20 ships with full materials, it may be unachievable before the effects pass optimises. The risk is that the benchmark becomes a ceiling the effects team is told not to exceed, rather than a diagnostic that shows where the budget is spent. Sins 2's spectacle works because the engine budgets are set around the spectacle, not the other way around; the capital ship ultimate abilities get their particle count because the budget was designed to accommodate them (Ironclad Games has discussed LOD and culling strategies for fleet-scale rendering in Sins 2 developer updates; https://store.steampowered.com/app/2064650/Sins_of_a_Solar_Empire_II/).
Guardrail: run the benchmark at three fidelity tiers (placeholder, current art, target art) and report all three. The committed number is the target-art tier, and the gap between current and target is the effects pass's budget to fill. Do not let a single number become a hard cap that the effects pass must stay under; let it become a spending plan.
Risk 3: Twenty tooling items crowd out the first playable Godot battle.
The addendum has seven items before cutover, six workflow patterns, four before-Steam items, and three later items. The chair's note says items 1 and 2 are the addendum and everything else is service or cheap. That is true in principle, but in practice, a developer working through a migration will encounter each item as a task with its own setup, testing, and edge cases. The risk is not any single item; it is the cumulative drag of seven before-cutover items on a single developer (Opus) whose primary job is to get a full battle playing in Godot so the owner can sign off. Every hour spent wiring gdlint or configuring worktree isolation is an hour not spent making the battle camera push in on a torpedo impact. The owner's stated fear is exactly this: engineering effort on harness and hygiene is effort not spent on the product.
Guardrail: sequence the before-cutover items explicitly against the "full battle plays in Godot" milestone. Items 1, 2, 3, 4, 5, 13 are preconditions or guards for that milestone and stay. Items 6, 7, 8, 9, 10, 11 are enablers and should be batched after the first playable battle, not interleaved with it. The addendum should state this ordering plainly rather than listing all seven under the same "before cutover" heading.

5. THE ONE THING
A scripted battle camera direction system, as described in section 2 rank 1.
The reason it is the one thing, above music, above destruction spectacle, above pacing controls, is that it is the multiplier. Every visual effect the big effects pass produces - the per-weapon damage marks, the plasma light-blue hits, the torpedo travel, the critical sparks - is seen through the camera. A perfect explosion viewed from a static wide shot at fleet distance is a speck. The same explosion with a two-second push-in, a hold on the impact, and a pull-back to show the fleet context is a moment. Rogue Trader proves this at the exact scale and turn structure Voidsworn uses: its voidship combat is strictly turn-based, small-fleet, firing-arc-driven, and it feels cinematic because the camera frames each phase as a shot, not because the rendering is extraordinary (Owlcat Games, https://store.steampowered.com/app/2186680/Warhammer_40000_Rogue_Trader/). Sins 2 proves it at scale: the zoom from fleet to capital ship is the spectacle, not the particle count. The Star Wars films prove it as grammar: the camera cuts to the reaction, not the action.
The camera system is also the item that makes every other missing thing better. Music needs moments to swell into; the camera creates those moments. Destruction spectacle needs a viewer at the right distance; the camera is the viewer. Pacing needs beats; the camera defines the beats. The playtest instrumentation in section 3 rank 1 becomes more useful when the owner can watch a replay through the cinematic camera and see what the player would see.
It is medium effort, requires no new rendering technology, no new dependencies, and no changes to the simulation. It is a state machine over the existing turn-resolution order with camera position and easing curves. It can be blocked in with placeholder timing before cutover and tuned after. And it is the single item that, if it exists in rough form when the owner plays his first Godot battle, will make that battle feel like a game he wants to finish rather than a tech demo he is evaluating.

OVERALL VERDICT
The addendum is well-researched, honestly scoped, and correctly sequenced in its broad strokes. Through the feel-and-fun lens, items 2 (screenshot suite) and 13 (frame budget) genuinely protect the cinematic and spectacle targets, and item 7's loudness target is a small but real feel decision. The chair is right that items 1 and 2 are the spine. But the addendum is, by its own nature, entirely defensive: it guards quality, enforces hygiene, and prepares shipping. It does not create a single moment of cinematic feel, and it does not add a single measurement to the fun loop. The owner described a game that should feel like Sins 2, a Star Wars film, and EVE Online, and the addendum contains zero items that build toward any of those feelings. The single change I would insist on is this: add an explicit item, before cutover, for a battle camera direction block-in - not the finished system, but the state-machine skeleton and one authored beat (push-in on fire, hold on impact, pull-back to wide) - so that the first playable Godot battle the owner sees is framed as a cinematic event rather than a top-down simulation view. Everything else in the addendum can stand as written, with the reordering of item 12 to later and the drafting hold on battle-damage goldens noted above. The tooling is sound. The game still needs a camera.
