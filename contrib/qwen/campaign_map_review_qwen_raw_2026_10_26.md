Voidsworn Campaign Screen Information Audit: Blind Review Leg
Scope and method
I have worked only from the attached evidence pack (seven files fetched from main on 26 October 2026, plus the Job 2 Nemesis review) and my own knowledge of the comparison games. Every claim about Voidsworn cites the pack section and, where possible, the file and line the pack references. Every claim about a comparison game cites its source or is tagged as from memory. I have not seen any other review leg. No screenshots exist for Voidsworn, so no verdict here rests on how the screens look.
1. Route planning
Verdict: PASS
Pack evidence
The pack establishes that commitment is per jump and that all planning information below is available before the jump (Section 1, citing map3d.gd lines 630-639 and campaign_ui.gd line 406). There is no undo in any fetched file (Section 1).
Before committing, the player can see:
Every node's type by its scene or icon, with a legend row naming all types (Section 1, map3d.gd lines 243-356, campaign_ui.gd line 381).
Fight identity: encounter name, board or map name, and node modifier short name printed on the map for fight nodes (Section 1, map_view.gd lines 56-59, map3d.gd lines 485, 516, 508-512).
Rewards: boon nodes carry the patron's sigil, glyph, short name, and a rare-or-better star; other rewards print as Refit, Credits, or Repairs (Section 1, map3d.gd lines 513-519, campaign_ui.gd lines 432-439).
The hover card on the 3D map gives the full per-node detail: type, board, faction, danger line, encounter name and description, archetype, modifier, reward, and route summary (Section 1, map3d.gd lines 548-606).
The route comparison layer is the strongest element. Hovering a node lights the safest route (cyan) and the richest route (gold) from the player's position through that node to the sector flagship (Section 1, map_intel.gd lines 115-127). Each node on a lit route gets a running-total chip showing fights so far and repair stops so far (Section 1, map3d.gd lines 522-526). The hover card prints both routes' totals: fight count with elite count, repair stops, shipyards, boons, refits, relics, and the worst fight's band name in its band colour (Section 1, map3d.gd lines 597-604). The boss fight is excluded from route danger sums because it ends every route (Section 1, map_intel.gd line 100).
Below the map, preview panels for the currently reachable fights list encounter name, archetype, modifier, elite flag, board shape, descriptions, and reward, deduplicated (Section 1, campaign_ui.gd lines 384-406).
The flat map has none of the planner layer: no danger bands, no routes, no hover card. It has icon nodes, on-node labels, dashed reachable lines, and a thinner tooltip (Section 1, map_view.gd lines 103-114). UNSURE from the pack: whether the 3D hover card and route planner are reachable by keyboard alone. The mechanism exists (keyboard focus cycles nodes and sets the hovered node, map3d.gd lines 618-627) but whether the card visibly opens on keyboard focus in the running game is not established by code alone (Section 1).
Comparison games
Sins of a Solar Empire 2. The strategic layer is the star map: planets connected by phase lanes, each planet showing its type, bonuses, and militia presence. Route planning is about which phase lanes to push through and in what order. The player sees fleet compositions as ship counts and types, and can compare fleet strength before committing to an attack. However, Sins 2 is a real-time strategy game, so "committing" to a route is a continuous decision, not a discrete one-jump-at-a-time choice. There is no equivalent of a pre-commit route summary showing "this path has 4 fights, 2 repairs, and a shipyard before the boss." The campaign mode, added after the early-access period, layers mission objectives onto the star map but does not add a route-scoring overlay. (Source: Ironclad Games developer journals on the Sins 2 Steam community hub and the game's own in-game tutorial text, which I recall from playing the early-access and release builds. I have not opened a specific page for this claim today, so I flag it as from memory of the game and its dev blogs.)
The key difference: Sins 2 lets you see the topology and the fleets, but the "what will this path cost me and pay me" synthesis is left to the player. Voidsworn's safest/richest route overlay with running totals does that synthesis for the player, which is a meaningful step forward for a turn-based game where each jump is irreversible.
BattleTech (2018, Harebrained Schemes). The star map shows systems connected by jump points. Each system displays its faction ownership and whether contracts are available. The contract board at each system lists available missions with a skull rating (difficulty), the employer, the opposition faction, the lance tonnage limit, and the reward in C-bills and salvage. The player picks a contract, then picks which systems to travel to next, with each jump costing time and fuel. Route planning is therefore: which contracts to take, in what order, balancing difficulty against reward against travel cost. The game does not show you a multi-contract path summary. You plan one contract at a time. (Source: the BattleTech game's own UI, documented in the official wiki at battletechgame.fandom.com and in the Paradox Interactive forum guides. Also described in the PC Gamer review by Andy Kelly, published May 2018, and the Rock Paper Shotgun review. I recall these from reading at the time and note them as from memory of those sources.)
The key difference: BattleTech's planning is contract-by-contract with a static difficulty rating. Voidsworn's route overlay, which chains multiple nodes and shows cumulative fights, repairs, and rewards across the whole remaining sector, gives the player a longer planning horizon in a single view.
Warhammer 40,000: Rogue Trader (2023, Owlcat Games). The Koronus Expanse chart shows explored and unexplored systems. The voidship route screen lets the player plot a course between systems, showing warp travel time and hazard risk. Each destination system shows what is available: trade goods, encounters, story events. The player picks a route and commits to it. The route screen shows the sequence of systems but does not aggregate "total fights" or "total rewards" across the route. Danger is per-system, shown as an encounter level or a warp hazard descriptor. (Source: Owlcat's official Rogue Trader wiki and the game's own UI. Also described in the PCGamer review and the Eurogamer review, both from late 2023. I recall the route screen's structure from playing the game and note it as from memory.)
The key difference: Rogue Trader's route screen is a sequence of waypoints with per-stop information. Voidsworn's route planner adds the aggregate layer (total fights, repairs, boons, worst fight) that Rogue Trader does not compute.
Assessment
Voidsworn's route planning information is the strongest area in this audit. The safest/richest dual-route overlay with running totals, the worst-fight band per route, and the preview panels for reachable fights together give the player more structured planning information before an irreversible commitment than any of the three comparison games provide at their equivalent decision point. The flat map's lack of the planner is a real gap (Section 1, OBSERVED), but the 3D map is the default (use_3d_map defaults true, Section 1, map_view.gd header note). The keyboard question is UNSURE.
2. Information at a glance
Verdict: PASS, with one noted gap
Pack evidence
With nothing hovered and no screen opened, the map page shows (Section 2):
Sector and region name in the header, Save and quit button, the 3D chart with the fleet token on the current node (Section 2, campaign_ui.gd line 370 area, map3d.gd lines 386-404).
The intel strip: up to five boxes covering hostile presence (faction name in glow colour, blurb as tooltip), rival raiders (two faction names with an explanatory tooltip), sector condition (name, description as tooltip), patrons watching (short names in patron colours, sigil rule as tooltip), and the Nemesis box (Section 2, campaign_ui.gd lines 410-437).
The side panel, 380 units wide, scrollable, containing in order: Credits and Command points as large readouts; admiral name and doctrine; the Nemesis card; the faction action panel; the full fleet list with per-ship detail (name, flagship star, hull as numbers and a 20-segment bar, class, Mark, captain rank and name, readiness word, free refit slots, promotion-ready flags); a fleet-condition warning with average hull percent and repair-route advice; a Manage fleet button; hold count; flagship aspect name and description; every boon with text; patrons; every relic with text (Section 2, campaign_ui.gd lines 462-514).
Spatial run progress: passed nodes go dark and grey, traversed lanes turn gilt, the fleet token marks the current node (Section 2, map3d.gd lines 402-408, map_view.gd lines 47-53).
No numeric progress counter (nodes cleared of total, or fights won this sector) appears in any fetched file (Section 2, OBSERVED absence).
Heat rules are printed on the aspect screen (campaign_ui.gd lines 354-358) but no fetched map-page code repeats them. UNSURE whether any other surface repeats them during a sector (Section 2).
Toasts exist as a transient channel (campaign_ui.gd lines 239-244) but what triggers them is decided in campaign.gd, which was not fetched. UNSURE (Section 2).
The layering is deliberate. The side panel puts the most decision-relevant state (money, fleet condition, build) in always-on space. The intel strip puts sector-level context (faction, rivals, condition, patrons, Nemesis) in always-on space. Per-node detail is one hover away. Explanatory text for strip boxes lives in tooltips. One-time announcements live in modals.
Comparison games
BattleTech. The main screen (the Argosy) always shows: C-bills (money), the current date, faction reputation bars, and available contracts. The MechBay screen shows each mech's tonnage, variant, and damage state. The pilot roster shows each pilot's skills, injuries, and morale. The star map shows the current system and faction borders. What is always visible without opening anything: money, date, and a high-level fleet/army status. Detailed per-unit status requires opening the MechBay. (Source: the BattleTech game's UI, from memory of playing and from the official wiki. The Argosy as the always-visible hub is described in most guides and reviews.)
Voidsworn's side panel goes further: it shows per-ship hull, readiness, captain, and refit state in the always-on panel, not behind a separate screen. This is more information at a glance than BattleTech provides.
Sins of a Solar Empire 2. The always-visible HUD shows: metal, crystal, and antimatter income and totals; fleet supply used and cap; the selected fleet's composition. Planet tooltips show income, bonuses, and militia. The strategic map shows planet ownership and phase lane connections. There is no equivalent of a "build summary" (boons, relics, aspect) because Sins 2 does not have that kind of persistent build layer. (Source: from memory of playing Sins 2 and from the Ironclad developer journals. The resource HUD layout is standard across the Sins series.)
Voidsworn's side panel, which shows the full build (boons, relics, aspect) alongside the fleet list, is doing something Sins 2 does not need to do, because Sins 2's "build" is your tech tree and fleet composition, visible in other ways.
Rogue Trader. The always-visible information on the exploration screen includes: the current system name, the ship's hull integrity, void shield status, and warp drive readiness. The character portrait and level are shown. Crew morale is accessible but not always on the main screen. Resources (trade goods, influence) are shown on the trade screen, not always visible. The exploration map shows the current location and nearby systems. (Source: from memory of playing Rogue Trader and from the Owlcat wiki. The ship status display is described in several guides.)
Voidsworn's always-on set is richer than Rogue Trader's in terms of fleet detail and build summary. Rogue Trader relies more on opening sub-screens for detail.
Assessment
The always-on set is well chosen. Money, command points, fleet condition ship by ship, build, sector context, and the Nemesis are all visible without opening anything. The fleet-condition warning with repair-route advice (Section 2, campaign_ui.gd lines 491-497) is a particularly good piece of proactive information: it tells the player not just that the fleet is hurt but whether a repair stop is still reachable before the boss.
The gap is the missing numeric sector progress counter (Section 2, OBSERVED absence). The spatial darkening of passed nodes gives a sense of progress, but a "4 of 9 nodes cleared" or "3 fights won this sector" counter would help the player gauge how much of the sector remains, especially in large sectors. The comparison games do not all have this either (Sins 2 does not, being continuous), but BattleTech's contract completion tracking and Rogue Trader's system exploration percentage serve a similar purpose.
The Heat rules gap is noted: they appear on the aspect screen but no fetched map-page code repeats them (Section 2). If Heat rules modify fight difficulty or rewards during the sector, the player needs them visible or at least one hover away on the map page. UNSURE whether they matter during the sector or only at the aspect choice.

3. Danger readability
Verdict: PASS
Pack evidence
The danger scheme is defined in map_intel.gd (Section 3):
Four bands: Easy (under 0.8, green), Even (under 1.05, gold), Hard (under 1.4, orange), Deadly (above, red) (Section 3, map_intel.gd line 11).
The estimate uses the same budget numbers the game uses to build the enemy fleet: column budget, ramp growth, elite and boss factors, node modifiers, Heat flags, the AI level setting, all compared against the player's current fleet power (Section 3, map_intel.gd lines 17-58).
The file header is explicit that the fleet is rolled at the fight, so the band is the expected strength, not the exact one (Section 3, map_intel.gd lines 5-9).
The band shows as a coloured chip with the band name in capitals under every unpassed fight node on the 3D map (Section 3, map3d.gd lines 506-512).
The hover card adds the ratio in words: "their strength about N% of your fleet's" (Section 3, map3d.gd line 580).
Route totals name the worst band on each route (Section 1, map3d.gd lines 597-604).
The estimate follows the battle AI setting (Section 3, map_intel.gd lines 14, 48, map3d.gd line 155).
The flat map has no danger bands at all (Section 3, OBSERVED gap).
UNSURE: whether danger is shown at the moment of the fight itself (pre-battle and battle screens are outside this fetch), and whether the band thresholds were tuned against play (Section 3).
Elite marking uses three channels: a brighter red diamond with a gold crown ring, animated red hazard chevrons on lanes into elite and boss nodes, and the word "elite" in preview panels and route fight counts (Section 3, map3d.gd lines 268-276, 351, 70-73, campaign_ui.gd line 393).
Boss marking: the boss node is the enemy flagship's own hull model at larger scale, inside a large red crown ring with a red light, labelled "Sector flagship" (Section 3, map3d.gd lines 277-288, 487). Its danger band is computed like any fight's (Section 3, map_intel.gd lines 40-47).
Rival marking: a rival raider node gets an extra ring in that faction's colour, the hover card appends "(rival raiders)", and patrol ships drift around unpassed rival nodes (Section 3, map3d.gd lines 347-375).
Comparison games
BattleTech. The skull rating is a fixed number per contract, typically 1 to 5 skulls in the base game, going higher with difficulty settings and later contracts. It represents the contract's inherent difficulty, not a dynamic comparison against the player's current lance. A 5-skull contract is hard whether you bring a full lance of assault mechs or a single light mech. The player learns through experience what each skull level means for their current force. The contract board also shows the opposition faction and the lance tonnage limit, which together give the player a rough sense of what they will face. (Source: the BattleTech official wiki and the in-game contract board UI. The skull system is described in the game's tutorial and in most guides. I recall this from playing and from the wiki. The skull system was also discussed in the Harebrained Schemes developer blogs and in the PC Gamer and RPS reviews.)
The key difference: BattleTech's skulls are static and absolute. Voidsworn's bands are dynamic and relative to the player's current fleet. This is a more informative design, because a "Hard" fight means something different when your fleet is at full strength versus when it is crippled. The percentage in the hover card ("their strength about N% of your fleet's") adds precision without removing the quick-read band.
Sins of a Solar Empire 2. There is no per-encounter danger rating because the game is real-time and continuous. Fleet strength is shown as ship count, type, and supply cost. The player compares their fleet's supply cost and composition against the enemy's visible fleet. AI difficulty is a global setting chosen at game start. The militia on neutral planets have a fixed strength shown as ship count. (Source: from memory of playing Sins 2. The fleet supply and ship count display is standard across the Sins series and described in Ironclad's dev journals.)
The key difference: Sins 2 gives you the raw numbers and lets you judge. Voidsworn's bands do the judgment for the player and present it as a quick-read colour and word, with the percentage available for precision. For a turn-based game with irreversible jumps, the pre-computed estimate is the right call.
Rogue Trader. Encounter difficulty is shown through enemy ship levels and crew ratings on the encounter screen. Warp route risk is shown as a descriptor or percentage on the route screen. The game does not have a single unified danger scale; different encounters use different indicators. (Source: from memory of playing Rogue Trader and from the Owlcat wiki.)
Assessment
The four-band scheme is well calibrated for the game's needs. Four bands (Easy, Even, Hard, Deadly) give enough granularity for the player to distinguish "I should take this" from "I should avoid this" without creating false precision. The fact that the estimate is computed from the game's own fleet-building numbers means it tracks the actual difficulty curve, not a hand-placed label. The hover card's percentage adds precision for the player who wants it. The worst-fight band per route is a good summary statistic for route comparison.
The estimate-versus-exact question is answered well by the pack: the fleet is rolled at the fight, so exact information would be a lie (Section 3, map_intel.gd lines 5-9). An estimate with a percentage range is honest. BattleTech's static skulls are less honest in a different way: they do not account for the player's current state.
The flat map's lack of danger bands is a real gap (Section 3, OBSERVED). A player who switches to the flat map loses all strength estimates. This is mitigated by the 3D map being the default, but it is still a gap.
The elite, boss, and rival markings are clear and multi-channel. Three channels for elites (node marker, lane chevrons, text) is good redundancy. The boss as the enemy flagship's own hull model is a strong visual identifier, though I cannot judge how readable it is without a screenshot (UNSURE for visual readability).

Verdict: PARTIAL

Full name with title and epithet, in red, on the side-panel card and the hover card (Section 4, campaign_ui.gd lines 516-540). The intel strip uses only the plain name.
Faction, on the card and the hover card (Section 4, line 523, line 573).
Portrait, when the asset exists: 76-unit framed on the card, 38-unit on the map node. The portrait index and scar kinds are derived from a name hash because the run stores no portrait number (Section 4, lines 524-528, map3d.gd lines 493-498, campaign_ui.gd lines 541-561).

Adaptation effect text and reason, except in the one-time modal (Section 4).
Comparison games
Shadow of Mordor / Shadow of War (2014/2017, Monolith/WB). The Nemesis system is the most developed persistent-rival information system in games. Each orc captain has: a name and title; a tribal affiliation; a portrait; a set of strengths (explicitly listed, with their mechanical effect, e.g. "Enraged: becomes stronger when hurt"); a set of weaknesses (explicitly listed, with their mechanical effect, e.g. "Fear of fire: takes extra damage from fire"); a rank in the hierarchy; and a history with the player ("killed you at the Black Gate", "betrayed you at the siege"). The "Know Your Enemy" intel system lets the player interrogate captains to reveal strengths and weaknesses before fighting. The army screen shows the full hierarchy: overlords, captains, and their rivalries and alliances. When a captain adapts (gains a new strength or immunity), it is announced in text at the moment it happens and reflected in the intel screen. (Source: the Nemesis system is extensively documented. The official Shadow of Mordor wiki at shadowofmordor.fandom.com describes the strength and weakness lists. The "Know Your Enemy" mechanic is described in the game's tutorial and in the IGN guide. The adaptation announcement is visible in gameplay footage and described in the Monolith GDC talk on the Nemesis system, presented by Michael de Plater at GDC 2015, available on the GDC vault. I recall these from playing both games and from the GDC talk, and I flag the GDC talk reference as from memory of the talk's content rather than a re-watch today.)
The key comparison: Shadow of Mordor surfaces the rival's strengths and weaknesses with their mechanical effects, not just their names. Voidsworn's weakness line shows the name only, never the effect text (Section 4). Shadow of Mordor announces adaptations at the moment they happen. Voidsworn's adaptation explanation appears once in a sector-change modal, and adaptations gained at other times appear only as a generic name on the card (Section 4). Shadow of Mordor's intel is built around preparation: you learn the rival's weaknesses so you can exploit them. Voidsworn's weakness reveal system has the same intent, but the effect text is missing, so the player knows the name of a weakness without knowing what it does mechanically.
BattleTech. BattleTech does not have a persistent rival system. Enemy lances are one-off contracts. The closest equivalent is the faction reputation system, which tracks your standing with each faction and affects available contracts. There is no individual enemy commander who hunts you across the campaign. (Source: the BattleTech game's design, from memory of playing. The contract system is described in the official wiki and reviews.)
This is a gap in BattleTech that Voidsworn fills. The Nemesis is a differentiator.
Rogue Trader. Rogue Trader has recurring antagonists in its story (certain chaos figures, rival traders), but they are scripted story characters, not a systemic rival. There is no equivalent of a persistent, adapting, clock-driven hunter. The game's information about antagonists is delivered through story dialogue and codex entries, not through a dedicated rival card. (Source: from memory of playing Rogue Trader. The story antagonists are described in the game's main quest and in the Owlcat wiki.)
Again, a gap that Voidsworn fills.
Assessment
The Nemesis card does a good job of surfacing the rival's identity, location, clock, and threat level. The clock dots, the strike warning, and the node marking together give the player a clear picture of when and where the Nemesis will appear. The weakness reveal system is a good design concept.
However, the missing items are significant. The most costly missing item is the behaviour text. The player is never told on the campaign screens what the Nemesis's faction strike actually does (Section 4). A Corsair who can take credits or a crippled ship, a Deathless who returns once: these are mechanically important facts that change how the player should prepare. Without them, the player meets the Nemesis blind to his special rules. The behaviour text is the single highest-value addition.
The second most costly gap is the adaptation effect text. The card lists adaptation names but not what they do (Section 4). The one-time modal explains the adaptation for the sector change, but adaptations gained at other times (Chorus adapts after every meeting, per Job 2) appear only as a name (Section 4, UNSURE item 3). The player who sees "Adapted: Reinforced Shields" on the card does not know what that means mechanically unless they remember the modal text from three sectors ago.
The third gap is the weakness effect text. The card shows the weakness name but not its effect (Section 4). The player knows the Nemesis has a weakness called something like "Void-touched" but does not know what that means in combat. Shadow of Mordor's model, where each weakness has a one-line effect description, is the right reference.
The surfaced item that does the most work is the strike warning combined with the node marking. "His fleet is coming: your next fight is his" plus the red NEMESIS chip on the specific node gives the player exactly the information they need to prepare or avoid. The clock dots add the "when" to the "where". Together, these two items make the Nemesis feel present and threatening without any behaviour text at all.
The fed and riches counters being invisible is a design choice that preserves mystery, but it also means the player cannot tell why the Nemesis seems stronger or has more ships than expected. The danger estimate absorbs the strength difference silently (Section 4). This is tolerable if the danger band shifts visibly, but the player has no way to know the cause.
The persona and traits being unsurfaced is less costly in the short term, because they are flavour rather than mechanics. But a persistent rival lives on personality, and the persona is where that personality would be conveyed on the campaign screen. Its absence makes the Nemesis feel more like a mechanical threat than a character.

5. Information layering and duplication risks at code level
Verdict: PASS, with one design concern
Pack evidence
Section 5 inventories the layers:
Always on the map page: header row, intel strip (up to five boxes), 3D chart, node-type legend row, reachable-fight preview panels, hint line, and the full side panel. The side panel stacks credits, command points, admiral, Nemesis card, faction action, fleet list, fleet warning, fleet button, hold count, aspect, boons, patrons, relics. It scrolls, so lower sections are on-demand in practice (Section 5, campaign_ui.gd lines 462-514, 466-468).
Per unpassed node on the 3D chart, all drawn at once: name label, NEMESIS chip and portrait when converted, danger band chip, shape and modifier sub-line, reward label or patron sigil, board name above the node, hazard chevrons on lanes into elites and the boss. While a node is hovered, route running-total chips appear on every node of both lit routes (Section 5, map3d.gd lines 473-527).
On demand: the hover card carries deep per-node detail and route comparison. Tooltips carry explanatory text for strip boxes and captain perks. Modals carry one-time announcements (Section 5).
Duplications (listed neutrally in the pack):
The Nemesis clock appears in three places at once: strip box dots, card dots, and the strike tail on the card (Section 5).
Reachable fights are described twice: on-node labels and chips, and the preview panel row under the map (Section 5).
Danger appears as node chips, hover-card text, and route worst-fight text (Section 5).
Structural notes: the 3D map reserves the side panel's width in its camera framing, so the chart and panel do not overlap by construction (Section 5, map3d.gd line 48, line 443). The fallback UI is not player-facing (Section 5, campaign_host.gd lines 84-87). The UI renders what the campaign's page data dictates (Section 5, campaign_ui.gd header, lines 2-4).
Assessment
The layering is sound as design. The always-on set is the right set: money, fleet condition, build, sector context, and the Nemesis are the facts the player needs to make the next jump decision. Per-node detail is one hover away, which is appropriate for information that is specific to one node. The preview panels for reachable fights are a good middle layer: they surface the next decision's options without requiring a hover, but they sit below the map rather than on it, so they do not add to the per-node label stack.
The tooltip-only text is a design concern. The sector condition's effect is written only in the tooltip; the box prints the condition's name only (Section 5, campaign_ui.gd lines 424-425). The faction blurb, the rival-raiders explanation, the patrons sigil rule, and the Nemesis clock explanation are also tooltip-only (Section 5). A player who does not hover the strip boxes will see names and colours but not meanings. This is acceptable for the faction name (the player likely knows their sector faction) but less acceptable for the sector condition, which modifies gameplay and whose effect is only in the tooltip.
The duplication of the Nemesis clock in three places (strip dots, card dots, strike tail) is helpful rather than risky. The three instances serve different reading contexts: the strip is a glance, the card is a study, and the strike tail is an alert. They all read from the same state, so contradiction is not a risk at the code level. The duplication reinforces the clock's importance.
The duplication of reachable fights (on-node labels and preview panels) is also helpful. The on-node labels let the player scan the map spatially. The preview panels let the player read the details in a fixed, scrollable list below. They serve different reading modes. The deduplication among preview panels (Section 1, campaign_ui.gd lines 384-406) prevents redundant entries.
The duplication of danger (node chips, hover text, route worst-fight) is three levels of the same fact at three granularities: the chip is the quick read, the hover is the precise read, and the route worst-fight is the summary. This is good layering.
The one design concern is the per-node label stack on the 3D chart. Each unpassed node draws: a name label, possibly a NEMESIS chip and portrait, a danger band chip, a shape and modifier sub-line, a reward label or patron sigil, and the board name above. (c) One-sentence verdicts
1. Route planning: PASS. The safest/richest dual-route overlay with running totals, worst-fight band, and per-node preview panels gives the player more structured pre-commitment planning information than Sins 2, BattleTech, or Rogue Trader provide at their equivalent decision points.
2. Information at a glance: PASS, with the noted gap of a missing numeric sector progress counter and the Heat rules not repeated on the map page.
3. Danger readability: PASS. The four-band dynamic estimate computed from the game's own fleet-building numbers, shown as chips, hover percentages, and route worst-fight summaries, is a well-calibrated scheme that improves on BattleTech's static skulls and Sins 2's raw fleet counts.
4. The Nemesis as information: PARTIAL. The card surfaces identity, clock, location, and threat level well, but the missing behaviour text, weakness effect text, and adaptation effect text leave the player underprepared for the fight, and the missing persona, traits, fed, and riches counters leave the rival under-characterised.
5. Information layering and duplication: PASS. The always-on set is the right set, the hover layer carries appropriate depth, and the duplications serve different reading contexts rather than risking contradiction; the one concern is the modal as the only home for adaptation intelligence, and the per-node label density is UNSURE pending screenshots.
Overall verdict: Voidsworn's campaign screen information design is strong, with route planning and danger readability standing out as genuinely ahead of the comparison games, and the Nemesis card is a good foundation that needs three text fields (behaviour, weakness effects, adaptation effects) surfaced to reach the depth the commissioning criteria ask for.
